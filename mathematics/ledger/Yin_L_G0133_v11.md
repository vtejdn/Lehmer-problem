## Scope and adopted inputs

The question is how much of the original no-circle-root domain can possess the integral certificates needed by the reflection mechanism.
[L133 v10](Yin_L_G0133_v10.md#low-rank-return-and-limits) retains the preceding low-rank transfer and noncommuting-shear exploration.
Versions v1--v10 remain fixed, and their particular failures are not promoted to restrictions on every matrix construction.

The [C868 v6 certificate statement](../claims/Yin_C_G0868_v6.md#integer-reflection-certificate-statement), with its [selected integral-reflection assessment](../claims/Yin_C_G0868_v6.md#decision-whole-integral-reflection-review-2026-11-05), is an adopted input.
That decision adopts the degree-bounded certificate theorem and its exact multiplier/content criterion, not universal certificate existence.
Its [positivity and circle exclusion](../claims/Yin_C_G0868_v6.md#certificate-positivity-and-circle-exclusion) already show why irreducible inputs with a circle conjugate cannot have such certificates.
The reflection construction, exact original pole price and integer-germ bound are not re-proved here.

Unless another scope is stated, P is monic irreducible reciprocal in Z[z], has degree 2d with d>=1, and P(0)=1.
Write
\[
 p(z)=\varepsilon z^{-d}P(z),\qquad
 \varepsilon\in\{+1,-1\},\qquad
 M(P)=\exp(m(P)).
 \tag{1}
\]
All logarithmic measures are total measures, with normalized Haar measure on the unit circle and no degree normalization.
For real Laurent coefficients, f^star(z)=f(z^{-1}).
The symbol r below counts nonzero square summands; it is not the rank of an arbitrarily chosen Gram matrix.

<a id="certificate-term-count-mahler-bound"></a>
## A general multiplier retains its complete term-count cost

Suppose that c is a positive integer and
\[
 cp=\sum_{j=1}^{r}v_jv_j^\star,\qquad
 0\ne v_j\in\mathbb Z[z],\qquad
 \deg v_j\le d.
 \tag{2}
\]
Zero summands are deleted before counting r.
The adopted positivity argument makes p strictly positive on the circle.
For this input one has the exact necessary inequality
\[
 m(P)\ge
 \log\frac{r}{c}+\frac2r\sum_{j=1}^{r}m(v_j)
 \ge\log\frac{r}{c}.
 \tag{3}
\]

Indeed, away from the finite union of the circle zeros of the nonzero v_j, the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality gives
\[
 cp(e^{i\theta})
   \ge r\prod_{j=1}^{r}|v_j(e^{i\theta})|^{2/r}.
 \tag{4}
\]
Take logarithms and integrate.
Every nonzero polynomial has an integrable logarithm of its absolute value on the circle, including at its finitely many circle zeros.
Moreover log p is continuous here, so the integrated inequality is legitimate and yields
\[
 \log c+m(P)\ge\log r+\frac2r\sum_jm(v_j).
\]
For a nonzero integer polynomial, Jensen's formula gives
\[
 m(v)=\log|\operatorname{lc}(v)|
        +\sum_{v(\alpha)=0}\log\max\{1,|\alpha|\}\ge0.
\]
This proves (3).
The same AM--GM conclusion holds for nonzero integer Laurent summands of arbitrary finite widths whenever the displayed identity supplies the same nonzero p>=0: multiplying each summand by a monomial leaves its circle norm and Mahler measure unchanged, and log p remains integrable.
Only this analytic term-count inequality is extended by that observation; it does not extend the adopted reflection theorem beyond its stated degree window.

Let g>0 be the greatest common divisor of all coefficients of all v_j.
For (2), the [adopted exact multiplier/content criterion](../claims/Yin_C_G0868_v6.md#integer-reflection-certificate-statement) gives g^2 dividing c and identifies integrality of its particular reflection germ with
\[
 k=c/g^2\in\{1,2\}.
\]
Putting u_j=v_j/g gives nonzero integer polynomials of the same degree support, collective coefficient content one, and
\[
 kp=\sum_{j=1}^{r}u_ju_j^\star,\qquad
 m(P)\ge\log\frac{r}{k}+\frac2r\sum_jm(u_j).
 \tag{5}
\]
The adopted irreducibility argument excludes r=1 in this support window.
Consequently, among the certificates whose reflection actually has an integer germ, the condition M(P)<3/2 forces
\[
 \boxed{k=2,\qquad r=2.}
 \tag{6}
\]
For k=1, (5) and r>=2 give M(P)>=2.
For k=2, they give M(P)>=r/2, so r>=3 contradicts the strict upper bound.
In particular a degree-bounded doubled certificate on an input with M(P)<3/2 must already have exactly two nonzero summands.

This conclusion concerns certificates (2) with the exact integral-reflection content condition.
An arbitrary multiplier c with c/g^2>2 may have many summands and is not excluded by (6).
Nor is a low-measure original input asserted to possess any such certificate.

<a id="endpoint-two-square-obstruction"></a>
## Endpoint tests for the only remaining small-measure certificate shape

If 2p=u u^star+v v^star with integer polynomial or integer Laurent summands, evaluation at z=1 and z=-1 gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 2\varepsilon P(1)&=u(1)^2+v(1)^2,\\
 2\varepsilon(-1)^dP(-1)&=u(-1)^2+v(-1)^2.
 \end{aligned}
 \tag{7}
\]
For the strictly positive p both integers on the left are positive.
The evaluations are integers even for arbitrary finite Laurent widths, because 1 and -1 are integral units.
Thus these endpoint conditions do not depend on the support bound.

For completeness, if a positive integer n is a sum of two integer squares, every prime ell congruent to 3 modulo 4 occurs in n to an even exponent.
If ell divides a^2+b^2 and b is nonzero modulo ell, then (a/b)^2=-1 modulo ell.
Fermat's theorem would give
\[
 1=(a/b)^{\ell-1}
   =(-1)^{(\ell-1)/2}=-1\pmod\ell,
\]
a contradiction.
Hence ell divides b and then a.
Dividing a^2+b^2 by ell^2 and repeating proves the even-valuation assertion.
Therefore a prime congruent to 3 modulo 4 with odd valuation in either positive integer on the left of (7) rules out every two-summand integer representation.

Combining this test with (6), an actual no-circle-root original P with M(P)<3/2 and such an endpoint obstruction would have no doubled certificate of degree at most d, regardless of the number of summands proposed.
It would also have no degree-bounded certificate whose common-content reduction gives an integer reflection under the adopted criterion.
The bounded search below did not supply such a P.
This is a precise potential counterexample test, not a claim that the necessary original input exists.

<a id="quartic-three-squares-any-width"></a>
## The existing quartic needs three squares even without a width bound

Use the [adopted doubled-quartic control](../claims/Yin_C_G0868_v6.md#doubled-quartic-minimum-rank):
\[
 P_4=z^4+z^3+3z^2+z+1,\qquad
 p_4=z^{-2}P_4=3+z+z^{-1}+z^2+z^{-2}.
 \tag{8}
\]
Its irreducibility and strict positivity are already proved there.
That control also supplies the actual identity
\[
 2p_4=|1+z+z^2|^2+|1+z^2|^2+1.
 \tag{9}
\]
Since p_4(-1)=3, a two-summand integer Laurent representation would yield a^2+b^2=6.
Squares modulo 3 force both a and b to be divisible by 3, which would make their squared sum divisible by 9, a contradiction.
A one-summand representation is also excluded by padding with a zero second summand.
Thus the minimum number of nonzero integer Hermitian-square summands of 2p_4 is exactly three even when arbitrary finite Laurent widths are permitted.

The older C868 v6 statement of minimum three within degree at most two remains true and unchanged.
The new endpoint argument extends that particular minimum-number conclusion to arbitrary widths; it does not provide a general integral support-compression theorem.
The quartic already has a valid doubled certificate, so it is not a counterexample to universal doubling.
Indeed (3) applied to (9) gives M(P_4)>=3/2, which already excludes it from the strict small-measure range required by the proposed counterexample test.
No floating Mahler estimate is needed for that exclusion.

<a id="strict-positive-central-coefficient-screen"></a>
## Strict positivity excludes height-one original inputs

Suppose P has no unit-circle root, independently of any certificate.
Reciprocity makes z^{-d}P real on the circle, and continuity and nonvanishing make its sign constant.
Choose epsilon so that p in (1) is strictly positive.
Write
\[
 p(z)=\sum_{j=-d}^{d}p_jz^j,\qquad
 p_{-j}=p_j\in\mathbb Z,\qquad |p_d|=1.
\]
Then
\[
 p_0=\int_{\mathbb T}p\,d\mu
       >\left|\int_{\mathbb T}p(z)z^{-d}\,d\mu\right|
       =|p_d|=1.
 \tag{10}
\]
To justify strictness, choose a complex scalar eta of modulus one so that eta p_d is positive real.
The difference between the first integral and the real part of eta times the second is
\[
 \int_0^{2\pi}p(e^{i\theta})
       \bigl(1-\operatorname{Re}(\eta e^{-id\theta})\bigr)
       \,\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}>0.
\]
The integrand is nonnegative, p is everywhere positive, and d>=1 makes the second factor positive on a nonempty open arc.
This proves (10).
Since p_0 is an integer, p_0>=2.
Equivalently the absolute value of the middle coefficient of P is at least two, so a reciprocal monic constant-one integer polynomial of height one cannot be a no-circle-root candidate.
This screen is independent of irreducibility and of the Mahler upper bound.
It supplies no general lower bound for M(P) by itself.

<a id="small-measure-control-has-circle-roots"></a>
## The already retained small-measure input fails the no-circle-root requirement

The [C227 statement](../claims/Yin_C_G0227_v1.md#statement), at its [selected complete adoption](../claims/Yin_C_G0227_v1.md#decision-complete-retained-proof-adoption), retains
\[
 P_0=z^{14}-z^{12}+z^7-z^2+1,\qquad 1<M(P_0)<13/10
 \tag{11}
\]
at its accepted-source scope.
The measure bound is reused, not re-established from rounded roots or table entries.
On the circle its phase-balanced value is
\[
 e^{-7i\theta}P_0(e^{i\theta})
 =1+2\cos(7\theta)-2\cos(5\theta)
 =1-4\sin(6\theta)\sin\theta.
 \tag{12}
\]
At theta=0 this equals one.
At theta=pi/12 it equals 1+sqrt(2)-sqrt(6)<0, since sqrt(6)>1+sqrt(2).
The latter inequality follows by squaring positive quantities and using 3>2sqrt(2).
The intermediate value theorem therefore gives a unit-circle root with angle strictly between zero and pi/12.
No sign epsilon makes this phase-balanced function strictly positive, and P_0 cannot supply the no-circle-root counterexample requested here.

The actual inspected pages were the main-branch GitHub views, not a pinned commit or a locally acquired complete data snapshot.
The table headings define v as the number of roots outside the unit circle and give reciprocal polynomials.
For a reciprocal polynomial of degree D, the reciprocal pairing makes the number of roots inside the circle equal v, with multiplicity, so no circle roots would require D=2v.

## Consequence for the next original-input question

The adopted reflection theorem has a genuine original-input measure bound on its supplied certificate class.
The present deductions sharpen the coverage test: below M(P)=3/2, an integer reflection arising from the adopted content criterion must reduce to exactly two square summands with multiplier two, and its positive endpoints must pass the two-square valuation test.
The existing quartic supplies an exact width-independent three-summand control, while the height screen and the C227 sign calculation prevent two concrete types of mistaken small-measure candidates.

The missing object remains either an actual no-circle-root small-measure input failing these necessary tests, or a mechanism supplying the required certificates on a clearly stated original-input class.
A new candidate must come with exact irreducibility, no-circle-root and strict Mahler-bound evidence before the endpoint calculation can refute universal doubling in that domain.
An exact candidate or a substantively different construction would justify reopening this bounded search.
Nothing here supplies a general doubled-certificate theorem, rules out all alternative matrix mechanisms, covers irreducible inputs with circle roots, or resolves G0001.
