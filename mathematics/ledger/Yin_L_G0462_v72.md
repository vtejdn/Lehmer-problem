# The separation of two trace offsets restricts the first denominator coefficient

<a id="separation"></a>
## A necessary divisor and a bound on its quotient

Consider a sequence of positive-constant Pisot inputs with \(\theta-q\to0\) and six-term trace lifts
\[
  \Pi/j=1+\alpha_i z^d+\beta_i z^e-\beta_i z^{N-e}-\alpha_i z^{N-d}-z^N,
  \qquad 1\le d<e\le h,\qquad N=2h+2 .
\]
Here \(j\) is the positive integer branch index and \(j\alpha_i,j\beta_i\) are the two nonzero integer coefficients.
Pass to limits \(\alpha_i\to\alpha,\ \beta_i\to\beta\), which exist on a subsequence by the [sparse height inequality](../audits/literature/DS17/Yin_U002.md#sparse-height-bound).
Suppose \(\alpha\notin\mathbb Z\), and put \(c=e-d,\ \Delta=\operatorname{dist}(\alpha,\mathbb Z)>0\).
Write \(B^\sharp=\sum b_l z^l\) for the monic lifted trace denominator.
If its first strictly positive nonzero coefficient below \(d\) is at \(u=o(d)\), then eventually \(u\mid c\) and
\[
                               (c/u)\Delta^2\le1 .
\]
Moreover at least one of \(\alpha+\beta,\alpha-\beta\) must be an integer.
Thus if \(c/d\) stays bounded away from zero, or if both signed sums are nonintegral, there is a zero block \(b_l=0\) for \(1\le l\le \kappa d\), with some \(\kappa>0\) eventually on the sequence.

For \(\alpha,\beta\in\{-1/2,1/2\}\) the sharper alternatives are
\[
                              c=u\qquad\text{or}\qquad c=2u .
\]
This applies to the branch-two unit family left by the [six-term coefficient restriction](Yin_L_G0462_v61.md#six), including bounded positive differences \(e-d\) with \(d\to\infty\).
If \(c\) is fixed, \(d\to\infty\), and both limiting moduli are \(1/2\), every coefficientwise limit of the inverse series is therefore \(1,\ 1+\epsilon z^u\), or \((1-\epsilon z^u)^{-1}\), with \(u=c\) or \(2u=c\) in the last two cases and \(\epsilon=\pm1\).

<a id="mixed"></a>
## Testing the mixed moments at the first coefficient

Let \(J_i=j/K_q\) have the actual [symmetric signed measure](Yin_L_G0462_v49.md#signed-measure) \(\omega_i\).
It has mass one, its negative mass tends to zero, and \(B^\sharp=(\Pi/j)J_i\).
The [arbitrary-branch divisibility obstruction](../claims/Yin_C_G0345_v13.md#statement) gives \(d=o(h)\) since the normalized lift is bounded and its first \(d-1\) coefficients after the constant vanish.
In particular indices \(d+lu\) are below \(N-e\) for every fixed \(l\) if \(u=o(d)\).
Below \(d\) the Taylor coefficients of \(J_i\) are the integers \(b_l\).
The [actual-input model on an integral prefix](Yin_L_G0462_v68.md#low) applies through \(\lfloor\kappa_0d\rfloor\) after extraction for some \(\kappa_0>0\).
Its derivation uses only this integrality, the vanishing negative atom and the \(2h\) positive interior atoms.
Whenever \(u=o(d)\) is present, the model through that prefix is \(1+\epsilon z^u\) or \((1-\epsilon z^u)^{-1}\).
Fix its type and sign by extraction.

Push \(\omega_i\) to \((x,y)=(v^u,v^d)\) and take a weak limit probability \(\Omega\).
The first marginal has Herglotz series \(G(w)=1+\epsilon w\) or \(G(w)=(1-\epsilon w)^{-1}\), since all fixed multiples of \(u\) lie in the model prefix.
Push \(2y\,d\Omega\) to \(w=\bar x\) and write \(T_l\) for the resulting Fourier coefficient with convention \(\int w^{-l}\).
These are the limiting doubled moments at \(d+lu\).
For each \(l<0\) this coefficient is an integer, since the original index lies strictly between zero and \(d\).

Suppose first that \(c/u\to\infty\), or that \(u\nmid c\) on a subsequence with \(c/u\) bounded.
In the former case \(d+lu<e\) eventually at every fixed \(l\).
In the latter, when \(d+lu>e\), the coefficient at \(lu-c\) is zero in both models since it is \(o(d)\) and is not divisible by \(u\).
The equality \(lu=c\) cannot occur in that latter case.
Consequently for every \(l\in\mathbb Z\) one has \(T_l+[w^l](\alpha G)\in\mathbb Z\), interpreting negative series coefficients as zero.
If these alternatives fail, pass to \(c=ku\) with \(k\ge1\) fixed.
Then the same calculation gives
\[
                      T_l+[w^l](A G)\in\mathbb Z,\qquad
                      A(w)=\alpha+\beta w^k .
\]
This also holds at \(l=0,k\), since the zeroth Taylor coefficient is one.
In the two preceding alternatives write \(A=\alpha\).
All computations concern limits of bounded moments at a fixed number of indices, so coefficient ratios may vary before extraction.

<a id="two-models"></a>
## Reduction to a Laurent polynomial bounded by one

In the polynomial model put \(g=1+\epsilon w\).
The doubled marginal has density \(|g|^2\) with respect to Haar probability, so the reflected mixed measure has a density \(F\) with \(|F|\le|g|^2\).
Its coefficients tend to zero, and outside the finite support of \(Ag\) they are integers.
Thus \(F\) is Laurent polynomial and \(P=F+Ag\) is an integer Laurent polynomial.
At \(\xi=-\epsilon\), \(F\) vanishes to order at least two by the pointwise bound, and \(P\) vanishes there.
The integer Laurent quotient \(R=P/g\) has \(R(\xi)=A(\xi)\) since \(R-A=F/g\) vanishes at \(\xi\).
In particular \(A(\xi)\) is integral, excluding the case \(A=\alpha\).
For \(A=\alpha+\beta w^k\) put \(H=F/g^2=(R-A)/g\).
This Laurent polynomial satisfies \(\|H\|_\infty\le1\) and
\[
              H=\frac{R-A(\xi)}{g}
                       -\beta\frac{w^k-\xi^k}{g}.
\]
The first quotient is integral because \(R(\xi)=A(\xi)\in\mathbb Z\) and \(\operatorname{lc}(g)=\pm1\).
The last quotient before the scalar has exactly the consecutive powers \(0,\ldots,k-1\), with coefficients of modulus one.
Thus the coefficients of \(H\) on this whole block have distance \(\operatorname{dist}(\beta,\mathbb Z)=\Delta\) from the integers, and all other coefficients are integers.

In the geometric model the doubled first marginal is Haar probability plus the unit mass at \(w=\epsilon\).
Its reflected mixed measure has form \(F\,dm+D\delta_\epsilon\), with \(|F|\le1,\ |D|\le1\).
As \(l\to-\infty\), \(T_l-D\epsilon^l\to0\) by decay of the Fourier coefficients of \(F\).
The negative \(T_l\) are integers, so \(D\) is an integer by taking even negative \(l\).
For all sufficiently large positive \(l\), the contribution from \(AG\) equals \(\epsilon^l A(\epsilon)\).
The same decay and integrality give \(A(\epsilon)\in\mathbb Z\), excluding \(A=\alpha\) again.
For \(A=\alpha+\beta w^k\), all coefficients of \(F\) outside \(0,\ldots,k-1\) are integers and hence eventually zero.
On \(0\le l<k\), the coefficient of \(AG\) is \(\alpha\epsilon^l\), and the atomic contribution is an integer.
Taking \(H=F\) therefore gives the same Laurent polynomial norm bound and the same distances on the consecutive block.

In either model Parseval gives \(k\Delta^2\le\int|H|^2\,dm\le1\), and integrality of \(A\) at one of the endpoints gives the signed-sum condition.
An infinite sequence violating divisibility or the quotient bound would admit one of the excluded extractions, which proves the eventual assertions.
Likewise, failure of every positive fractional zero block would allow a subsequence with a first nonzero index \(u=o(d)\).
For bounded \(d\), a sufficiently short fractional block contains no positive indices.

When \(\alpha,\beta=\pm1/2\), Parseval gives \(k\le4\).
If \(k=4\), the four coefficients must have modulus \(1/2\) and all others vanish.
Their polynomial would then have modulus one everywhere by equality of its squared mean and its squared supremum bound.
A Laurent polynomial of constant modulus has only one nonzero coefficient, since otherwise its squared modulus has a nonzero coefficient at the difference between its two extreme exponents.
For \(k=3\), the integral coefficients outside the block must vanish, and each of the three coefficients has modulus \(1/2\).
If the two extreme signs agree, evaluate at one of \(1,-1\) to align the three terms and obtain modulus \(3/2\).
If they differ, evaluation at \(i\) gives modulus \(\sqrt5/2\).
Both violate the bound, leaving only \(k=1,2\).

<a id="truncated"></a>
## A cyclotomic multiplier determined by the two offsets

For the nonintegral limiting \(\alpha\) above, put \(P_d=\sum_{l=0}^d b_l z^l\).
The polynomial \((1-z^{2(e-d)})P_d\) has boundedly many terms on the sequence.
To see this, suppose its term count diverges along a subsequence.
Bounded \(d\) is impossible, and if the least positive nonzero index below \(d\) is absent or stays above some fixed positive fraction of \(d\), the [Haar Gram estimate](Yin_L_G0462_v68.md#low) bounds the number of nonzeros in \(P_d\).
Indeed all differences within a block of sufficiently short fractional diameter have zero nonconstant moments, and boundedly many blocks cover \([1,d-1]\).
Otherwise extract \(u=o(d)\).
The polynomial model again gives bounded support of \(P_d\) by the bounded-density Gram estimate in that argument.
In the geometric model the same argument bounds the support of \((1-\epsilon z^u)P_d\), including both truncated edges.
Since \(c=ku\) with bounded \(k\), the quotient \((1-z^{2c})/(1-\epsilon z^u)\) is a polynomial with \(2k\) terms for either sign.
This contradicts divergence in every case.

<a id="all-intervals"></a>
## Bounded support on every interval of length \(d\)

In fact the number of nonzero coefficients of \((1-z^{2c})B^\sharp\) in any interval of \(d\) consecutive indices is bounded uniformly along the sequence.
In particular its total number of terms is \(O(1+h/d)\), since \(c\le h\).
The bound on each interval also applies to the unmultiplied \(B^\sharp\) when \(c/d\) stays bounded away from zero or both signed sums are nonintegral.

Suppose by extraction that a sequence of intervals violates the asserted bound, so \(d\to\infty\).
Let \(S=\{0,d,e,N-e,N-d,N\}\) and write \(\lambda_s=[z^s]\Pi/j\), with \(\sum_{s\in S}|\lambda_s|\) bounded.
Every coefficient of \(B^\sharp\) away from the six indices in \(S\) is
\[
       b_l=2\int v^l \Big(\sum_{s\in S,\ s<l}\lambda_s v^{-s}\Big)\,d\omega_i .
\]
This uses the exact Taylor identity also beyond the last trace offset, where coefficients beyond the degree of \(B^\sharp\) are zero.
Between successive offsets the multiplier in parentheses is fixed and uniformly bounded on the circle.

When \(b_l\) vanishes through a fixed fraction of \(d\), characters with indices in a block of this diameter have signed Gram equal to the Haar Gram.
When \(u=o(d)\) and the prefix model is polynomial, the same Gram has norm at most two because the matching model probability has density at most two.
These two cases cover subsequences with no \(u=o(d)\) after reducing the first fraction if necessary.
For each fixed number of indices the additional Gram correction in passing to \(\omega_i^+\) is \(o(1)\), and replacing each integral against \(\omega_i\) by the positive-part integral costs \(o(1)\).
The Bessel bound with the fixed bounded multiplier then allows only boundedly many nonzero integer coefficients in the block.
Indeed the sum of squared corrected integrals is bounded by the Gram norm times the squared norm of twice the multiplier, while testing \(K\) nonzero integers gives a lower bound \(K(1-o(1))^2\) for every fixed \(K\).
Splitting an interval of length \(d\) at \(S\) and into blocks of the matching fractional diameter costs boundedly many blocks.
The same conclusion holds after multiplication by \(1-z^{2c}\) because this only takes a difference of two translates.

It remains to treat the geometric model with \(u=o(d)\).
First take \(U=1-\epsilon z^u\), and omit the at most twelve indices \(s,s+u\), \(s\in S\), where a Taylor constant enters.
Split the tested interval at these indices.
If \(l\) lies in no interval \(s<l<s+u\), each shifted term of \(\Pi/j\) contributes either both moments or neither to \([z^l]U B^\sharp\).
The coefficient is then the signed integral of the character \(v^l(1-\epsilon v^{-u})\) against twice a bounded multiplier times \(\omega_i\), with the multiplier fixed on this piece.
Within blocks of diameter \(\kappa_0d/2\), the signed Gram of the weighted characters agrees with that in the geometric model, since all differences and their shifts by \(\pm u\) belong to the matching prefix.
The weight kills the geometric model's atoms and leaves a bounded Gram norm by character orthogonality against Haar probability.
The fixed-size Bessel test just used applies equally to these weighted characters.

On every other piece some \(s<l<s+u\) holds throughout, so the diameter is strictly less than \(u\).
Use unweighted characters there, whose signed Gram equals the Haar Gram because all positive differences less than \(u\) have zero moment.
The multiplier now collects both the active terms from \(B^\sharp\) and the active terms from its translate \(-\epsilon z^uB^\sharp\).
It is fixed on the piece and has circle supremum at most twice \(\sum|\lambda_s|\) before doubling the measure.
The same Bessel test bounds the support in these transition pieces, including any overlapping offsets.
Thus \(U B^\sharp\) has bounded support in every interval of length \(d\), uniformly also in the location of that interval.
The quotient \((1-z^{2c})/U\) has boundedly many terms by the divisor conclusion, so the desired polynomial is a bounded sum of translates and satisfies the same assertion.
