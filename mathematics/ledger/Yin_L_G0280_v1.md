### 2. An exact whole-class hierarchy with degree-uniform error

Let sigma be normalized probability on the real sphere S^(s-1).
For s=1 it gives mass1/2 to each of its two points.
Define

    A_k(P)=integral N_P(c)^(2k) d sigma(c),
    D_(s,k)=binomial((k+1)s-1,s-1),
    b_k(P)=log|P(1)P(-1)|/(2s)-log(2)/2
                                      +log A_k(P)/(2ks),
    b(P)=sup_(primitive c in Z^s, c!=0) B_P(c).          (4)

All k in this section are positive integers.
These quantities include the complete original resultant; they do not replace it by a selected trace embedding.
Then

    b_k(P) <= b(P) <= b_k(P)+log D_(s,k)/(2ks),
    0 <= b(P)-b_k(P) <= log(e(k+1))/(2k).               (5)

In particular the approximation error is independent of both the actual input and its degree.
The computational dimension is NOT independent of degree.
Computing high moment levels need not be practical.

Here is the full proof.
N_P is not identically zero since N_P(1,0,...,0)=1.
Let M=max_(||c||=1)|N_P(c)|>0.
Homogeneity and density of rational directions on the sphere give

    b(P)=log|P(1)P(-1)|/(2s)-log(2)/2+(log M)/s.         (6)

The rational directions can be made primitive integral by clearing denominators and dividing the gcd, without changing B.
Irreducibility of Q ensures nonzero integral directions never have zero norm.
Continuity near a maximizing real direction proves equality of suprema; it does not assert attainment by an integer direction.

Let H_l be the restrictions to the sphere of real homogeneous polynomials of degree l=ks in s variables.
Restriction is injective: by homogeneity a polynomial vanishing on the sphere vanishes on every real nonzero vector, hence identically.
Thus dim H_l=binomial(l+s-1,s-1)=D_(s,k).
Equip it with its genuine L2(sigma) inner product and choose a real orthonormal basis e_1,...,e_D.
Orthogonal transformations preserve both the space and its inner product, so the diagonal kernel K(c)=sum e_i(c)^2 is invariant under O(s).
This group acts transitively on the sphere, and integral K=D.
Therefore K(c)=D everywhere.
Cauchy--Schwarz for the expansion of any f in H_l gives |f(c)|^2<=D integral f^2.
Apply this to f=N_P^k and combine with the trivial integral bound to obtain

    A_k(P) <= M^(2k) <= D_(s,k) A_k(P).                (7)

This proves the first line of(5).
It is precisely the classical invariant-kernel mechanism attributed to DGT20, now proved on the required homogeneous subspace with normalized probability.
It is not a new spherical Nikolskii theorem.
The case s=1 is also covered directly: D=1 and both sides of(7) coincide.

For the degree-uniform estimate, D_(s,k)<=binomial((k+1)s,s)<=(e(k+1))^s.
The first inequality is the adjacent binomial identity; the second follows from binomial(n,s)<=n^s/s! and log(s!)>=s log s-s, the latter from summing log j and its elementary integral.
This proves the second line of(5), including the s=1 boundary.
No inaccurate bound with exponent s-1 is being used.
The lower estimates b_k are nondecreasing by the probability-space Lp inequality, although the displayed upper estimates need not be monotone.

The exact rational calculation needs no quadrature.
If N_P(c)^(2k)=sum_a n_a c^a, odd-coordinate monomials have zero integral; for a=2nu one has

    integral c^(2nu) d sigma(c)
       =product_i (2nu_i-1)!! /
                      [s(s+2)...(s+2|nu|-2)],           (8)

with (-1)!!=1.
To check(8), take s independent standard real Gaussians.
Their joint density is radial, so polar integration makes direction uniform and independent of radius.
One-variable integration by parts gives E G_i^(2nu_i)=(2nu_i-1)!!, while radial integration by parts gives E ||G||^(2L)=s(s+2)...(s+2L-2).
Taking the ratio proves(8).
The homogeneous degree here is2ks, so L=ks.
Thus A_k is a positive rational number calculated exactly from an integral form, without floating integration or a root approximation.
