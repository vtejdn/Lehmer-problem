# Full degree growth in all square-root towers over the reciprocal cubic seed

<a id="all-towers"></a>
## Every nontorsion factor retains its measure at every step

For every positive odd \(n\), set \(g(t)=t^3-t-1\), \(b(t)=1-t^2-t^3\), \(f=-g/b\), and \(A_n(x)=g(x^n)+xb(x^n)\).
Let \(\alpha\) be any nontorsion root, \(P\) its monic minimal polynomial, \(K=\mathbb Q(\alpha)\), and \(d=\deg P\).
Then neither \(\alpha\) nor \(-\alpha\) is a square in \(K\).
Consequently for every \(k\ge0\) and every \(\gamma\) satisfying \(\gamma^{2^k}=\alpha\),
\[
 [\mathbb Q(\gamma):\mathbb Q]=2^k d,\qquad P_\gamma(x)=P(x^{2^k}),
 \qquad m(P_\gamma)=m(P),
\]
where \(m\) is logarithmic Mahler measure.

Indeed the first quadratic extension has degree two because \(\alpha\) is a nonsquare.
If \(x^{2^k}-\alpha\) is irreducible over \(K\) for \(k\ge1\), the norm of any of its roots down to \(K\) equals \(-\alpha\).
A square root in the same extension would make that norm a square in \(K\).
This proves the induction, and the power identity for absolute height gives the measure equality.

<a id="dyadic"></a>
## Two reductions at the prime two

Put \(\beta=\alpha^n\).
The identity \(-tg(t)+(1-t)b(t)=1\) and the equation \(g(\beta)=-\alpha b(\beta)\) make \(b(\beta)\) a unit and give \(\alpha=f(\beta)\).
Thus \(K=\mathbb Q(\beta)\), and both \(\alpha,\beta\) are units since \(-A_n\) is monic with constant term \(1\).

First suppose \(P(1)\) is even.
Since \(A_n(1)=A_n(-1)=-2\) and \(P(-1)\equiv P(1)\pmod2\), both \(|P(1)|\) and \(|P(-1)|\) equal \(2\).
For either \(\epsilon\in\{1,-1\}\), the principal ideal generated by \(1-\epsilon\alpha\) in the integer ring of \(K\) is therefore prime of norm two.
If \(\epsilon\alpha=u^2\) in \(K\), then \(u\) is an algebraic integer unit and reduces to \(1\) at this prime.
Both \(1-u\) and \(1+u\) belong to the prime, contradicting the valuation one of their product.

Now suppose \(P(1)\) is odd.
All roots of \(\overline P\) in \(\overline{\mathbb F}_2\) are simple, where a bar denotes reduction modulo two.
In fact, at a root \(a\) of \(\overline A_n\), put \(t=a^n\).
The Bézout identity gives \(b(t)\ne0\), so \(a=f(t)\).
A repeated root of \(b(x^n)(x-f(x^n))\) would give \(n t f'(t)=f(t)\).
Since \(n\) is odd and
\[
             t(g'b-gb')-gb\equiv(t+1)^6\pmod2,
\]
such a root has \(t=1\), hence \(a=f(1)=1\) in characteristic two.
The polynomial \(\overline P\) has no root there.
Its \(d\) roots \(a\) give \(d\) distinct values \(t=a^n\), since \(a=f(t)\).

<a id="frobenius"></a>
## A lift of Frobenius modulo four detects both signs of a square

Continue with \(P(1)\) odd.
The discriminant of \(P\) is odd by squarefreeness modulo two, so the index of \(\mathbb Z[\alpha]\) in the integer ring \(\mathcal O_K\) is odd by the order discriminant formula.
In particular \(B=\mathcal O_K/4\mathcal O_K=(\mathbb Z/4)[x]/(P)\) under \(x\mapsto\alpha\).
This ring admits an endomorphism \(\sigma\) reducing modulo two to squaring.
To construct it, lift \(x^2\) to a root \(x^2+2w\) of \(P\) in \(B\).
The element \(P(x^2)\) vanishes modulo two, and \(P'(x^2)\) is a unit modulo two because \(P\) is squarefree there.
The equation \(P(x^2)+2wP'(x^2)=0\) therefore has a solution in \(B\).
Sending \(x\) to this root defines \(\sigma\).

Suppose \(\alpha=\epsilon u^2\) with \(\epsilon=\pm1\) and \(u\in K\).
Both \(u\) and its inverse are integral.
Since \(\sigma(u)\equiv u^2\bmod2\), squaring gives \(\sigma(u)^2=u^4\) in \(B\).
Thus \(\sigma(\alpha)=\epsilon\alpha^2\), and oddness of \(n\) gives \(\sigma(\beta)=\epsilon\beta^2\).
The unit \(b(\beta)\) remains a unit under \(\sigma\), so
\[
                   f(\epsilon\beta^2)=\epsilon f(\beta)^2\quad\text{in }B.
\]
The [two cleared identities](Yin_L_G0462_v158.md#cyclotomic) give, respectively,
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 -g(t^2)b(t)^2-g(t)^2b(t^2)&=-2tL(t),\\
 -g(-t^2)b(t)^2+g(t)^2b(-t^2)&=2\Phi_{12}(t)\Phi_{30}(t),\\
 L(t)&=t^{10}+t^9-t^7-t^6-t^5-t^4-t^3+t+1 .
 \end{aligned}
\]
Canceling the factor two from divisibility by four gives \(L(\beta)=0\bmod2\) for \(\epsilon=1\), since \(\beta\) is a unit, and \(\Phi_{12}(\beta)\Phi_{30}(\beta)=0\bmod2\) for \(\epsilon=-1\).
In each case the indicated polynomial must vanish at every one of the \(d\) distinct residues \(t=a^n\) above.

<a id="minus"></a>
## Exclusion of the negative sign

In characteristic two \(\Phi_{12}=\Phi_3^2\) and \(\Phi_{30}=\Phi_{15}\).
An element \(t\) of order \(15\) satisfies \(f(t)=t^9\) by \(g(t)+t^9 b(t)=-(t^4+1)\Phi_{30}(t)\).
Then \(a=f(t)\) has order \(5\), whereas \(a^n=t\) has order \(15\).
The only remaining residues \(t\) have order three, so \(d\le2\).

The polynomial \(A_n\) has no real root.
For \(x\ge1\) the expression \((1-x)(x^{3n}-1)-x^n-x^{2n+1}\) is negative, while for \(u\ge1\) one has
\[
 A_n(-u)=-(1+u)(u^{3n}+1)+u^n+u^{2n+1}<0.
\]
Here \(u^n\le u^{3n}\) and \(u^{2n+1}\le u^{3n+1}\).
Reciprocity and \(A_n(0)=-1\) exclude all remaining real arguments.
A quadratic unit with nonreal conjugates has norm \(1\) and both conjugates on the unit circle, hence is torsion by Kronecker's theorem.
This excludes every nontorsion factor of degree at most two.

<a id="plus"></a>
## The positive sign leaves one finite calculation

In \(\mathbb F_2[t]\), put
\[
 q_1=t^5+t^3+t^2+t+1,\qquad q_2=t^5+t^4+t^3+t^2+1.
\]
Direct division gives \(\overline L=q_1q_2\) and, for both \(q_i\),
\[
            t^{32}-t\equiv0,\qquad t^{23}b(t)+g(t)\equiv0\pmod{q_i}.
\]
Neither \(q_i\) has a root in \(\mathbb F_2\), so its irreducible factors have degree dividing five and greater than one.
Each \(q_i\) is therefore irreducible, with all roots of multiplicative order \(31\).
For any of the \(d\) residues above, \(a=f(t)=t^{23}\).
Thus \(d\le10\) and \(23n\equiv1\pmod{31}\), or \(n\equiv27\pmod{31}\).

We next bound \(n\) over the integers.
The Bézout identity gives \(\max(|g(z)|,|b(z)|)\ge1/3\) for \(|z|\le1\).
Reversal \(b(z)=z^3g(1/z)\) then gives \(\max(|g(z)|,|b(z)|)\ge\max(1,|z|)^3/3\) for every \(z\).
Taking archimedean means over \(K\), using the unit \(b(\beta)\) and \(\alpha=f(\beta)\), gives \(h(\alpha)\ge3h(\beta)-\log3\) for absolute logarithmic height \(h\).
Since \(h(\beta)=n h(\alpha)\), we obtain \(h(\beta)\le\log3/(3-1/n)\).
Every nontorsion factor has \(d\ge2\), so [Voutier's Corollary 2](../audits/literature/Vou96/Yin_U001.md#height) gives \(d h(\alpha)>2/(\log(3d))^3\).
For \(n\ge3\) and \(d\le10\) these bounds imply
\[
               n<\frac{3\log3}{8}\,\frac{10(\log30)^3}{2}<82,
\]
where \(\log3<1.1\) and \(\log30<3.41\) suffice.
The only odd \(n\) in this range congruent to \(27\bmod31\) is \(27\), and \(A_1=-(x^4+1)\) is entirely cyclotomic.

At \(n=27\) the monic polynomial is \(H=-A_{27}=x^{82}-x^{81}+x^{55}+x^{27}-x+1\).
Over \(\mathbb F_3\) put \(r_0=x\) and \(r_j=r_{j-1}^3\bmod H\).
For \(j=6,7,8,9,10\) the respective degrees of \(r_j\) are \(80,81,81,79,80\), and every \(\gcd(H,r_j-x)\) equals one.
The following finite calculation reproduces the divisions.

~~~python
from sympy import Poly, symbols

x = symbols("x")
X = Poly(x, x, modulus=3)
H = X**82 - X**81 + X**55 + X**27 - X + 1
r = X
for j in range(1, 11):
    r = (r**3).rem(H)
    if j >= 6:
        print(j, r.degree(), H.gcd(r - X).as_expr())
~~~

Every integer from one through ten divides at least one of these five \(j\)'s.
Any modular irreducible factor of such a degree would divide one of \(x^{3^j}-x\), contrary to these gcds.
A monic integral factor of degree at most ten would retain its degree modulo three and have a modular irreducible divisor of degree at most ten.
This excludes \(n=27\) and completes the positive-sign proof.

## The remaining factor-degree question

The [factor-height comparison](Yin_L_G0462_v158.md#factor-height) and the exact measure identity now give \(m(P_\gamma)=m(P)\asymp d/n\) for every branch of every square-root tower of these seeds.
In particular the [reciprocal inputs constructed by square-root lifting](../claims/Yin_C_G0345_v371.md#lift) can have measures tending to zero exactly along selections whose base degrees satisfy \(d/n\to0\).
The existence of such a sequence of base factors, and the existence of a uniform positive lower bound for the largest base degree divided by \(n\), remain open here.
