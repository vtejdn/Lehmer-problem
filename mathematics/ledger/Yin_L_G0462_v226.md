# Two primes determine the least rational factor degree independently of the number of factors

<a id="minimum"></a>
## A degree threshold independent of the number of rational factors

Let \(H\in\mathbb Z[Y]\) be monic, separable and nonconstant, and suppose its Galois group on the roots contains the product of the alternating groups acting independently on the rational irreducible factors.
Let \(m\) be the smallest of their degrees and \(S(p)\) the set of positive degrees of monic divisors of \(H\bmod p\).
For \(m\ge81\), two good primes determine \(m\) by
\[
 m=\max_{\substack{p\ne q\\p\equiv u\bmod M,\ q\equiv v\bmod N}}
             \min\bigl(S(p)\cap S(q)\bigr),                         \tag{1}
\]
where \(p,q\) range over primes prime to \(\operatorname{disc}H\), and their two reduced residue classes are arbitrary.
The bound is uniform in the number of rational factors and in the number of them having any particular degree.
The [two-prime formula with a bound on the number of factors](../claims/Yin_C_G0345_v402.md#pair) uses cycles of length one at one prime.
Here the smallest cycles used at that prime have length \(\lfloor m/2\rfloor\), so at most two can contribute to a degree below \(m\).

Every rational factor of degree \(m\) reduces to a monic divisor of that degree at both primes.
For the reverse inequality, at each prime fix any feasible vector of signs on the rational factors in the corresponding [cyclotomic fiber](../claims/Yin_C_G0345_v402.md#classes).
On each rational factor of degree \(d\) take a full \(d\)-cycle whenever its sign agrees with that vector.
Otherwise take two cycles of positive lengths summing to \(d\), which gives the opposite sign.
The independent alternating groups and Chebotarev's theorem realize the prescriptions below at arbitrarily large distinct primes in their chosen classes.
All full cycles have length at least \(m\).

<a id="second"></a>
## The degrees at the second prime

Write \(h=\lfloor m/2\rfloor\) and \(j=\lfloor 2m/9\rfloor\).
At \(q\), use lengths \(j,d-j\) on every block requiring two cycles, except when \(d=m+j-1\), where the lengths are \(j+1,m-2\).
Thus a short cycle has length \(j\) or \(j+1\), and its partner always has length at least \(m-j\) and never \(m-1\).
Indeed \(d-j=m-1\) only at the exceptional degree, and \(m-2\ge m-j\) because \(j\ge2\).
A sum involving a long partner and any further cycle is at least \(m\), since \(m-j>m/2\) and every other cycle has length at least \(j\).
Also \(5j>m\), since \(j>2m/9-1\) and \(m\ge81\).
Consequently
\[
 S(q)\cap[1,m-1]\ \subseteq\
 [m-j,m-2]\ \cup\ \bigcup_{k=1}^4[kj,kj+k].                      \tag{2}
\]
In particular \(m-1\) is absent, since \(4(j+1)\le8m/9+4<m-1\).

<a id="first"></a>
## The degrees at the first prime

Set
\[
       A=\bigl([h,m-j-1]\setminus[3j,3j+3]\bigr)\cap\mathbb Z .
\]
No element of \(A\) belongs to the right side of (2).
Indeed \(2(j+1)\le4m/9+2<h\), the third interval has been deleted, and \(4j>m-j-1\) follows from \(5j>m\).
The interval for the long cycles begins at \(m-j\).
Also \(h\in A\), since \(h<3j\) and \(h\le m-j-1\).

For \(p\), each \(d\ge m+h\) requiring two cycles is written as \(h+(d-h)\), whose second term is at least \(m\).
For \(m\le d<m+h\), start with \(x=\lfloor d/2\rfloor\) and \(y=\lceil d/2\rceil\).
These integers lie in \([h,m-j-1]\), since \(y\le(m+h)/2\le3m/4\le7m/9-1\le m-j-1\).
If one belongs to \([3j,3j+3]\), replace them by \(x-4,y+4\).
In this case \(3j-1\le x\le3j+3\) and \(3j\le y\le3j+4\), so the two new terms avoid the deleted interval.
They remain in the allowed range because
\[
 x-4\ \ge\ 3j-5\ >\ 2m/3-8\ \ge\ h,\qquad
 y+4\ \le\ 3j+8\ \le\ 2m/3+8\ \le\ 7m/9-1\ \le\ m-j-1 .
\]
This gives a two-cycle decomposition for every degree with the required sign, with every length either in \(A\) or at least \(m\).

Every sum of two lengths from \(A\) is at least \(2h\ge m-1\), and a sum of three exceeds \(m\).
Thus \(S(p)\cap[1,m-1]\subseteq A\cup\{m-1\}\), including the possibility of several blocks of the smallest odd degree.
The right side is disjoint from (2), proving (1).
Any pair certifies its computed lower bound for the smallest rational degree, while (1) guarantees the existence of a pair attaining the exact value.
