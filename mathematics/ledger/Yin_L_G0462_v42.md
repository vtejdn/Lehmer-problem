# Cyclic resultants under changes of exponent and the five-term reciprocal case

<a id="substitution"></a>
## Divisors evaluated at different powers

Let \(P\in\mathbb Z[z]\) have no zeros among the \(2n\)-th roots of unity, and put \(C_k(P)=|\operatorname{Res}(P,z^k-1)|\) and \(m=\log M\).
For each sign \(\epsilon=\pm1\), suppose that in the quotient \(\mathbb Z[z]/(z^n-\epsilon)\) there is a factorization
\[
     P(z)=c_\epsilon G_\epsilon(z)
                     \prod_i A_{\epsilon,i}(z^{a_{\epsilon,i}}),
          \qquad c_\epsilon\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\},\quad
             G_\epsilon\in\mathbb Z[z].
\]
The lists may be empty, factors may repeat, and each \(A=A_{\epsilon,i}\) is a primitive irreducible integer polynomial with \(m(A)>0\) and degree \(t=t_{\epsilon,i}\).
For its individually chosen positive exponent \(a=a_{\epsilon,i}\) put \(g=\gcd(a,n)\) and \(p=p_{\epsilon,i}=n/g\).
For an absolute \(K\), set
\[
   \Delta=K\max_{\epsilon,i}\frac{t^3\log^4(2t)\log(2p)}p ,
\]
with an empty maximum equal to zero.
For \(\Delta<1\) one has
\[
  \frac{\log C_{2n}(P)}n
       \ \ge\ \log|c_+c_-|+(1-\Delta)\sum_{\epsilon,i}m(A_{\epsilon,i}).
                                                                        \tag{1}
\]
In particular, on any sequence of irreducible algebraic units of degree \(d\) with \(1<M(P)\to1\), the sum on the right tends to zero uniformly over these decompositions when \(n/d\) stays in a fixed compact subinterval of \((0,\infty)\) and \(\Delta\) stays below a fixed constant less than one.
This follows from the [original degree-window bound](../claims/Yin_C_G0391_v1.md#bounded-power-degree-escape).
The exponents need not be invertible modulo \(n\), and the degrees of the polynomials \(A(z^a)\) can exceed the full period.
The proof-side contributor obtained this extension of the [fixed-generator modular divisor test](Yin_L_G0462_v35.md#factor-test) and the following applications for [Yin's Lehmer Task](../tasks/Yin_T_G0001_v1.md#contract) on 2026-11-16.

The powers \(y=z^a\) at \(z^n=\epsilon\) run through the solutions of \(y^p=\epsilon^{a/g}\), each exactly \(g\) times, by the kernel and image of the power map on the \(n\)-th roots of unity.
Consequently the absolute resultant contribution is
\[
       |\operatorname{Res}(A,y^p-\epsilon^{a/g})|^g .
\]
The [signed root-distance estimate](Yin_L_G0462_v35.md#middle-span) applied to the roots of \(A\) gives, for either sign \(\eta\) with nonzero resultant,
\[
   \left|\log|\operatorname{Res}(A,y^p-\eta)|-p\,m(A)\right|
       \le C t^3\log(2t)\log(2p)(1+m(A)).                             \tag{2}
\]
Here the proof for an unrestricted-height primitive factor uses its actual degree and measure, with a normalized upper distance of at most two at every root.
The [positive-measure degree bound](../claims/Yin_C_G0895_v1.md#transfer) gives \(m(A)\ge c/\log^3(2t)\), including a nonunit leading coefficient since that gives \(m(A)\ge\log2\).
Equations (2) and \(gp=n\) give a lower bound \(n(1-\Delta)m(A)\) on each contribution to the logarithm.
Each cofactor resultant is a nonzero integer, and the scalar contributes \(n\log|c_\epsilon|\).
Multiplication over both signs proves (1).

<a id="five-term-return"></a>
## A uniform return at the exact degree for every five-term reciprocal input

There is an absolute \(\gamma>0\) such that every monic irreducible reciprocal integer polynomial \(P\) of degree \(d\) with \(M(P)>1\) and at most five terms satisfies
\[
                        \frac{\log C_d(P)}d\ge\gamma .                  \tag{3}
\]
There is no restriction on the positions or sizes of the coefficients or on the fields generated by powers of a root.
Since a reciprocal polynomial of odd degree has the root \(-1\), write \(d=2n\).
The trinomial \(P=z^{2n}+c z^n+1\) can have positive measure only for \(|c|\ge3\), and then \(C_d(P)=|c^2-4|^n\).
Every other possible input has the form
\[
 P=z^{2n}+1+c z^n+b(z^{n+r}+z^{n-r}),\qquad 0<r<n,\quad b\ne0 .       \tag{4}
\]
Take \(g=\gcd(r,n)\), \(p=n/g,\ k=r/g\), and denote its primitive exponent pattern by \(Q\), so \(P=Q(z^g)\).
At \(z^n=\epsilon\), multiplication by a unit-modulus monomial and sign gives the quadratic
\[
     A_\epsilon(y)=b y^2+(c+2\epsilon)y+b,\qquad y=z^r .
\]
The exact product is
\[
 C_d(P)=
 \left(\prod_{\epsilon=\pm1}|\operatorname{Res}(A_\epsilon,
                                     y^p-\epsilon^k)|\right)^g
       = C_{2p}(Q)^g .                                                 \tag{5}
\]

For \(|b|\ge2\) both logarithmic Mahler measures are at least \(\log2\).
For \(|b|=1,\ c=0\), (4) equals \((z^{n-r}+b)(z^{n+r}+b)\), so this input is excluded.
For \(|b|=1,\ c\ne0\), at least one quadratic has \(|c+2\epsilon|\ge3\) and a root of modulus at least \((3+\sqrt5)/2\).
In particular the sum of the two logarithmic measures has a uniform positive lower bound in all admissible cases.
The root argument of (2) in degree two applies also to these possibly reducible quadratics with content, since each root degree is at most two and each primitive factor measure is at most \(M(A_\epsilon)\).
The leading coefficient enters the resultant and the Mahler measure with exactly the same exponent.
Thus the right side of (5), after taking its logarithm and dividing by \(d\), is at least a positive absolute constant for all \(p\) above an absolute threshold, uniformly even in \(b,c\).

For the remaining \(p\) it suffices to exclude \(C_{2p}(Q)=1\).
If it were one, the nonzero integers \(Q(1)=2+c+2b\) and \(Q(-1)\) would both have absolute value one.
If \(p\) is even then \(k\) is odd and \(Q(-1)=2+c-2b\), which differs from \(Q(1)\) by \(4b\).
If \(p\) is odd and \(k\) even then \(Q(-1)=4-Q(1)\).
Neither case permits both unit values.
For odd \(p,k\), their sum is \(4+4b\), forcing \(b=-1,\ c=\pm1\).
The quadratic with \(|c+2\epsilon|=3\) has its two roots of moduli \(\lambda=(3+\sqrt5)/2\) and \(\lambda^{-1}\), so its absolute resultant is at least \(\lambda^p(1-\lambda^{-p})^2>1\) for \(p\ge2\).
This rules out the last case, proving \(C_{2p}(Q)\ge2\).
For bounded \(p\) the lower bound \(\log2/(2p)\) completes (3).

<a id="sharp-unit-coefficients"></a>
## The sharp rate for off-center coefficients of absolute value one

For (4) with \(|b|=1\) the optimal absolute constant on the right of (3) is \(\log(45)/8\).
Indeed, take the sign with \(|c+2\epsilon|=|c|+2\) and let \(\lambda>1\) be the larger root modulus of that quadratic.
The companion resultant in (5) is a nonzero integer, so
\[
 \frac{\log C_d(P)}d
      \ge \frac12\log\lambda+\frac1p\log(1-\lambda^{-p}).             \tag{6}
\]
This expression increases in \(p\ge2\) and in \(\lambda>1\).
For \(|c|\ge2\), \(\lambda+\lambda^{-1}\ge4\), and at \(p=2\) the resulting lower bound is \(\log12/4>\log45/8\).
For \(|c|=1,\ p\ge4\), \(\lambda+\lambda^{-1}=3\), and at \(p=4\) the bound equals \(\log45/8\).
For the remaining \(|c|=1\) the exact products (5) at primitive exponents are as follows.

| \(p\) | \(b\) | \(c\) | \(C_{2p}(Q)\) at \(k=1,\ldots,p-1\) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | either sign | \(-1\) | 27 |
| 2 | either sign | \(1\) | 5 |
| 3 | \(-1\) | \(-1\) | 64, 80 |
| 3 | \(1\) | \(-1\) | 0, 0 |
| 3 | \(-1\) | \(1\) | 64, 0 |
| 3 | \(1\) | \(1\) | 0, 80 |

These entries follow by writing \(u=-(c+2\epsilon)/b\) and using \(|2-\epsilon^k T_p(u)|\) for each absolute quadratic resultant, with \(T_2=u^2-2,\ T_3=u^3-3u\).
The two values 5 occur at \(Q=\Phi_5\) or \(\Phi_{10}\), whose substitutions remain cyclotomic products.
The zero entries also have torsion roots.
Every other entry gives the asserted bound.

It is attained by \(P=z^8-z^5+z^4-z^3+1\).
Here \(p=4,\ k=1,\ b=-1,\ c=1\), and the two quadratic resultant magnitudes in (5) are 45 and 1.
Its trace polynomial is \(J(x)=x^4-4x^2-x+3\), irreducible modulo two because \(x^4+x+1\) has no linear factor and has remainder one on division by \(x^2+x+1\).
Since \(J(0)>0>J(1)\), the trace field has an embedding in which \(x^2-4<0\); consequently the quadratic lift \(P\) is irreducible.
The polynomial \(J\) has no negative root: for \(u\ge1\), \(J(-u)=(u^2-2)^2+u-1>0\), and for \(0\le u\le1\), \(J(-u)=(u^2-1)(u^2-3)+u>0\).
The sum of its roots is zero and it has a positive root, so some roots are nonreal, which gives \(M(P)>1\).

<a id="seven-term-unit"></a>
## Seven terms suffice for a unit resultant at the exact degree

No positive irreducible reciprocal input of at most six terms can have \(C_d=1\).
For six terms the central coefficient is zero by symmetry, so \(P(1)\) is even and divides the nonzero integer \(C_d\) up to sign.
For at most five terms the exclusion follows from (3).
The support bound for this exact unit question is optimal.
Take
\[
      Q(z)=z^{14}-2z^{10}+z^8+z^7+z^6-2z^4+1.
\]
Its trace polynomial \(J=T_7-2T_3+T_1+1=x^7-7x^5+12x^3+1\) is irreducible modulo three.
Indeed \(x^{3^7}\equiv x\bmod J\) over \(\mathbb F_3\), and \(J(0),J(1),J(-1)\) are \(1,7,-5\), all nonzero modulo three.
Since 7 is prime this excludes every proper factor.
The signs at \(-3,-2,-1,0\) are respectively \(-,+,-,+\), giving one trace root below \(-2\) and another in \((-1,0)\).
The latter real embedding excludes a square discriminant \(x^2-4\) for the quadratic lift, so \(Q\) is irreducible and the former gives \(M(Q)>1\).
The signed resultants of \(Q\) with \(\Phi_1,\Phi_2,\Phi_7,\Phi_{14}\) are respectively \(1,-1,1,1\).
The two finite arithmetic checks can be reproduced directly.

~~~python
from sympy import Poly, cyclotomic_poly, symbols

x = symbols("x")
J = Poly(x**7 - 7*x**5 + 12*x**3 + 1, x, modulus=3)
X = Poly(x, x, modulus=3)
r = X
for i in range(7):
    r = (r**3).rem(J)
assert r == X
Q = Poly(x**14 - 2*x**10 + x**8 + x**7 + x**6 - 2*x**4 + 1, x)
assert [Q.resultant(Poly(cyclotomic_poly(i,x), x))
        for i in (1,2,7,14)] == [1,-1,1,1]
~~~

For every sufficiently large prime \(\ell\), \(P=Q(z^\ell)\) is irreducible by the [prime-power substitution argument](Yin_L_G0462_v35.md#unit-at-twice-degree), and its degree is \(d=14\ell\).
Every such polynomial has exactly seven terms and \(C_d(P)=C_{14}(Q)^\ell=1\).
Therefore (1) forbids even a single positive-measure factor in any of the displayed modular factorizations with \(\Delta<1\) at \(n=d/2\).
This example leaves open the existence of a uniform positive exponential rate at \(d\) for six terms.

<a id="adjacent-exponent"></a>
## The adjacent exponent for power substitutions

For this seven-term family the normalized logarithm at \(d+1\) tends to \(m(Q)>0\).
More generally suppose \(P(z)=Q(z^g)\) is irreducible of positive measure, for monic \(Q\in\mathbb Z[z]\) of degree \(D\), and \(d=gD\).
Since \(\gcd(d+1,g)=1\), the power \(z\mapsto z^g\) permutes the \((d+1)\)-st roots of unity and \(C_{d+1}(P)=C_{d+1}(Q)\).
Applying (2) and the same degree lower bound to the irreducible \(Q\) gives
\[
 \left|\frac{\log C_{d+1}(P)}{(d+1)m(P)}-1\right|
        \le C \frac{D^3\log^4(2D)\log(2d)}{d+1},
\]
where \(m(P)=m(Q)\) by circle integration.
In particular the relative error tends to zero when \(D\le d^{1/3-\eta}\) for a fixed \(0<\eta<1/3\), without a height bound.
The adjacent exponent recovers the original measure in that range even when the exact-degree norm is one.
