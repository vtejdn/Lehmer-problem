# The reciprocal coefficient series and the first departure on the leading-two path

<a id="signed-measure"></a>
## One negative atom for every positive-constant input

For the [normalized Pisot series](Yin_L_G0462_v36.md#limit) \(K_q(0)=j\), the rational function \(J_q=j/K_q\) is the Herglotz transform of a real symmetric signed measure \(\omega_q\) on the circle, of total mass one.
Its only negative mass is the atom \(-j/[4(q-1)]\) at 1.
This formula controls all Fourier indices at once.
Here the transform of a symmetric circle measure has series \(\omega(\mathbb T)+2\sum_{l\ge1}(\int v^l\,d\omega)z^l\).

Use \(F=jB/C=x-a-\sum_i w_i/(x-y_i)\) from the [inverse simplex](Yin_L_G0462_v43.md#endpoints), where \(w_i>0\), \(-2<y_i<2\), and set \(d\kappa(y)=\sum_i w_i(4-y_i^2)^{-1}\delta_{y_i}\).
For \(x=z+z^{-1}\) we have \(J_q=zF(x)/(1-z^2)\).
The identity \(4-y^2=(4-x^2)+(x-y)(x+y)\) gives
\[
 F(x)=(1-\kappa(\mathbb R))x-a-\int y\,d\kappa
                        -(4-x^2)\int\frac{d\kappa(y)}{x-y}.
\]
Multiplying by \(z/(1-z^2)\) turns the last term into the Herglotz transform of the symmetric lift of \(\kappa\).
Indeed \((1-z^2)/(1-yz+z^2)\) is the average of the circle kernels at the inverse pair over \(y\).
The remaining term is the sum of the endpoint kernels with weights \(\alpha=F(2)/4\), \(\beta=-F(-2)/4\), by evaluation after multiplying by \(1-z^2\).
The endpoint formulas give \(\alpha=-j/[4(q-1)]\) and \(\beta\ge j/[4(q+1)]>0\).
This proves the assertion including the case \(\kappa=0\).

<a id="path"></a>
## An integral discrepancy in a growing reciprocal denominator

Take \(C_h(x)=2U_h(x/2)-U_{h-1}(x/2)\) with \(U_l\) the Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind.
Suppose that the exact inverse test gives Pisot inputs with \(q\to\infty\) along this family.
Then \(h\to\infty\) by \(q-1\mid h+2\).
On a subsequence let \(K_q\) tend to one of the [nine fixed candidates](Yin_L_G0462_v44.md#profiles) \(K=(2-z)U_0/V\), where here \(U_0,V\) denote the coprime cyclotomic products in that list.
Write \(B^\sharp=z^{h+1}B(z+z^{-1})\), \(B_\infty=V/U_0\), and \(J=2/K=B_\infty/(1-z/2)\).
The [exact path numerator](Yin_L_G0462_v44.md#profiles) gives
\[
   B^\sharp=(1-z/2)J_q+O(z^{2h+1}).
\]
The integral series \(B^\sharp\) consequently agrees with \(B_\infty\) through every fixed order eventually.
The first index \(n\) of discrepancy must be at most \(h+1\) for all sufficiently large inputs.
Otherwise the signed reciprocal polynomial \(U_0 B^\sharp\) agrees with \(V\) through that index \(h+1\) and its reciprocal symmetry fills the opposite end, leaving at most \(\deg U_0+2\) middle coefficients.
Its number of terms would be bounded, whereas its measure equals the unique exterior root of the lift of \(B\), strictly above one and tending to one by the [delay-one return and fixed-support gap](Yin_L_G0462_v32.md#boyd-return).
Thus \(n\to\infty\), \(2h-n\to\infty\), and all fixed shifts of \(n\) lie in the displayed Taylor range.

<a id="departure"></a>
## An analytic function dominated by the continuous density

There is a polynomial \(H\in\mathbb Z[z]\) with \(H(0)\ne0\) such that for almost every \(v\) on the circle,
\[
       |H(v)|\le |1-v/2|\,\sigma(v),
       \qquad \sigma(v)=2\Re J(v).
                                                                        \tag{1}
\]
In fact \(F_1=H/(1-z/2)\) has boundary modulus at most \(\sigma\).
The coefficients of \(H\) are the successive limiting deviations of \(B^\sharp\) from \(B_\infty\) starting at \(n\).

To see this, the positive measures \(2(\omega_q-\alpha\delta_1)\) have mass tending to two.
Their moments converge at each fixed index to those of a measure \(\tau\) with Herglotz transform \(2J\).
For the rational \(J\) here this measure has a bounded rational continuous density \(\sigma\) and finitely many atoms.
Indeed each circle pole of a rational Herglotz function contributes the kernel of its atom, as follows by taking the radial limit of \((1-r)J(rv)\) in the integral formula.
The kernel bound on radii prevents higher poles, and the real part of the remaining function, regular across the circle, gives the density by the Poisson formula.
The atoms are at roots of unity by the displayed form of \(J\).

Push the positive measures to pairs \((v,u)=(v,v^n)\) and take a weak subsequential limit \(\Omega\).
It has first marginal \(\tau\), and \(\chi(dv)=\int u\,d\Omega(v,u)\) has variation dominated by \(\tau\).
Extract with \(v^n\) fixed at each atom of \(\tau\) and let \(\chi_0\) be the atomic part of \(\tau\) weighted by these phases.
The coefficient \(\int v^l\,d(\chi-\chi_0)\) is the limiting difference of the coefficients of \(J_q,J\) at \(n+l\), for each fixed integer \(l\).
Indeed the removed mass at 1 tends to zero for every index, and the Fourier coefficients of the bounded density of \(\tau\) tend to zero at infinity.
For \(l<0\) the difference is zero by prefix agreement and the unit constant in \(1-z/2\).
Consequently \(\chi-\chi_0\) has no atoms, as averaging its vanishing negative-index characters after multiplying by the cancelling phase at a given point recovers the mass of that point.
Its remaining density therefore has modulus at most \(\sigma\).
Reflecting the circle gives an analytic boundary function \(F_1\) whose Taylor coefficients are the differences at \(l=0,1,\ldots\).
Explicitly the reflected density has zero negative Fourier coefficients, so its Poisson extension is analytic, bounded and has that density as radial boundary values.
Reflection preserves \(\sigma\) by the real symmetry.

Multiplication by \(1-z/2\) gives the limiting discrepancies of the two integral \(B\) series in this window.
Every coefficient of \(H=(1-z/2)F_1\) is therefore integral.
At the first one, a convergent sequence of nonzero integers has nonzero integer limit.
The bounded analytic function \(H\) has square summable coefficients by Parseval, so only finitely many of these integers are nonzero.
This proves (1).

## Six exclusions and three remaining profiles

Write \(\rho=2\Re K\) on the circle away from the finitely many poles and zeros.
Jensen's inequality for the rational \(F_1\) and (1) give
\[
      1\le |F_1(0)|\le M(F_1)\le M(\sigma)
                 =\frac{2M(\rho)}{M(K)^2}=\frac{M(\rho)}2 .
\]
Here \(M(K)=2\) by its cyclotomic factors, and \(\sigma=2\rho/|K|^2\).
Thus four of the nine profiles are excluded by their value \(M(\rho)=1\).
For the two cases \(M(\rho)=2\) in the table we have \(U_0=1\), \(V=1-z^2\) or \(1-z+z^2\), and \(\rho=2\).
Put \(D_0=V/(1-z/2)\).
Then \(\sigma=|D_0|^2\), \(D_0(0)=1\), and (1) bounds \(|F_1/D_0^2|\) by one on the circle.
This quotient is rational and has no pole in the disk, since \(V\) has only circle roots.
Any circle pole would contradict the modulus bound on nearby regular points.
At zero its modulus is at least one, so the maximum principle forces \(F_1=\pm D_0^2\).
But the coefficient of \(z\) in \(H=\pm V^2/(1-z/2)\) is \(\pm(2V'(0)+1/2)\), which is not an integer.
This excludes both boundary cases.

The only candidates left on this particular leading-two path are
\[
             K=2-z,\qquad K=\frac{2-z}{1-z},\qquad K=(2-z)(1+z).
\]
They remain necessary shapes of a subsequential limit.
The proof gives neither an integer denominator attaining any of these limits nor a sequence of Salem numbers approaching one.
