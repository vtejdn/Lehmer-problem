### 2. The primitive alternating kernel and a full scalar identity

Let

    V(a,b,c)=[[1,a,b],[0,1,c],[0,0,1]], a,b,c in Z,
    E=a^2+b^2+c^2, D=E-abc.

Expansion of the full pencil gives

    p_V(t)=(t+1)[t^2+(2-D)t+1].                    (1)

Equivalently its four coefficients are1,3-D,3-D,1 in ascending order.
No factor has been removed; every proper rank-three polynomial necessarily has the displayed Phi2 factor.

If at least one of a,b,c is nonzero, the alternating matrix V-V^T has rank two.
Set g=gcd(|a|,|b|,|c|)>0.
Its integral kernel is the primitive line generated by

    v=(c,-b,a)^T/g,
    v^T Vv=D/g^2.                                  (2)

Both statements follow by multiplication; primitivity follows from the chosen greatest common divisor.
This is the complete integer kernel, not merely a rational line or a vector of possibly nonprimitive content.
If a=b=c=0 the kernel has rank three and the form is positive identity, so it has no norm-minus-one vector and is outside the following hypothesis.

Suppose the primitive vector in(2) has norm minus one.
Then D=-g^2 and abc=E+g^2>0, so a,b,c are all nonzero.
A diagonal sign congruence can make all three positive: choose signs so the new a and b are positive; their product with the new c remains abc>0.
It preserves g, the original polynomial and every absolute-companion entry.
Write the positive absolute entries as gX,gY,gZ.
The exact equation is

    X^2+Y^2+Z^2+1=gXYZ,
    X,Y,Z positive integers, gcd(X,Y,Z)=1.          (3)

Conversely a positive solution of(3) gives the stated primitive norm-minus-one kernel, provided the displayed gcd is one.
The classification below in fact supplies this gcd automatically.
A scalar endpoint condition alone does not imply(3); the norm of the primitive kernel is an additional form condition.

### 3. Complete positive-integer classification of the needed equation

**Lemma 1.** For positive integers g,X,Y,Z, equation(3) is solvable exactly when g=4.
Every positive solution for g=4 is obtained from(1,1,1) by permuting coordinates and repeatedly replacing one coordinate, for example

    Z -> 4XY-Z.                                    (4)

All these solutions are odd and primitive.

*Proof.* First reduce(3) modulo four.
If none or exactly one of X,Y,Z is odd, the left side is respectively1 or2 modulo four and the right side is divisible by four.
If exactly two are odd, the left side is3 and the right side is even.
Both cases are impossible.
Thus all three are odd, their squared sum plus one is0 modulo four, and XYZ is odd.
Consequently g is divisible by four, in particular g>=4.

For any positive solution, the other root of the monic quadratic in Z is

    Z'=gXY-Z=(X^2+Y^2+1)/Z.                         (5)

It is a positive integer and gives another solution.
If Z is the largest coordinate and Z>gXY/2, replace it by Z'; the sum of the three coordinates strictly decreases.
Reorder and repeat.
The positive integer sum cannot decrease indefinitely, so one reaches a solution with X<=Y<=Z<=gXY/2.
For

    f(t)=t^2-gXYt+(X^2+Y^2+1),

the attained Z is a root and f is decreasing up to gXY/2.
Since Y<=Z, it follows that

    0<=f(Y)=X^2+1-(gX-2)Y^2.                        (6)

If X>=2, then gX-2>=6 and Y>=X, making the right side at most1-5X^2<0.
If X=1, it is2-(g-2)Y^2.
With g>=4 this is nonnegative only when g=4 and Y=1.
The remaining equation is Z^2-4Z+3=0; the terminal condition Z<=2 selects Z=1, rather than its other root3.
Therefore the terminal solution is exactly g=4,(1,1,1).

Every descent step is reversible by the same involution and a permutation.
Starting from(1,1,1), formula(5) ensures that each coordinate replacement stays positive; it is integral by(4), preserves the equation, and is its own inverse.
Hence it generates exactly all positive solutions.
Each replacement preserves the common gcd because gcd(X,Y,4XY-Z)=gcd(X,Y,Z), and it preserves oddness.
The base is primitive and odd, proving the final statements.
This is an unrestricted height argument, not a finite-coordinate enumeration.
The standard quadratic-root descent mechanism is classical; only its precise input and use here are asserted.

**Theorem 2.** A proper integral rank-three matrix has primitive alternating-kernel norm minus one if and only if, after a diagonal sign congruence, it is V(4X,4Y,4Z) for one of the positive triples of Lemma1.
In particular its alternating content is exactly4 and its ENTIRE polynomial is always

The necessity follows from(2)--(3) and Lemma1.

Thus v^T Vv=-1, g=4 and(1) gives(7).

### 4. Exact negative-line stabilization scope

Indeed G(v,x)=G(x,v) for every x, and

    x_perp=x+G(x,v)v

is integral and satisfies G(x_perp,v)=G(v,x_perp)=0.
Also x=x_perp-G(x,v)v.

    |s-t|=4.                                       (8)
