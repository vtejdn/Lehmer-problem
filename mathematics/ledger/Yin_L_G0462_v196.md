# Multiplicative orders of roots of the primitive cubic over finite fields

<a id="orders"></a>
## The five exceptions for a full relative degree

Let \(a,b\) be coprime positive integers, \(g(T)=T^3-T-1\), \(B(T)=1-T^2-T^3\), and \(A(X)=g(X^a)+X^bB(X^a)\).
For a prime \(\ell\), let \(z\) be a root of the reduction of \(A\) in \(\overline{\mathbb F}_\ell\), and let \(N\) be its multiplicative order.
The constant coefficient is \(-1\), so \(z\ne0\) and \(\ell\nmid N\).
If \(D>1\) divides \(N\) and
\[
     [\mathbb F_\ell(z):\mathbb F_\ell(z^D)]
      =\frac{\operatorname{ord}_N(\ell)}{\operatorname{ord}_{N/D}(\ell)}=D,
                                                                  \tag{1}
\]
then \((D,N)\) belongs to the following list.
All five pairs occur.
Here and below the multiplicative order modulo one is taken to be one.

| \(D\) | \(N\) | Witness \((\ell,a,b)\) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| \(2\) | \(4\) | \((3,1,2)\) |
| \(2\) | \(8\) | \((5,1,1)\) |
| \(2\) | \(12\) | \((7,1,2)\) |
| \(3\) | \(9\) | \((2,1,4)\) |
| \(3\) | \(18\) | \((5,1,4)\) |

For the witnesses take any primitive \(N\)-th root in the indicated characteristic.
Indeed \(A_{1,1}=-\Phi_8\), \(A_{1,2}=-\Phi_2\Phi_{12}\), and \(A_{1,4}=-\Phi_2\Phi_{18}\).
In characteristic three \(\Phi_{12}=(X^2+1)^2\), and in characteristic two \(\Phi_{18}=\Phi_9\).
The orders of \(\ell\) modulo \(N\) and \(N/D\), in table order, are \((2,1),(2,1),(2,1),(6,2),(6,2)\).

<a id="residues"></a>
## How the six coefficients restrict the order

Write \(t=z^a,\ y=z^b\).
Together \(t,y\) generate the same cyclic group as \(z\).
Under (1), the elements \(1,z,\ldots,z^{D-1}\) are linearly independent over \(\mathbb F_\ell(z^D)\) by the degree of \(z\) over this field.
Grouping the six original signed monomials of \(A(z)\) according to their exponents modulo \(D\), each occupied group therefore sums to zero.
In particular every occupied residue has at least two occurrences, including when original exponents coincide as integers.

The six support points before specialization are \((i,0)\) for \(i=0,1,3\) and \((i,1)\) for \(i=0,2,3\).
All differences within a residue group have dot product divisible by \(D\) with \((a,b)\).
If they span two dimensions, two independent differences have nonzero determinant of absolute value at most six because their coordinate magnitudes are at most three and one.
The two congruences imply that \(D\) divides this determinant times each of \(a,b\), hence divides the determinant by coprimality.
If all differences are horizontal, each row of three must occupy just one residue, giving \(D\mid a\).
The row residues are then distinct by \(\gcd(a,b)=1\), and their separate vanishing would give \(g(t)=B(t)=0\), contradicting the integral identity \(-Tg+(1-T)B=1\).
If all differences are parallel and nonhorizontal, each group contains at most one point of each row and must be a pair.
Their common direction would translate the row positions \(\{0,1,3\}\) onto \(\{0,2,3\}\), which is impossible since the minima agree and the sets differ.
Thus \(D\le6\), and the same row argument always excludes \(D\mid a\).

The following table groups the possibilities for \(2\le D\le6\).
The two middle columns give exactly those residues whose occupied groups all have size at least two, after the exclusions \(\gcd(a,b,D)>1\) and \(D\mid a\).
In each remaining case \(a\) is invertible modulo \(D\), so write \(c=b/a\bmod D\).
The last column gives the group-sum equations, simplified with \(t,y\ne0\).

| \(D\) | \(a\bmod D\) | \(c\) | Equations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| \(2\) | \(1\) | \(0\) | \(t^4-t^2+1=0,\ y=t^2\) |
| \(2\) | \(1\) | \(1\) | \((t^2-1)(t^4+1)=0,\ y=-t^{-3}\) |
| \(3\) | \(1,2\) | \(1\) | \(t^6-t^3+1=0,\ y=t^4\) |
| \(3\) | \(1,2\) | \(2\) | \(t^3=1,\ y=-t^{-1}\) |
| \(4\) | \(1,3\) | \(1\) | \(t^4=-1,\ y=t\) |
| \(5\) | \(1,2,3,4\) | \(3\) | \(t^5=1,\ y=-t^3\) |
| \(6\) | none | | |

To verify the residue columns, take \(1\le a<D,\ 0\le b<D\) with \(\gcd(a,b,D)=1\) and retain the pair precisely when every entry of \((0,a,3a,b,2a+b,3a+b)\bmod D\) occurs at least twice.
For the equations, sum the corresponding entries of \((-1,-t,t^3,y,-t^2y,-t^3y)\) within each residue.
For example \(D=3,c=1\) gives \(t^3-1=t^2y\) and \(t=y(1-t^3)\).
Hence \(u=t^3\) satisfies \(u^2-u+1=0\), so \(y=(u-1)/t^2=t^4\).
For \(D=2,c=0\) the two sums give \(y(1-t^2)=1\) and \(y=1-t^{-2}\), yielding the first row.
The other four nonempty rows follow directly from their two-term sums or, for \(D=2,c=1\), by substituting \(y=-t^{-3}\) in the remaining sum.

Since \(t,y\) generate \(z\), the possible orders \(N\) in the six nonempty rows divide \(12,8,18,6,8,10\), respectively.
For instance \(u^2-u+1=0\) gives \(u^6=1\) in every characteristic.
For \(D=4\) the orders of units modulo eight are at most two, and for \(D=5\) the orders modulo ten are at most four.
The \(D=3,c=2\) row similarly has degree at most two.
For \(D=2\) and \(N\mid12\) or \(8\), (1) requires \(N=4,8,12\), and for \(D=3,\ N\mid18\) it requires \(N=9,18\).
This gives the [stated list](#orders).

<a id="degrees"></a>
## The field degree carried by the squarefree part of an order

Put \(R=\gcd(N,4)\prod_{p\mid N,\ p\ {\rm odd}}p\).
For every order \(N\) outside \(\{4,8,9,12,18\}\) occurring in the reduction of a primitive cubic,
\[
                        \operatorname{ord}_N(\ell)
                           =\operatorname{ord}_R(\ell).                \tag{2}
\]
In particular, if \(N=p^e>18\) is an odd prime power then \(p^e\mid\ell^{\operatorname{ord}_p(\ell)}-1\), and if \(N=2^e>18\) then \(2^e\mid\ell^2-1\).

To see (2), write \(d=\operatorname{ord}_R(\ell)\).
For every \(p^e\parallel N\) with \(e>v_p(R)\), put \(u_p=v_p(\ell^d-1)\).
One has \(u_p\ge1\) for odd \(p\), and \(u_2\ge2\) when applicable.
Then \(v_p(\ell^{dj}-1)=u_p+v_p(j)\) for positive \(j\).
Indeed raising an element \(1+p^uv\), with \(p\nmid v\), to the \(p\)-th power raises \(u\) by one by the binomial expansion in these ranges, and raising it to a power prime to \(p\) preserves \(u\).
Every exponent giving one modulo \(N\) is a multiple of \(d\).
Thus the least such exponent is \(d\prod_{e>v_p(R)}p^{\max(0,e-u_p)}\), the product running over \(p^e\parallel N\).
If \(e>u_p\) for some \(p\), deleting one copy of \(p\) from \(N\) leaves \(R\) unchanged and divides that least exponent by \(p\).
This gives (1) with \(D=p\), contrary to the [five exceptions](#orders).
Therefore (2) follows.

For the [modular trace test for a single rational factor](Yin_L_G0462_v190.md#criterion), let a modular trace root \(z+z^{-1}\) have degree \(h>6\).
The degree of \(z\) is \(h\) or \(2h\) by the trace quadratic, so its order falls outside all five exceptions.
Consequently \(h\le\operatorname{ord}_{R}(\ell)\le\varphi(R)\le\operatorname{rad}(N)\).
Thus any such trace witness requires a root order with squarefree radical at least its trace degree.

## A ninth-order residue in a genuine nontorsion factor

There are arbitrarily large coprime odd \(a,b\) with \(b/a\to\infty\) for which exactly one rational nontorsion irreducible factor of \(A\) has a primitive ninth root in its reduction at two.
More generally this holds whenever the two exponents are odd and coprime, \(3\nmid a\), and \(b\equiv4a\bmod9\).
At a primitive ninth root \(z\) in characteristic two, \(t=z^a\) still has order nine and \(y=z^b=t^4\).
Then \(t^6+t^3+1=0\), and the \(D=3,c=1\) group sums vanish.
All six such roots form one Frobenius orbit since \(\operatorname{ord}_9(2)=6\).
Simplicity follows by differentiating at a root in characteristic two with \(a,b\) odd, which gives
\[
        zB(t)A'(z)=t(g'B-gB')-gB=(t+1)^6\ne0 .
\]
The derivative identity uses \(yB(t)=-g(t)\) and the final polynomial equality follows by expansion.
Consequently exactly one rational irreducible factor reduces to a multiple of \(\Phi_9\bmod2\), by monic integral factorization of \(-A\).
The [cyclotomic classification](Yin_L_G0462_v172.md#cyclotomic) permits only \(\Phi_8,\Phi_{30}\) when both exponents are odd.
Their roots in characteristic two have orders one and fifteen, respectively, proving that this distinguished factor is nontorsion.
In fact the distinguished factor \(P\) is reciprocal at every stated slope.
Reversal preserves the parent up to sign, and would take \(P\) to another factor with the same self-reciprocal modular divisor \(\Phi_9\), contrary to uniqueness unless \(P\) is reciprocal.
Its trace polynomial \(H_P\), defined by \(P(X)=X^{\deg(P)/2}H_P(X+X^{-1})\), has \(Y^3+Y+1\) as a simple factor modulo two.
Indeed \(z^3\) has order three, so \(Y=z+z^{-1}\) satisfies \(Y^3+Y=z^3+z^{-3}=1\) in characteristic two.
The cubic has no root in \(\mathbb F_2\), and its square dividing the trace would give a repeated root in the original reduction.
The six roots above this trace orbit form a single orbit, with inversion acting on them as the third power of Frobenius since \(2^3\equiv-1\bmod9\).
For an escaping family take \(a\to\infty\) coprime to \(18\) and \(b=(13+18j)a+18\) with \(j\to\infty\).
The ninth-order residue identifies a factor throughout these directions, while its share of the exterior pairs and the irreducibility of the whole nontorsion part remain open.
