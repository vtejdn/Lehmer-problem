# The shortest integer numerator under power substitution

<a id="descent"></a>
## Descent through residue classes of exponents

For a continuous positive function \(u\) on the unit circle, let \(s(u)\) be the least degree of a nonzero \(A\in\mathbb Z[X]\) with \(|A|^2\le4u\) on the entire circle, taking \(s(u)=\infty\) when no such \(A\) exists.
For every positive integer \(k\), the pullback \(u^{(k)}(z)=u(z^k)\) satisfies \(s(u^{(k)})=k s(u)\), with \(k\infty=\infty\).
Indeed a certificate for \(u\) pulls back by substituting \(X^k\).
Conversely, decompose a certificate \(A\) for \(u^{(k)}\) uniquely as \(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}X^j A_j(X^k)\).
For \(w=z^k\) on the circle, orthogonality of the \(k\)-th roots of unity gives
\[
 \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}|A_j(w)|^2
   =\frac1k\sum_{\zeta^k=1}|A(\zeta z)|^2\le4u(w).
\]
Every nonzero \(A_j\) is a certificate for \(u\), and has degree at most \(\lfloor\deg A/k\rfloor\).
Thus stripping a common divisor from the input frequencies before the [integer-numerator test](../claims/Yin_C_G0345_v607.md#termination) preserves the exact yes or no answer.

## Irreducible inputs requiring arbitrarily high numerator degrees

For the [degree-twelve hyperbolic polynomial](../claims/Yin_C_G0345_v605.md#example) take \(u(z)=|P(z)|\).
Every nonzero integer constant fails where \(u<1/4\), while \(1-X\) passes, so \(s(u)=1\).
For all sufficiently large primes \(p\), the polynomial \(P(X^p)\) is irreducible.
To check this, fix a root \(\alpha\) of \(P\) and put \(K=\mathbb Q(\alpha)\).
If \(X^p-\alpha\) were reducible over \(K\), a proper monic factor of degree \(e\) would give an element \(b\in K\) equal to the product of its \(e\) roots, hence \(b^p=\alpha^e\).
Since \(1\le e<p\), Bézout's identity supplies \(c\in K\) with \(c^p=\alpha\).
Such a \(c\) is an algebraic integer of degree at most twelve, and its conjugate moduli are bounded by \(\max(1,\max_{P(\beta)=0}|\beta|)\).
Only finitely many such \(c\) exist, by bounding the coefficients of their monic minimal polynomials.
Each can serve at most one prime since \(\alpha\) is nontorsion.
For the remaining primes every \(p\)-th root of \(\alpha\) therefore has degree \(12p\) by the tower law, proving irreducibility.

These irreducible reciprocal polynomials have no circle roots, the same logarithmic Mahler measure as \(P\) by invariance of Haar measure, and shortest certificate degree exactly \(p\).
Their circle functions even have identical distributions of values under Haar measure.
Thus controlling that distribution or the measure alone cannot bound the required numerator degree before removing power substitutions.
