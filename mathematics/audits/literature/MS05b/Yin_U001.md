# Small induced subgraphs of spectral radius greater than two

The [retained McKee–Smyth manuscript](../../../literature/pdf/MS05b--arxiv-v1.pdf) uses the ordinary adjacency matrix of a finite undirected simple graph and calls its largest eigenvalue its index.
On printed pp. 2–3, Lemma 2 states interlacing under vertex deletion, and Lemma 3 classifies connected graphs with all eigenvalues in \([-2,2]\) as the connected induced subgraphs of the affine \(A,D,E\) diagrams in Figure 1.
In particular such a graph has at most two vertices of degree at least three.
Lemma 5 on p. 3 bounds the degree of every vertex by the square of the index.

Proposition 7 on pp. 4–5 assumes that the index exceeds two and the second largest eigenvalue is at most two.
Its proof on p. 5 invokes the classification of minimal induced graphs of index greater than two into eighteen graphs, each on at most ten vertices.
For the application to an arbitrary graph of index greater than two, an induced subgraph minimal with this property is connected, and vertex deletion and interlacing put its second largest eigenvalue at most two.
Thus the stated classification applies to that subgraph without a bound on the number of outliers in the original graph.
This use takes the classification as an established result as stated by McKee and Smyth; the proof of the underlying enumeration was not inspected.

The definition on pp. 1–2 distinguishes a nonbipartite Salem graph, with one eigenvalue \(t>2\) and none below \(-2\), from a bipartite Salem graph, with one positive outlier \(t>2\) and its negative.
If \(w>1\) satisfies \(w+w^{-1}=t\), the source's associated graph Salem number for a nontrivial Salem graph is \(w\) in the first case and \(w^2\) in the second.
Section 1 defines graph Pisot numbers using limit points of graph Salem numbers.
These definitions, Figure 1 and the stated graph inputs were inspected in the retained edition; the paper's classification of Salem trees, its complete limit theorem and its tables of measures were not audited.
