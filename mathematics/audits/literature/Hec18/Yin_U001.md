## Normalization, chambers and the simple-root interface

Definitions 2.1--2.2 on page 21 use a finite subset R of a Euclidean space V, with every root having squared norm 2 and every root reflection preserving R.
For a root alpha the reflection is s_alpha(x)=x-(x,alpha)alpha.
The generated group W(R) acts trivially on the orthogonal complement of the span of R and is finite on its essential span.
Definition 2.7 on page 22 calls a connected component of the complement of the root mirrors a chamber.
A chosen chamber determines positive roots by their positive scalar product with its points.
Definition 2.9 on page 23 calls a positive root simple when it cannot be written as x alpha+y beta with positive roots alpha,beta and real coefficients x,y each at least 1.

Theorem 2.12 on pages 23--24 states that the simple reflections generate W(R) and every root is a W(R)-translate of a simple root.
Proposition 2.15, Theorem 2.16 and Corollary 2.17 on page 26 give a linearly independent simple-root basis for the essential span, with off-diagonal scalar products -2 cos(pi/m_ij), where m_ij is an integer at least 2.
Theorem 2.18 on page 27 identifies full-rank normalized root systems with the same simple-root Gram matrix by an orthogonal isomorphism.
Definitions 2.19--2.21 encode these integers in a symmetric Coxeter matrix, with diagonal m_ii=1 and Gram matrix G_ij=-2 cos(pi/m_ij).
The diagram joins distinct vertices precisely when m_ij is at least 3, labels bonds of order at least 4, and is called elliptic when G is positive definite.
These are normalized real root systems, not an assumption that all scalar products are integers.

For a finite real group generated by orthogonal reflections, taking both normalized normal vectors of every reflection mirror gives this source interface on its essential span.
Finiteness gives a finite set, and conjugation by a group element transports reflection mirrors, so the required closure holds.
This is the direct applicability check; it does not assume that a specified lattice basis already consists of simple roots.

## Classification and classical models

Theorem 2.22 and its table on page 28 classify connected elliptic diagrams as
\[
A_n\ (n\ge1),\quad B_n\ (n\ge2),\quad D_n\ (n\ge4),\quad
E_6,E_7,E_8,F_4,H_3,H_4,\quad I_2(m)\ (m\ge5).
\]
The source separates orthogonal components and removes the low-rank duplicate labels B1=A1, D3=A3, I2(3)=A2 and I2(4)=B2.
The list includes noncrystallographic systems; the table alone does not classify integral lattice representations.

Examples 2.3--2.5 on pages 21--22, with the simple bases on page 29, use an orthonormal coordinate basis e_i:

| Type and essential space | Normalized roots | Reflection group | Order by counting the displayed group |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A_n, the coordinate-sum-zero subspace of R^(n+1) | plus or minus (e_i-e_j), i<j | Coordinate permutations S_(n+1) | (n+1)! |
| B_n, R^n | plus or minus sqrt(2)e_i and all signed e_i plus or minus e_j, i<j | All signed coordinate permutations | 2^n n! |
| D_n, R^n | All signed e_i plus or minus e_j, i<j | Coordinate permutations with an even number of sign changes | 2^(n-1)n! |

The order formulas are elementary counts of the source's explicit groups, rather than additional source classification assertions.
For A_n the source's Example 2.3 uses n+1 coordinates after this harmless rank relabeling.

## The normalized F4 model

Example 2.34 on page 35 gives the 48 roots obtained by all coordinate permutations and sign choices from
\[
(1,1,0,0),\qquad(\sqrt2,0,0,0),\qquad(1,1,1,1)/\sqrt2.
\]
Its ordered simple roots are
\[
(0,1,-1,0),\quad(0,0,1,-1),\quad(0,0,0,\sqrt2),\quad
(1,-1,-1,-1)/\sqrt2.
\]
Each has squared norm 2; consecutive scalar products are -1,-sqrt(2),-1, and other distinct scalar products vanish.
This records the source's normalized real representation; arithmetic lattice coordinates and a rescaling of root lengths require a separate applicability check.
No Mahler-measure bound, arbitrary-lattice coverage or project canonical-cover theorem is imported from these notes.
