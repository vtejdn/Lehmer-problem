## Definition and source conventions

In the source's homogeneous coordinates, use
\[
C=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-1\\0&1&0\\-1&0&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\Gamma=SU(C;\mathbb Z[i])
=\{G\in SL_3(\mathbb Z[i]):G^*CG=C\}.
\]
Here \(G^*\) is conjugate transpose.
The source writes this group as \(SU(2,1;\mathbb Z[i])\).
Its complex hyperbolic model is
\[
\mathcal D^2=\{(z_1,z_2)\in\mathbb C^2:\operatorname{Re}z_2>|z_1|^2/2\}.
\]

For \(\gamma\in\mathbb C\), \(r\in\mathbb R\), and
\(a=(\gamma,|\gamma|^2/2+ir)\in\partial\mathcal D^2\), the Heisenberg translation is
\[
N_a=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&0\\
\gamma&1&0\\
|\gamma|^2/2+ir&\bar\gamma&1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Its action is \((z_1,z_2)\mapsto(z_1+a_1,z_2+a_2+z_1\bar a_1)\).
The source's equations (3)--(4) give
\[
N_aN_b=N_{(a_1+b_1,\ a_2+b_2+\bar a_1b_1)},\qquad
N_a^{-1}=N_{(-a_1,\,-a_2+|a_1|^2)}.
\]
The other Section 2 matrices are
\[
A_\delta=\operatorname{diag}(\delta^{-1},1,\delta)\quad(\delta>0),
\qquad
M_\beta=\operatorname{diag}(\beta,\beta^{-2},\beta)\quad(|\beta|=1),
\qquad
J=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-1\\0&-1&0\\-1&0&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
These formulas retain the source's normalization of the Hermitian form; a different isotropic basis requires its own coordinate correspondence.

## Theorem 1 and the exact integral cusp condition

Theorem 1, page 5, states that \(\Gamma\) is generated by
\[
N_{(0,i)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\i&0&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
N_{(1+i,1)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\1+i&1&0\\1&1-i&1\end{pmatrix},
\]
\[
M_i=\operatorname{diag}(i,-1,i),\qquad J.
\]
Remark 1 says that decomposition (5) and the Euclidean algorithm provide a constructive expression in these four generators.
This does not state a uniform word-length or spectral-radius bound.

Lemma 1, page 6, identifies the stabilizer of infinity in \(SU(C;\mathbb C)\) by \(g_{13}=0\), equivalently the lower triangular subgroup \(\mathcal P\).
For a lower triangular element written in the source's form \(P=N_aA_\delta M_\beta\), membership in \(\Gamma\) is equivalent to
\[
\delta=1,\qquad
\beta\in\{1,i,-1,-i\},\qquad
r\in\mathbb Z,\qquad
\gamma\in\mathbb Z[i],\qquad
|\gamma|^2\in2\mathbb Z.
\]
In the displayed proof, \(p_{11}=\beta/\delta\) and \(p_{33}=\beta\delta\) are nonzero Gaussian integers.
Their moduli are at least one and their product of moduli is one, forcing \(\delta=1\).
The other conditions follow from the displayed entries and the Gaussian unit \(\beta\).
The converse uses the same matrix formula.
This is the integral cusp statement; it is not a statement that every factor arising from a general decomposition is integral.

The preceding informal generator list at the bottom of page 4 prints \(N_{(0,1)}\).
Use the explicit \(N_{(0,i)}\) matrix in Theorem 1 and the consistent occurrence in Proposition 1 instead.
With the Section 2 convention, \((0,1)\) is not a boundary point because its second coordinate has nonzero real part.
This local label slip does not change the explicitly stated theorem.

## General decomposition and excluded conclusions

For \(G\) outside the stabilizer of infinity, page 4 writes
\[
G=N_{G(\infty)}JP=N_{G(\infty)}JN_aA_\delta M_\beta.
\tag{5}
\]
The paragraph immediately following (5) explicitly says that the factors denoted \(N\) and \(P\) need not lie in the Picard modular group even if \(G\) does.
Their entries need not be in the coefficient integer ring.
Equation (6) only supplies \(g_{1j}=-p_{3j}\), hence integrality of that row for an integral \(G\); it does not supply integrality of all of \(P\).

The integral cusp conclusion of Lemma 1 therefore cannot be imposed on an arbitrary \(P\) obtained in (5) without separately establishing \(P\in\Gamma\).
The source gives no higher-dimensional integral decomposition with bounded word length and no small-dilation, small-spectral-radius or Mahler-measure theorem in these inspected passages.
Its page-5 remarks distinguish possible extensions from the proved \(SU(2,1;\mathbb Z[i])\) result.
No block extension, high-dimensional construction or subsequent project estimate is attributed to this source unit.
