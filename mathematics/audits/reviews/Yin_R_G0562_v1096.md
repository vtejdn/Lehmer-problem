# Independent review of dyadic trace rigidity and the gap for integral Laurent trace equations

## The norm threshold, the power comparison and the zero cases

At 2026-11-24T21:29 UTC an agent outside the proof development independently reviewed for Yin the [statement](../../claims/Yin_C_G0901_v39.md#statement) and all three proof sections of C0901 v39.
The reviewer received the fixed proof and its mathematical inputs, without development discussion, author explanations or previous findings about v39.
The strict dyadic height test, the pointwise test at an algebraic unit, and the uniform multivariate dichotomy at prime thirteen all hold as stated.

For an integral nonzero \(u\), extension of the number field raises its absolute element norm to the extension degree, so the normalized dyadic contribution \(w_2(u)\) is invariant.
It is at most the normalized log absolute norm because that absolute norm is a positive integer.
In the cyclic estimate at \(k=1,N=15\), division by the degree gives \((\log16)/15+(17/3)h(z)\) for the upper bound on \(w_2(1-z)\) at a nontorsion unit \(z\).
At \(h(z)=7\log2/170\), the two contributions are \((4/15)\log2\) and \((7/30)\log2\), with sum \((\log2)/2\).
The strict height hypothesis therefore bounds the nontorsion contribution strictly below this half.
For a torsion element of order \(b>2\), its primitive norm is \(\Phi_b(1)\).
The values of \(\Phi_b(1)\) are a prime at powers of that prime and one elsewhere, by induction in the displayed divisor product.
Only \(b=2^e,\ e\ge2\) gives positive dyadic contribution here, equal to \((\log2)/\varphi(2^e)\le(\log2)/2\).

Multiplication of the trace difference by \(x\) gives exactly \((1-xy)(1-x/y)\).
If either \(xy\) or \(x/y\) is \(1\) or \(-1\), it gives one of the four permitted values of \(y\).
Otherwise both factors are nonzero, the two units inside the differences have height at most \(h(x)+h(y)\), and at least one is nontorsion by taking their product and quotient.
Their dyadic contributions sum to strictly less than \(\log2\).
Divisibility of the product by \(2\) in the common integer ring would force its normalized dyadic norm to be at least \(\log2\), proving the first assertion.
For any two indices in the stated range, \((j+k)h(\alpha)\) meets the same strict threshold.
A congruence in the trace field extends to \(\mathbb Q(\alpha)\), and the four possible signed inverse equalities are impossible for distinct positive powers of a nontorsion unit.

In characteristic \(p\) the substitution \(U=V+V^{-1}\) is injective because the leading degree in \(U\) contributes a unique highest power of \(V\).
It gives \(T_p(U)\equiv U^p\pmod p\), and Frobenius on every integral Laurent coefficient then gives \(B_p\in p\mathbb Z[\mathbf X^{\pm1}]\).
If this Laurent polynomial is nonzero, iterated Jensen applied to a leading coefficient gives \(m(B_p)\ge\log p\), allowing monomial shifts.
The roots of \(Y^2-Q(\mathbf z)Y+1\) are reciprocal, so the logarithm of the larger modulus \(g(\mathbf z)\) is a continuous nonnegative function even at coincident roots.
Fiberwise Jensen gives the total measure \(\int g\).
The logarithm's positive part is bounded on the compact torus and its negative-part fiber integrals are bounded by the positive-part integrals.
The two summands of \(B_p\) have moduli bounded by \(2e^{p g(\mathbf z)}\) and \(2e^{g(\mathbf z^p)}\).
Since both exponents are nonnegative, the log of the sum of these upper bounds is at most \(\log4+p g(\mathbf z)+g(\mathbf z^p)\).
Haar invariance under the coordinatewise \(p\)-th power gives \(\log(p/4)\le(p+1)m\).
Thus at \(p=13\) a nonidentity comparison gives \(m\ge\log(13/4)/14\) independently of support, coefficient sizes and the positive number of variables.

At a single unit \(a\), the values of the Laurent polynomial at \(a,a^p\) are integral.
Both quadratic roots over each value are units by the monic equations with product one.
In the finite compositum of these elements, a nonzero \(B_p(a)\) has normalized log absolute norm at least \(\log p\), since \(B_p(a)/p\) is integral.
In the same compositum, the average of \(\bigl|\log|\sigma(v)|\bigr|\) over all embeddings is \(2h(v)\), by the product formula for a unit, independently of subfield degrees.
Applying the preceding pointwise upper bounds at every embedding gives \(2p\,h(v)+2h(v')\ge\log(p/4)\) in the nonzero case.
Consequently an odd prime \(p>4\) and a strict reverse inequality imply equality of the traces and \(v'\in\{v^p,v^{-p}\}\), as the two roots of the same quadratic.
The choice \(v=a^j\) uses that same height hypothesis for this inference and makes no assertion of a Laurent identity from a single evaluation.

If \(B_{13}\) vanishes identically for a constant integer \(Q\), the unordered quadratic roots give \(v^{13}=v^{\pm1}\), forcing a torsion \(v\) and \(|Q|\le2\).
Every integer in this interval has both vertical roots on the circle, which suffices for the converse zero-measure assertion.
For a nonconstant univariate Laurent polynomial, inversion if necessary gives a positive highest exponent \(k\).
The analytic root \(v(X)\sim cX^k\) near infinity is the unique root of large modulus, hence \(v(X^{13})=v(X)^{13}\).
The leading coefficients give \(c^{12}=1\) with \(c\) integral, thus \(c=\pm1\).
The first possible nonconstant term of \(v/(cX^k)\) at order \(X^{-j}\) would contribute \(13bX^{-j}\) on raising to the thirteenth power but zero at that order on substitution, a contradiction.
This gives exactly \(Q=\pm(X^k+X^{-k})\) in this case.
In several variables one integer specialization avoids all the stated finitely many proper hyperplanes, so coefficients remain unchanged, different support exponents remain distinct and a nonzero vector sum cannot specialize to zero.
The classified pair of opposite univariate exponents thus comes from two opposite original vectors with the same coefficient \(1\) or \(-1\).
These binomials have circle-valued vertical roots on the entire torus, proving that all listed cases have measure zero.
The uniform positive gap concerns this class of integral Laurent trace equations and leaves the unrestricted Lehmer problem open.
