At 2026-11-16T18:55 UTC a separately assigned Codex reviewer for Yin checked the complete statement and argument of C345v26 and the complete supporting argument of L462v112.
The reviewer took no part in developing either proof and received the fixed materials without proof-generation dialogue or author explanations.

## The first fractional index and a later coefficient bounded away from zero

In both parities the normalized anti-reciprocal lift has constant one and all its roots on the circle.
The sparse height bound therefore controls its circle supremum, so C345v13 gives eventual existence of \(d_i\) and \(d_i=o(h_i)\) even when the branch varies.
If the asserted convergence to integers failed on \([0,A d_i]\), one of the finitely many ordered support positions would have a nonintegral limiting coefficient there after extraction.
It belongs to every initial group from scale \(d_i\) through the last scale strictly below \(h_i\).
The angular small-value bound is uniform in all exponents and allows zero limiting coefficients, so Lawton applies to a fixed full lattice polynomial of measure one.
The late exponent vectors admit no nonzero nonnegative relation modulo the early span, since evaluation would equate a quantity at least of the next scale to \(O(s)\) on the current scale \(s\).
Thus the initial groups are exposed faces and their constant vertex gives equality in the face measure bound.

I checked the obstruction to a nonzero graph density \(f\) for such a face \(S\).
In an integral direction exposing the constant and separating the support, the leading coefficient on each transverse slice is a nonvanishing monomial.
Equality in Jensen excludes interior roots on every slice.
Reality of \(2f=H/S\) pairs nonzero poles across the circle, and integrability on almost every slice excludes circle poles as well.
Division with that monomial leading coefficient therefore gives a Laurent polynomial \(2f\).
For a nonconstant \(2f\), choose a linear functional injective on the exponent lattice and let \(b\) be the exponent maximizing it on the support of \(2f\).
The corresponding extreme coefficient of \(S(2f)\) is a nonzero integer, and the extreme coefficient of \(S\) has modulus at most one.
Averaging \(2f\) over the annihilator of \(\mathbb Z b\) bounds its mean below by twice the modulus of its coefficient at \(b\), hence by two.
The mass bound forces equality and exhausts the marginal.
Nonnegativity gives double vanishing on every root of \(X^b=-\epsilon\), also for imprimitive \(b\), where \(\epsilon=\pm1\) is the extreme coefficient of \(2f\).
Division by \(2+\epsilon(X^b+X^{-b})\) leaves width zero in the injective direction, and the extreme monomial identifies the quotient as one.
The resulting ordered kernel is \(1+\epsilon X^{\pm b}\) with the positive exponent chosen.
Integrality of its product with \(S\) forces integrality of \(S\) successively on each coset of \(\mathbb Z b\).
The positive constant cases \(2f=1,2\) likewise force integrality, directly or by exhaustion as Haar measure.

Testing the actual reciprocal integer denominator at base frequencies and at \(kt+O(s)\), with \(k>0\) and \(s/t,t/H\to0\), gives integer limiting coefficients after freezing.
The replacement error is uniform because the Taylor coefficients of the inverse series are bounded.
The finitely valued transform of the weighted joint measure admits a coset decomposition by interpolation.
For a graph projecting onto the whole base, high common multiples of the graph periods separate the slopes by divergent Fourier shifts, giving the integral Laurent density \(2Sf\) just excluded.
For the last possible full-torus harmonic, conditional positivity gives the factor \(1/2\) in the moment bound.
The absolutely continuous marginal density has geometric mean strictly less than one since Haar would make \(S\) integral.
Thus a nonzero integral Laurent row would have measure less than one.
Mass on the zeros of \(S\) contributes zero to every weighted row.
The remaining rows are supported on the fixed proper torsion translates of the weighted base marginal, so one product of character differences kills all intermediate limits.
Integrality of its specialized product with the actual denominator turns convergence to zero into an exact interval of zeros.

The integral inverse moments below \(d_i\) give the initial all-location bound.
At each step there are boundedly many frozen factors, hence a finite real coefficient set for the next tested multiple of the bounded-height integer denominator.
The nonzero elements have a fixed positive minimum modulus.
For \(b\) selected indices the two-sided moment approximation and removal of the negative mass cost \(b\,o(1)\) in Gram norm.
Choosing \(b\) first makes the Bessel estimate uniform over positions, and the previous all-location bound controls each transition interval by boundedly many translates.
At scale \(h_i\) the bounded number of intervals covers the whole product.
Each face specialization has constant one, has measure at least one and has measure tending to one, so the full product has measure tending to one strictly from above because the actual denominator does.
Freezing a finite real tuple and reversing to expose its unit endpoint gives the contradiction in L462v97.
The denominator at \(d_i\) diverges since a noninteger reduced fraction of denominator \(D\) has distance at least \(1/D\) from every integer.

The second finding below verifies that a later coefficient has nonzero limiting modulus after further extraction from any subsequence.
In particular a subsequence with bounded largest lower exponent divided by \(d_i\) is impossible.
If the two uniform constants failed for some fixed \(K,A\), taking both trial constants equal to \(1/i\) would give vanishing excess and make every lower coefficient beyond \(A d_i\) tend uniformly to zero.
This contradicts the extracted coefficient, including when the tail is empty.
I find the statement valid with its stated subsequence quantifiers and with either sign allowed for that coefficient.

## The rounded face, the signed fold and the truncation edge

On every given subsequence the bounded term count allows simultaneous extraction of the lower coefficient limits and exponent ratios to \(d_i\).
The terms with bounded ratio have integer limits by the first-scale result.
Every omitted exponent with nonzero limiting coefficient is separated from these terms, so face exposure applies even if some retained coefficients disappear.
Lawton gives convergence of the integer specializations \(A_i\) to measure one on the stabilized lattice.
They have bounded term count and constant one, so the integral sparse gap gives \(M(A_i)=1\) eventually.
The root product then gives unit leading coefficient in modulus and all roots on the circle.
Conjugation preserves multiplicities, giving reversal with sign \(\sigma_i=\operatorname{lc}(A_i)\), also for \(A_i=1\).

If all later limits vanished, finite support and exact reflection would give coefficient-sum error \(o(1)\) from \((1-\sigma z^p)A\), where \(k=\deg A=O(d)\) and \(p=2h+2-k>k\) eventually.
For \(0\le l\le k\) the remainder coefficient is \(I_l=b_l+\sigma b_{k-l}\).
Replacing \(b_l,b_{k-l}\) by the corresponding coefficients of \(AJ\) costs uniform \(o(1)\), since \(l,k-l<p\) and all Taylor coefficients of \(J\) are bounded.
Using \(a_{k-r}=\sigma a_r\) gives the moment \(2\int v^l A(v^{-1})\,d\omega\), with the doubled constant at \(l=r\).
For \(k<l<p\) the reflected Taylor indices are negative, giving the same moment formula.
These identities also hold for \(k=0\).
At a nearest signed grid point \(\zeta\) the angular error satisfies \(l|\arg(v/\zeta)|\le(\pi/2)|1-\sigma v^p|\).
On the support of the inverse measure the right side times \(|A(v^{-1})|\) is uniformly \(o(1)\), by the lift's exact zeros.
Sharing ties preserves conjugation and the projected variation is bounded.
The signed continuation on \(\mathbb Z/(2p)\) therefore approximates the integer function uniformly by a real function of bounded Fourier algebra norm.
The near-integer lemma and the coset theorem give uniformly many signed progressions on the first period.
Clearing each progression by its binomial gives bounded support for \(QI\), including singleton progressions and the empty sum.

Since \(B=FJ\) with \(B\) integral, the unit constant and integral coefficients of \(F\) below \(d\) give the required integral Taylor prefix even with earlier nonconstant terms.
The initial interval estimate controls \(UP_k\) through \(k=O(d)\), where \(P_k\) truncates \(B\) through that index.
For bounded \(d\), take \(U=1\).
When \(U=1-\epsilon z^u\), the added upper coefficients are translates of \(b_r\) for \(k-u<r\le k\), with \(b_r=0\) at negative indices.
Break this interval at the boundedly many numerator exponents, counting the endpoints separately.
Each open piece has a fixed bounded partial multiplier, and all its nonzero index differences have modulus less than \(u\), where the matched reciprocal model has zero moments.
The positive-part correction on any fixed set of indices tends to zero, so Bessel's inequality bounds the number of nonzero integer coefficients there, including for \(k<u\).
Reciprocity gives the quotient \(z^kP_k(1/z)\) in division by \(z^p-\sigma\).
Multiplication by the reverse of \(U\) bounds the quotient's resulting term count by that of \(UP_k\), and multiplication of the division identity by \(QU^{\rm rev}\) gives a nonzero integer polynomial of bounded support.
Its measure equals \(M(B)>1\) tending to one, contradicting the same sparse gap.
This verifies the entire second-scale assertion, including its uniform excess bound when all lower exponents are at most \(A d\).
