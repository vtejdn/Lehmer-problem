## Finding

The three atomic mass cases exhaust the stated harmonic profiles.
The period bound of 60 applies to the exceptional component sequence after the specified dilation.
For an integral Herglotz series of positive integral mass supported on a finite conjugation-symmetric torsion set, the presence of an atom at \(-1\) is exactly the obstruction to the prescribed exact negative-sign realization for all sufficiently large constants.

**Mass zero and mass one.**
Uniqueness of a periodic sequence modulo finitely supported sequences makes the two integer parts individually real and even.
Lebesgue decomposition of the positive measure gives nonnegative masses, and their individual zeroth Fourier coefficients are integers summing to two.
The finite coefficient part gives a continuous nonnegative trigonometric polynomial.
At atomic mass zero the hypotheses of the cited integer mean-two density theorem hold exactly.
At atomic mass one, the density's nonconstant Fourier coefficients have modulus strictly below one since a nonconstant circle character cannot have constant phase on an open interval of positive density.
Thus they vanish.
For the atomic probability the integer coefficients of modulus one occur precisely where the character is constant on the support, and these indices form a subgroup containing a positive common order.
Writing it as \(h\mathbb Z\) and using symmetry gives \(\widehat\nu_{\rm atom}(h)=\eta=\pm1\).
Its other Fourier values are zero off \(h\mathbb Z\) and \(\eta^{n/h}\) on that subgroup, as for the uniform measure on \(z^h=\eta\).

**Purely atomic classification.**
The orbit of \(1\) under multiplication by \(z\) is finite because the support consists of roots of unity.
Its real Gram matrix is integral, and positive definiteness on the actual real span avoids taking an abstract singular Gram presentation as a lattice.
Coordinates of the integral span lie in \(G^{-1}\mathbb Z^b\) for a nonsingular basis Gram matrix \(G\), proving discreteness.
The lattice is generated by norm-two vectors, as required by the finite root lattice argument.
Each norm-two vector belongs to one indecomposable summand by evenness, and the simple roots of each summand are connected under nonorthogonality.
Every root in that summand has nonzero product with at least one of its simple roots since they span it.
Thus the orthogonal decomposition is intrinsic and multiplication by \(z\) acts on its components.
The orbit generates the entire lattice, making this permutation transitive and hence an \(h\)-cycle.
Only indices divisible by \(h\) can then have nonzero correlation with the original vector.

In the \(A_b\) and \(D_b\) coordinate models every root has two entries of absolute value one.
Root reflections are signed coordinate permutations, and the chamber-wall argument reduces any remaining isometry to a symmetry of the diagram.
The chain reversal of \(A_b\) and the fork interchange of \(D_b\) for \(b\ge5\) have the signed extensions given in the record.
The triality of \(D_4\) therefore causes no assumption on its coordinate extension.
On different signed cycles the two diagonal contributions are the two functions in case 1.
On a common cycle the root is, up to overall sign, \(e_i+\tau S^k e_i\).
Expanding its inner product with its \(n\)-th shift gives both translates in case 2 with the stated sign even when the two congruences coincide.
Conversely such signed shifts have integral positive definite correlation in the harmonic sense, and case 3 has that property by finite Fourier inversion.
Uniform lifting through \(z\mapsto z^h\) gives the indicated zero coefficients and preserves the mass.

The remaining components have rank at most eight.
A finite-order integral isometry is diagonalizable in characteristic zero, so only distinct eigenvalue orders matter for its order, and their cyclotomic degrees sum to at most the rank.
I enumerated all distinct order sets subject to this degree condition independently.
The search through \(1\le d\le128\) is exhaustive because \(\varphi(d)^2/d\ge1/2\) by the prime-power formula, with \(2^1\) the only factor contributing a ratio below one.
With only degrees up to two the maximum least common multiple is 12.
With respective higher-degree lists \((4),(6),(8),(4,4)\) the maxima are \(60,42,30,60\), attained for example by orders \((3,4,5),(3,14),(30),(5,12)\).
This gives an actual period at most 60 for \(S=U^h\) on the exceptional component.
No such upper bound on \(h\) or on the lengths of the two signed cycles is used.

**Exact opposite-sign realization.**
Combining a conjugate pair in the Herglotz kernel gives \(m_i/(x-b_i)\) after multiplication by \(z/(1-z^2)\), with \(m_i\) the sum of its two masses.
A real atom gives the same expression with its mass taken once, including the cases \(b_i=\pm2\).
The higher Taylor coefficients of \(K\) are periodic integers, giving rationality over \(\mathbb Q\).
The fixed-field identity for reciprocal substitution follows from \(z^2-xz+1=0\) and the nontrivial involution.
Each distinct \(b_i\) is an actual simple pole since its residue is positive.
Galois invariance and algebraic integrality give the monic integral minimal denominator \(B\).
The inverse power series for \(x^{-1}=z/(1+z^2)\) is integral, by recursively solving a series with integral coefficients and linear coefficient one.
Thus \(R\) has an integral series in \(x^{-1}\), starting with \(j/x\), and the polynomial \(C=BR\) is integral of degree \(s-1\).
In particular \(C(b_i)=m_i B'(b_i)\ne0\), so none of these poles is removable.

The term \(z^sC(z+1/z)\) has powers from 1 to \(2s-1\).
Reversal in degree \(2s+1\) gives \(h_q^*-zh_q=(1-z^2)V\), preserving the claimed monicity, constant and exact normalized quotient.
The zeros of \(V\) lie on the circle and are disjoint from those of \(h_q\), even when \(b_i=\pm2\) gives a repeated root of \(V\).
At a nonreal point on or outside the circle and away from these poles, the signs of the imaginary parts in \(z-q=R(z+1/z)\) are incompatible, with the right side real at modulus one.
On either real exterior ray the derivative of \(z-q-R(z+1/z)\) is strictly positive.
Its value at \(q\) is negative and its limit at positive infinity is positive.
At \(1\) the sign or the pole calculation excludes a root.
When \(-2\) is absent, the endpoint value \(-1-q-R(-2)\) is eventually negative, excluding the negative ray and its endpoint.
The unique exterior root is consequently simple and positive, and an additional monic factor would have a nonzero integral constant of modulus less than one.
The expansion \(R(x)=j/x+O_K(x^{-2})\) at that exterior root first gives \(\theta=q+O_K(q^{-1})\) and then \(\theta-q=j/q+O_K(q^{-2})\).

When \(-2\) is present, \(R(x)\to-\infty\) for \(x\uparrow-2\) from below.
Strict monotonicity and the opposite endpoint signs give a negative root \(z_0<-1\) of the exterior equation.
Here \(1-z_0^2\ne0\) and \(V(z_0)\ne0\), so the rational expression for the normalized numerator quotient has an actual zero there.
For any alternative negative-constant polynomial with the same \(q,K\), cross multiplication forces its numerator to vanish at \(z_0\) even if its own displayed quotient had a common factor.
This excludes every alternative strict Pisot realization with the same exact function.
The harmonic classification of limits for positive constants supplies no such exact positive-constant realization.
No correction was identified.
