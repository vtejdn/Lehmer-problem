### Finite residues

At an odd prime a common zero \(t\) of \(g,B\) would have \(t^{s-2}=-1\) and \(t^2+t+1=0\), which imply \(1=t^{3(s-2)}=-1\), even in characteristic three.
For \(p\mid a\), primitivity makes \(bX^{b-1}B(X^a)\) nonzero at each root of the section.
Otherwise \(t=X^a\) is a local parameter up to translation at each nonzero root.
The derivative identity bounds the excess multiplicity there by the order of \(aT(g'B-gB')-bgB\) at \(t\), including when \(p\) divides the multiplicity.
This polynomial has constant coefficient \(b\), and when \(p\mid b\) its linear coefficient is \(-a\ne0\).
Distinct roots give distinct \(t\) since \(z^a=t\) and \(z^b=-g(t)/B(t)\) determine \(z\).
Summing gives the excess bound \(2s\).

At two the Euclidean reduction of \(\bar g\) modulo \(T^{s-2}+1\) gives \(C=T^2+T+1\), which divides \(T^{s-2}+1\) for every \(s\equiv2\bmod3\).
At either primitive cube root \(t\), the derivatives of the two rows are \(1,1\) for even \(s\) and \(t+1,t\) for odd \(s\).
Since \(C'=1\), the ratio after division by \(C\) is \(y=1\) or \(y=t\), respectively.
A common residue of \(C(X^a)\) and \(H\) over this \(t\) exists exactly when \(t^b=y^a\).
The inverse \(z=t^i y^j\) for \(ia+jb=1\) proves uniqueness even when a power map is inseparable.
Thus both primitive cube roots contribute once when \(3\mid b\) for even \(s\), or \(3\mid(b-a)\) for odd \(s\), and otherwise neither contributes.

The quotients \(G,V\) are coprime and their constant and linear coefficient pairs are \((1,0)\) and \((1,1)\).
For odd \(a\) the degree-\(\le2(s-2)\) derivative polynomial for \(H\) has nonzero constant coefficient for odd \(b\) and nonzero linear coefficient for even \(b\).
The same local summation applies to \(H\), and its product with the separable \(C(X^a)\) adds exactly \(c\) to the excess.
For even \(a\) the derivative of \(H\) is \(X^{b-1}V(X^a)\), a unit at each zero of \(H\).
The repeated residue fibers then lie over \(C(X^a)=0\), with at most \(2a+c\) positions counted with multiplicity, including all powers of two dividing \(a\).
The parent has unit endpoints, so all roots are integral units and the multiplicity of each residue equals the sum of the characteristic-zero multiplicities in its fiber.
Inertia fixes each singleton fiber, and a residue of multiplicity \(e\ge2\) accounts for at most \(e\le2(e-1)\) distinct characteristic-zero roots.
These counts give (1) for the entire parent, including wild inertia.

### Intermediate fields and trace blocks

The fixed field of the normal subgroup generated by all finite inertia in the rational splitting field is unramified at every finite prime.
Absolute discriminant one and Minkowski's bound force this field to be \(\mathbb Q\).
For \(\mathbb Q\subsetneq E\subseteq K\), transitivity on the embeddings of \(E\) therefore gives an inertia element moving at least two fibers of size \([K:E]\).
For normal \(E\) a nontrivial restriction moves every embedding of \(E\).
This proves \(2[K:E]\le S\) and, in the normal case, \(d\le S\) in every primitive direction.

The odd-characteristic contradiction also proves coprimality in characteristic zero.
Linearity in the second variable and the two coordinate unit support differences give absolute irreducibility, full support lattice and projection gcd one.
The fixed-curve theorems consequently give uniform degree growth and singleton torsion-quotient classes.
The truncated-logarithm argument for primitive directions uses \(|B|=|g|\) on the circle, so \(\limsup m(F)\le m(g)\).
The reciprocal parent would contain both a nonreciprocal factor and its distinct reversal, each costing at least \(\log\theta\) by Smyth's theorem, whereas \(M(g)\le\sqrt3<\theta^2\).
Thus eventually the inverse pairs are exactly the multiplicative labels, and the quadratic trace map identifies them with the \(d/2\) distinct trace conjugates.
The bounded-measure block theorem applies with \(m(x)\le\log6\), giving the asserted unique partition, independent alternating actions and lower bound on \(h\).

Moving a block moves at least two whole blocks, each containing \(h\) inverse pairs and hence \(2h\) conjugates.
At fixed \(s\) eventually \(4h>4s\) uniformly over factors.
The quotient acting on the blocks therefore has trivial inertia at all odd primes and, for odd \(a\), also at two since \(4(s-2)+2c\le4s-4\).
In this latter case the fixed field of the kernel has discriminant one, so transitivity forces one block and the trace image lies between \(A_{d/2}\) and \(S_{d/2}\).

### Several blocks and the necessary slope bound

If several blocks occur, \(a\) must be even, and transitivity makes the Galois block field nontrivial.
Its dyadic inertia is nontrivial at each place above two by conjugacy, and conjugates of that inertia generate its Galois group.
At each such place an inertia element lifts to the splitting field and moves at least \(4h\) conjugates, all reducing into \(C(X^a)=0\).
Thus \(4h\le2a+c\), and \(d\ll(a+1)(\log(3d))^{212}\).
The graph has unit endpoints and rank-two support, so the individual factor estimate applies uniformly for large \(r=b/a\), giving \(a\sqrt r\ll_s d\).
Using \((a+1)/a\le2\) and \(d\le sa+b\), then squaring, gives the claimed exponent \(424\).
Bounded \(r\) is covered by enlarging the constant depending on \(s\).
If \(r/(\log(3(a+b)))^{424}\to\infty\), every nontorsion factor along the sequence therefore has a single block eventually.
The argument leaves existence of several blocks in the remaining even-\(a\) directions open.
