### Real roots and the rational slope

For positive \(X>1\), \(u=X^a>1\) and \(u^r B(u)<B(u)\), so \(g(u)+B(u)=-u-u^{s-1}<0\) excludes a root.
For negative \(X<-1\) with even \(a\), the same bound applies at even \(b\).
At odd \(b\) the section exceeds \(g(u)-B(u)=2(u^s-1)+u^{s-1}-u>0\).

For odd \(a\) and even \(s\), the equation is \(u^rV=(-1)^bG\), with \(G>0\) and \(V=u^{s-1}(u-1)-1\).
The latter has a unique simple zero \(\beta_s>1\).
For even \(b\), the solution must be above this zero.
There \(G/V=1+u^{s-1}/V+u/V\) has strictly negative derivative since both \(V/u^{s-1}\) and \(V/u\) are positive with strictly positive derivative.
Its endpoint limits are infinity and one, so the crossing with \(u^r\) is unique and simple for every \(r>0\).
For odd \(b\), the solution must have \(V<0\), and for \(r\le s+2\) the expression \(G+u^rV\) is at least \((u-1)(u^s-1)(u^{s+1}-1)>0\).
For \(r>s+2\), its negative derivative at one and its positive value at \(\beta_s\) give a root strictly between them.

For odd \(a,s\), the signs of \(g(-u)\) and \(B(-u)\) exclude odd \(b\).
At even \(b\), \(u^rD-A\) is bounded below by \((u-1)(u^{s-2}-1)(u^{s-1}-1)>0\) for \(r\ge s-2\), including equality.
Below that slope it has negative derivative at one and tends to positive infinity, giving a root above one.
In both cases requiring a root count, substitution \(u=y^a\) makes the expression a polynomial, preserves the order and multiplicities of positive roots, and changes no ordering of exponents.
The descending coefficient signs have three changes, from \(+,-,-,+,+,-\), including when terms within a same-sign pair coalesce.
In each case inversion multiplies the expression by \(-u^{-s-r}\).
The root at one together with the exterior root and its distinct inverse consume the three positive roots allowed by Descartes' rule, counted with multiplicity.
This proves simplicity throughout those cases, including the small positive slopes, with no primitivity requirement.

### Winding of the quartic phase

For \(s=4\), \(g+B=-z-z^3\), and \(g\) vanishes at none of \(0,i,-i\).
Since \(B\) is the reversal of \(g\), both are nonzero on the unit circle.
Expanding the reciprocal product gives \(|g|^2=-t^4-t^3+4t^2+4t+1>0\), and \(z^{-3}(g'B-gB')=-(t^3+2)\) for \(t=z+z^{-1}\).
The phase derivative is consequently the stated quotient.
For \(t\ge-\sqrt[3]2\) it is nonpositive, and for the other \(t\) the expression \(6|g|^2+t^3+2\) factors as \((t+2)(6v^3+7v^2-10v+4)\), where \(v=-t>0\).
The quadratic \(7v^2-10v+4\) is positive by its negative discriminant, so equality with six occurs only at \(z=-1\).

The quartic has one negative interior root and a positive exterior root \(\gamma<\beta_4\), giving its nonreal pair squared modulus \(\beta_4/\gamma>1\).
Reversal leaves \(B\) with three interior roots, so \(g/B\) has winding \(-2\).
The section phase has winding \(b+2a\) and derivative at least \(b-6a\).
At \(b=6a\) it still increases strictly around each turn since its only stationary points have \(z^a=-1\).
There \(z^b=1\) and \(B(-1)=g(-1)=1\), so the phase value is \(+1\).
Every crossing of \(-1\) therefore has nonzero derivative, giving exactly \(b+2a\) simple circle roots even at the endpoint.
Reciprocity pairs the remaining \(2a\) roots inside and outside, with multiplicity.

### Strict bounds and the Salem equivalence

At \(a=3,\ b>18\) both parities give one simple negative exterior root and the total exterior count is three.
The two remaining roots form a nonreal conjugate pair and each must be simple.
At odd \(b\), the bound \(\sigma^3<\beta_4\) gives \(\sigma<9/8\), since \(V(7/5)=61/625>0\) and \((9/8)^3-7/5=61/2560>0\).
At even \(b\), \(V(2)=7,\ G(2)=17\) imply \(\sigma<2^{1/3}<\theta\), by the strict monotone crossing and \(\theta^3=\theta+1>2\).
For \(w=9/8\), the integer difference in the test \(w^{26}V(w^3)>G(w^3)\) is \(5020922521027113760098290620651249>0\) after multiplication by \(8^{38}\).
In particular \(V(w^3)>0\), and increasing \(b\) preserves this strict inequality, so \(\sigma<9/8\) also at every even \(b\ge26\).

Every nontorsion factor of this monic-up-to-sign unit polynomial contains an exterior root by Kronecker's theorem.
Thus a split isolates the negative root in one factor, and rationality places the nonreal pair together in the other.
The first factor has measure \(\sigma<\theta\) in both parities, so Smyth's theorem forces reciprocity.
An irreducible linear unit has no exterior root, and a reciprocal quadratic after changing sign would have integral trace \(\sigma+\sigma^{-1}\) strictly between two and three.
The remaining reciprocal case has degree at least four, a single exterior root, its interior inverse, and unit-circle conjugates.
Changing sign makes \(\sigma\) Salem.
Conversely the sign change of a Salem polynomial has no other exterior conjugate of \(-\sigma\), and hence leaves the nonreal exterior pair to another factor.
Exterior simplicity forces both factors to have multiplicity one.
This gives the equivalence for every primitive \(b>18\), including the even \(b<26\) that use the weaker size bound.
The equivalence leaves existence of a split open.
