### Detection at finite width

The supports of \(A(X^p)\) and \(XA_b^*(X^p)\) are disjoint even at \(p=2\), so the graph of a nonzero row is nonzero at the polynomial level.
At the least exponent of \(UV\) the only contributing exponent pair has multiplier \(p(\operatorname{ord}_0 U-\operatorname{ord}_0 V)-1\), which cannot be zero.
Taking the Euler derivative of the graph and eliminating its second row gives the asserted identity for the nonzero \(W\).
If the numerator is nonzero at a graph root then so is its denominator, and multiplication by \(XV(X^p)\) preserves the exact derivative order there.
The second summand is divisible by the full root power, and \(p\alpha^{p-1}\ne0\) transfers the resulting order back to \(W\).
In the reverse test, \(z,V(z)\ne0\) and the field equation force \(U(z)\ne0\) and \(\alpha^p=z\).
The graph vanishes at the recovered \(\alpha\), which is nontorsion because \(z\) is.
This proves both directions including cases of multiplicity higher than the prescribed threshold.

The rational expression for \(\alpha\) gives equality of fields without any preliminary degree restriction.
Primitivity and Gauss's lemma retain the full power of its powered minimum over the integers.
The finite-place terms in absolute height are required here, and the equality of degrees makes the total logarithmic measure scale by \(p\).
For an independent small check, at \(p=2,b=3\) the plus row \((-216,-2286,1315,1188)\) in ascending order has graph \(-(X+1)(2X-3)^3(3X-2)^3\).
At \(\alpha=2/3\) its numerator has value \(-70304/81\), and direct formation gives \(W=(4Y-9)^2(9Y-4)^2(198Y^2-371Y+198)\).
This checks the order decrement on a nonintegral root and a nonmonic powered minimum.

The degree bound \(2b\) and the elementary norm bound \((2pb+1)\|A\|_1^2\) now yield both the cost bound and \(\deg P\le D_p\).
The fallback has \(2\lfloor ap/2\rfloor\ge ap-1\ge(a-1)p\).
Since \(D_p\ge1\), the polynomial \(X-2\) suffices for the same pointwise lower bounds when the fallback supplies the minimum.
The finite test has an integer coefficient box, exact factorization and number-field comparisons, so the running minima of the stated positive integer radicals are computable algebraic numbers.

### Uniform construction and normalization

For \(d\le\sqrt p<p-1\) at large primes, the norm of \(\alpha\) down to \(\mathbb Q(\alpha^p)\) provides a \(p\)-th root of \(\alpha^p\) there by Bézout since the extension degree is prime to \(p\).
A different root in the larger field would put a primitive \(p\)-th root of unity in a field of degree below \(p-1\).
Thus the powered unit has total logarithmic measure \(ph\).
For each divided derivative separately, differences in one real coordinate box have modulus at most \(1/2\), and in one complex box modulus at most \(1/\sqrt2\).
Distinct algebraic integers would have nonzero integer norm of absolute value less than one.
Multiplying the coordinate counts with each complex modulus counted twice gives the stated number of tuples, including the exponent \(tLh\).
With \(H=\lfloor\exp B\rfloor+1\), the coefficient domain strictly exceeds this count and the difference has one-norm at most \(nH\le2n\exp B\).

Put \(u=dt/n\).
Then \(u=O(p^{-1/4})\), and dividing the height term of \(2B\) by \(ph\) gives \(t/(1-u)=t+O(p^{-1/8})\).
The remaining terms in \(B\) are \(O(u(1+t)\log p)\).
After division by \(ph\), these terms, \(\log(2n)\), the derivative norm correction and the later \(b_0\log2\) all tend uniformly to zero by \(h>2/(\log(3d))^3\).
The nontorsion unit inputs have \(d\ge2\).

One surviving row alone would contain the full \(Q^t\) before substitution and cost at least \(tph\) in log norm, exceeding the available bound.
In the two-row case the integral gcd contributes \(2c\) to the order of the differential polynomial.
For \(c<t\) its removed graph still vanishes, and coprimality gives two nonzero quotient values, so the derivative contributes at least \(t-c-1\) more.
For \(c\ge t\), \(2c\ge t+c-1\) already.
The integral factor \(Q^{t+c-1}\) and the norm bound give \(c\le1+o(1)\) uniformly, hence \(c\le1\).
Dividing therefore retains at least \(t-1=a\) copies and leaves each row nonzero at the designated node.
Factoring a polynomial into its linear factors gives the stated one-norm bound for each integral quotient, since the primitive divisor has multiplicative measure at least one.
Formal reversal interchanges the two rows of the graph at width \(b_0\), even after either row loses degree, and retains all copies of the reciprocal minimum.
The sum of the two candidate first rows at the node equals twice the nonzero first quotient value.
Multiplication of the successful row by \(Y+1\) or \(Y-1\) gives the required reversal sign, increases width by one and preserves multiplicity and the nonzero value at that root.
These operations cost at most a further factor four in the larger quotient norm, verifying (4) for the same root and either sign.

### Genuine bounded-degree minima and the limit

At each bounded degree there are finitely many polynomials up to the coefficient bounds at measure at most \(\log2\).
The degree-ten benchmark supplies a positive measure strictly below \(\log(6/5)\) throughout the window.
Integral content, either nonunit endpoint of an irreducible factor with nonzero constant term, or a nonreciprocal unit cannot supply the minimum there, by the endpoint bounds and Smyth's theorem.
Factoring a minimizing polynomial and using nonnegative factor measures consequently gives an actual reciprocal unit minimum of degree at most \(D\) attaining \(\lambda_D\).

For every such seed, (4) and the fixed strict gap from \(\log2\) put its signed row below the cap uniformly as \(p\) grows.
Dividing (4) and the extra \(\log(2pb+1)\) by \(p\lambda_D\) gives at most \(a+1+o(1)\) with one error bound across the whole degree window.
The denominator \(a-1\) yields the stated relative excess \((2+o(1))/(a-1)\).
Every eligible root in the unrestricted test has degree at most \(D_p\), while removing the degree restriction at \(\lfloor\sqrt p\rfloor\) only lowers the minimum.
These are the two inequalities in (3) with their respective pointwise and eventual scopes.
The bounded-degree infima decrease to the unrestricted infimum since each integer polynomial has finite degree.
The upper and lower estimates thus prove both limits, including when \(\lambda=0\).
Eventually the fallback is strictly above the winning cost, and every eligible root of every winning row has positive measure at most \(T_p\), eventually below both threshold exclusions.
No rate of convergence of the degree minima to \(\lambda\) enters this argument.
