### Fixed lines and components

Write \(a=[\mathbb Q(\alpha):\mathbb Q]\).
The norm identity in the claim gives \(a h(\alpha)=[K:\mathbb Q]h(\eta)\), since \(h(x^j)=|j|h(x)\) also for negative \(j\).
The nonzero height of \(\eta\in K\) has a positive lower bound by Northcott finiteness in the fixed degree.
When \(\alpha^j=\beta\) ranges over a finite set of nontorsion roots, this lower bound for \(a h(\alpha)\) and \(h(\alpha)=h(\beta)/|j|\) give \(a\gg |j|\).
For \(v=jv_0\) one has \(N=|j|\|v_0\|_\infty\), even on either coordinate axis.
A collision of two support exponents \(d_1,d_2\) restricts \(v\) to the rational line \((d_1-d_2)\cdot v=0\).
There are only finitely many such lines for each fixed support, including that of a selected component.
A zero specialization throughout a line is excluded by hypothesis, and a nonzero Laurent monomial on that line has no nontorsion roots.

A positive-measure integral divisor has an irreducible factor of positive measure at most its own, by nonnegativity and additivity.
Rational prime factors and primitive factors with an endpoint of absolute value at least two have measure at least \(\log 2\).
Among the remaining factors, Smyth's bound excludes nonreciprocal noncyclotomic units when the measure tends to zero. [Smyth's theorem and conventions](../literature/Smy71/Yin_U001.md#smy71-statement-and-conventions)
Factoring \(F\) in the rational Laurent ring gives finitely many possible components, each of which can be scaled to have primitive integral coefficients.
For a reciprocal minimal polynomial, \(\alpha^{-1}\) is conjugate to \(\alpha\), so \(G_v(\alpha)=0\) implies \(G_v(\alpha^{-1})=0\).
If \(G\) and its inverse polynomial are nonassociate, an absolutely irreducible common component would give a rational common factor on taking the product of its distinct Galois conjugates with a fixed monomial normalization.
Hence the two torus curves meet in a finite set, and the coordinate field of the intersection point contains \(\alpha^{\gcd(p,q)}\) by Bézout's identity.
The preceding norm bound applies in the finitely many possible fields.
A one-dimensional component is a monomial times a polynomial in a primitive character.
When the specialized character exponent \(j\) is nonzero, \(\alpha^j\) belongs to the finite root set of that polynomial.
A zero exponent gives a constant times a monomial, and that constant must be nonzero since \(F_v\ne0\).
This leaves precisely the claimed possibility of a fixed reciprocal two-dimensional component.

### Separation of the exposed face

On the compact set \(\|u\|_\infty=1\), the width of the two-dimensional support polygon is continuous and strictly positive.
After collision lines are removed, its width times \(N\), evaluated at \(v/N\), is the degree \(D\) of the monomial-normalized specialization.
At a fixed limit \(u\), the difference between the common maximal scalar product and the next largest one is positive.
The minimum projection of the exposed face therefore exceeds the maximum projection off that face by \(g\gg_{F,u}N\) along the subsequence, and the range \(b_v\) of the face projections is \(o(N)\).
Both blocks are nonempty and their coefficient norms are bounded by the coefficient norm of \(F\).
A vertex gives a constant high block.
Along an edge the high block is \(B(Z^l)\) for the orientation in which projections increase and \(l=|e\cdot v|\ge1\).
The two endpoint coefficients survive, including when the oriented polynomial is reciprocal or has repeated circle roots.

The block theorem applies with total degree \(D\) and any sufficiently small fixed \(\delta>0\) satisfying \(g-2b_v\ge\delta D\) eventually.
Roots of \(B(Z^l)\) lie in the closed disk whenever roots of the oriented \(B\) do.
Failure of either uniform bound would give a vanishing ratio \(a/N\) or a positive measure tending to zero.
Bounded \(N\) allows only finitely many specialized polynomials and positive irreducible factors, up to Laurent units.
Unbounded \(N\) provides a convergent subsequence of directions to which the block theorem or the fixed-line estimate applies.
This argument needs no uniform lower bound on the gap between the highest and second highest scalar products over all vertex directions. [Block theorem](../../claims/Yin_C_G0345_v170.md#statement) · [Independent review of the block theorem](Yin_R_G0562_v170.md#finding)

Under the torsion hypothesis, \(B(\alpha^l)\ne0\) for every nontorsion \(\alpha\), and a nonzero vertex coefficient has the same property.
At each place \(w\) the two block estimates give
\[
 \log|B_v(\alpha)|_w\le \mathbf 1_{w\mid\infty}\log L-g\log^+|\alpha|_w,
\]
where \(L\) bounds both coefficient norms and is at least two.
Indeed the equation at \(|\alpha|_w>1\) supplies the gap exponent, and at \(|\alpha|_w\le1\) integrality bounds the value by one at every finite place.
The normalized local weights sum to one over the archimedean places, so the product formula gives \(g h(\alpha)\le\log L\).
For a primitive factor with a nonunit endpoint, \(a h(\alpha)=m(A)\ge\log2\) now gives \(a\gg_{F,u}N\), also when \(\alpha\) is nonintegral.
The preceding compactness argument and fixed-line estimates give the uniform lower bound.
The sum of the factor degrees with multiplicities is at most the projection span \(O_F(N)\), which gives both the degree upper bound and the count.

### Tangential exponents and the Salem rate

Applying the same face argument to \(G\) excludes a vertex and an oriented edge polynomial with all roots in the closed disk.
The primitive tangent \(e\) extends to an integral basis \(e,e'\).
If \(e\cdot v=s\) is bounded on a subsequence, fix its signed value there.
Then \(|e'\cdot v|\to\infty\) follows from \(N\to\infty\) and invertibility of the change of coordinates.
Fixing the sign \(\sigma\) of \(e'\cdot v\) gives the specialization of the single Laurent polynomial \(H(X^s,Y^\sigma)\) at \(X=Z,\ Y=Z^{|e'\cdot v|}\).
Its specialized value is nonzero up to the monomial normalization since \(F_v\ne0\).
The fixed-specialization gap excludes a bounded subsequence of the absolute tangent exponents, while their ratios to \(N\) tend to zero because \(e\cdot u=0\).
The lattice substitution applies directly when \(p,q\) have a common factor. [Fixed-specialization gap](../../claims/Yin_C_G0345_v168.md#statement) · [Independent review of the fixed specialization](Yin_R_G0562_v168.md#all-fixed-specialization-factors)

For a Salem minimal polynomial \(A\), \(\tau\) is the house and \(t=\log\tau=m(A)\).
The normalized integral multiple \(G_v\) has \(2\le k\le|\operatorname{supp}G|\) terms and degree at most a fixed multiple of \(N\).
Thus \(e^t-1\ge0.31a/(k!\deg G_v)\) by the published divisor lemma, and \(e^t-1\le2t\) eventually since \(t\to0\).
It follows that \(gt\gg_{G,u}a\), so \(|B(\tau^l)|\le L e^{-c a}\). [Dobrowolski, Lemma 1, p. 203](https://www.impan.pl/shop/publication/transaction/download/product/83526)
Northcott finiteness gives \(a\to\infty\), since bounded degree and bounded height give only finitely many such \(\tau\).
For \(d=\deg B>0\), the distance of \(y=\tau^l\) to the nearest root is at most \((|B(y)|/|\operatorname{lc}B|)^{1/d}\), including at a multiple root.
A fixed nearest root on a subsequence is consequently real and at least one.
Dimitrov's bound gives \(y-1\ge\tau-1\ge(\log 2)/(4a)\), which exceeds the exponential error for all sufficiently large \(a\).
Thus the limiting root exceeds one. [Dimitrov, Theorem 1](https://arxiv.org/html/1912.12545v1)

There are finitely many components, oriented edges and maximizing edge normals on the maximum-norm unit sphere.
Taking the minimum of the positive exponential rates and maximum of the prefactors over these finitely many choices gives constants depending only on \(F\).
For a fixed maximizing normal the initial polynomials of the factors multiply in the Laurent domain, so their product is the initial polynomial of \(F\).
The other faces are points or segments parallel to the edge of \(G\), and the two extreme coefficients of the product in the common primitive tangent character are nonzero.
Thus the corresponding parent face is an edge and, up to a nonzero scalar and monomial, its polynomial is divisible by the oriented edge polynomial of \(G\).
Every nonzero root used above transfers in that same orientation, proving the stated Salem exclusion when the only allowed positive real edge root is \(1\).
