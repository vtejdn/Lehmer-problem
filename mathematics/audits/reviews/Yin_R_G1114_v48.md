## Fourier cutoff and sublinear location

Put \(u=\deg U_0\) and \(L=2h+2+u\).
Since \(U_0(0)=1\), agreement below \(n\) gives \(P=U_0B^\sharp\) equal to the fixed \(V\) at those indices.
Signed reciprocity gives the reflected coefficients above \(L-n\).
The remaining central interval \([n,L-n]\) is nonempty because \(n\le h+1\).
For \(u+2\le k\le 2h\), every coefficient of \(J_q\) in \([z^k]\big((1-z/2)U_0J_q\big)\) has strictly positive index.
Its value is twice the corresponding moment of \(\omega_q\), so the measure \(2(1-v^{-1}/2)U_0(v^{-1})\omega_q(dv)\) produces exactly \(p_k\) with the positive exponent convention.
Its variation is bounded because \(U_0\) is fixed and the signed probability has total variation \(1+j/[2(q-1)]\).

For \(b=\lfloor n/3\rfloor\), put \(a=n-2b\).
The cutoff has plateau \([n,L-n]\) and support in \([a,L-a]\), with both endpoints included.
Since \(a\ge n/3\to\infty\), eventually \(a\ge u+2\) and \(L-a\le2h\).
Also \(a>\deg V\) eventually.
The two transition ranges are therefore contained among known zero coefficients by the prefix and its signed reversal.
Thus the product of \(p_k\) and the cutoff is exactly the integer central coefficient sequence, including the two endpoints of the plateau.
By orthogonality the respective \(L^2\) norms of the two interval sums are \(\sqrt{L-2n+2b+1}\) and \(\sqrt{2b+1}\).
Their product divided by \(2b+1\) has bounded circle \(L^1\) norm when \(n\ge\epsilon h\), and convolution with the reflected measure preserves boundedness.
This argument also covers \(n=h+1\), when the plateau may consist of a single index.

For each central polynomial, embed the coefficients in \(\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z\) without aliasing.
With the normalized finite Fourier transform, the algebra norm of this integer-valued sequence is precisely \(p^{-1}\sum_{v^p=1}|P_{\rm cen}(v)|\).
These averages converge to the circle integral for each fixed polynomial along the primes.
One may first choose a common norm bound larger than all the circle bounds and obtain its uniform coset count from Sanders's finite theorem.
For each individual polynomial choose a prime large enough for that norm bound and larger than the support span plus one plus the coset count.
At least one index is then outside both the coefficient support and the exceptional singleton cosets, forcing the sum of all whole-group terms to be zero.
The central support is consequently bounded by the uniform coset count.
Including the fixed end supports would give boundedly many terms for \(U_0B^\sharp\) on the proposed subsequence.
The multiplicative measure of this integer polynomial is the exterior root of \(B^\sharp\), greater than one and tending to one by the delay-one return.
The fixed-support gap therefore excludes every subsequence with \(n\ge\epsilon h\), proving \(n/h\to0\).
I used the finite Fourier algebra and fixed-support theorems at their statement and application scopes.

## Classification under the density bounds

For the three rows I substituted their \(K,\rho\) into \(|H|\le |1-z/2|\,2\rho/|K|^2\).
All resulting inequalities between continuous functions hold at the endpoints as well as almost everywhere.
Since \(H(0)\) is a nonzero integer and all bounds are invariant under the overall sign, the surviving constants can be normalized to one.

For \(K=2-z\) the squared envelope is \((4-x)^2/(5-2x)\).
Subtracting four gives \((x^2-4)/(5-2x)\), strictly negative for \(-2<x<2\) and zero at both ends.
Polynomial division and the mean \(1/3\) of \(|2-z|^{-2}\) give a mean of \(7/2\), so the sum of squares of the integer coefficients of \(H\) is at most three.
With three terms in nonarithmetic progression, the constant coefficient of \(|H|^2\) is three and its six other Fourier coefficients have modulus one.
Thus the fourth moment is fifteen, contradicting \(|H|^2\le4\) and the second moment three.
For exponents \(0,l,2l\), equal endpoint signs give modulus three at a choice \(z^l=\pm1\), while opposite endpoint signs give modulus \(\sqrt5\) at \(z^l=i\).
For a binomial every alignment point must be one of \(\pm1\).
The equations \(z^l=1\) or \(z^l=-1\) consequently leave \(1+z,1-z,1+z^2\).
Subtracting their squared moduli from the squared envelope and multiplying by \(5-2x>0\) gives, in order, \(3(x-1)(x-2)\), \((3-x)(x+2)\), and \(2(x-2)^2(x+2)\).
The first is negative on \(1<x<2\), and the other two are nonnegative throughout the full interval.
The constant also obeys the bound, since \((4-x)^2-(5-2x)=(x-3)^2+2>0\).

For \(K=(2-z)/(1-z)\) the factor \(|1-z|^2\) in the modulus bound forces divisibility of \(H\) by \((1-z)^2\), including its multiplicity.
The quotient \(P_0\) is integral with nonzero constant, and \(|(2-z)P_0|\le3\) everywhere.
Its coefficient square sum is bounded by the mean of \(9/|2-z|^2\), namely three.
Equality would require \((2-z)P_0\) to have constant modulus three.
A polynomial of constant nonzero circle modulus is a monomial, since its lowest and highest nonzero terms would otherwise produce a nonzero outermost Fourier coefficient of its squared modulus.
The zero at two makes equality impossible.
For a binomial the points where \(|P_0|=2\) would all satisfy \(x\ge11/8\).
At exponent \(l\ge2\) these points are a full rotation of \(\mu_l\), whose traces average to zero.
At exponent one the minus sign fails at \(-1\), and the plus sign has \((5-2x)(2+x)=10\) at \(x=0\).
The constant quotient obeys the bound, giving only \(H=\pm(1-z)^2\).

For \(K=(2-z)(1+z)\), cancellation gives \(|H|^2\le(3-x)^2/(5-2x)\).
Its derivative is \(-2(x-3)(x-2)/(5-2x)^2\), so the squared envelope ranges from \(25/9\) at \(-2\) down to one at \(2\).
There can be at most two unit coefficients, and a binomial attains modulus two, above the upper bound \(5/3\).
This leaves the two constant signs.
I also checked the cleared differences by symbolic arithmetic and tested all allowed unit-coefficient supports through exponent eight against a rational grid in each row.
The analytic argument covers every exponent and gives necessary shapes of limits at fixed offsets from \(n\).
