### Whole-polynomial rigidity

For an integral polynomial \(V\) of measure below \(\theta\) with nonzero constant, both endpoint coefficients have absolute value one because their absolute values are bounded by \(M(V)<2\).
A change of sign makes \(V\) monic, so all its monic irreducible factors are integral with nonzero constant.
Each has multiplicative measure at least one, hence at most \(M(V)<\theta\).
The [recorded statement of Smyth's theorem](../literature/Smy71/Yin_U001.md#smy71-statement-and-conventions) applies to a monic minimal polynomial of an algebraic integer other than zero or one.
It yields ordinary reciprocity for these factors except \(X-1\), whose reversal is its negative.
Multiplying the factors with their multiplicities yields \(V^*=\pm V\).
Constants of absolute value one satisfy this directly.
This checks the use of the published theorem at its stated interface and the extension to the whole polynomial.

Because the degree and constant exponents are both occupied and \(W<N/2\), two intervals of length at most \(W\) must meet the two endpoints separately.
Their portions in the degree range lie in the two indicated end intervals.
If \(m(P)<L\), reciprocity up to sign then gives high block \(sX^{N-a}U^*\) with \(s=\pm1\).
This uses the actual degree \(a\) of the low block even if \(a<W\).
If \(a=0\) the polynomial is already a signed copy in this case.
For \(a\ge1\), apply v332 with one fast degree, \(d=a\), \(n=N-a>a\), and zero phase.
Pointwise off the finitely many circle zeros of \(U\), linear Jensen in \(Y\) gives \(\log\max(|U|,|U^*|)=\log|U|\).
Its integral is finite, so the whole independent log measure is \(m(U)\).
Removing the common integral gcd and restoring it adds the same base measure to the independent and diagonal means.
The relative estimate in v332 thus gives (3) for the whole polynomial.

When \(U^*=\pm U\), the signed binomial factor has measure one.
Otherwise the subplastic reciprocity consequence gives \(m(U)\ge L\).
Writing the lower side of (3) as \((1-C/D)m(U)-(C/D)(1+\log(2D))\) justifies substituting \(L\) when \(D_0\ge C\).
For \(m(P)\ge L\) the stated lower bound holds directly.
When \(0<W<N/2\), the inequality \(D\ge(N-W)/W\) makes the error uniformly smaller than any fixed \(\delta>0\) for sufficiently small \(W/N\).
The case \(W=0\) was already covered by the constant low block.
An irreducible polynomial of positive logarithmic measure cannot be a signed copy, since its nonconstant binomial factor has zero logarithmic measure and an irreducible factorization would force the other factor to be a unit.
This proves the lower limit, including bounded-degree sequences with \(W/N\to0\).

### The two endpoint families

The cubic \(f=X^3-X-1\) has the real root \(\theta>1\) and a conjugate pair of modulus \(\theta^{-1/2}\).
Its reversal \(g\) has the same nonzero modulus on the circle.
For \(0\le t<1\) Rouché gives \(n+2\) interior roots of \(X^n f\pm t g\) and exactly one exterior root, counting multiplicities.
At \(t=1\), any strictly exterior roots can be enclosed in disks still outside the circle, so their combined multiplicity is at most one for each sign.
For \(P_n^-\), the root at one has derivative \(7-n<0\) for every \(n\ge8\), while \(-g(\theta)=\theta^2+\theta>0\).
For \(P_n^+\), the value at \(\theta\) is negative and the leading coefficient is positive.
Both signs therefore have exactly one simple positive exterior root, respectively below and above \(\theta\), and the whole logarithmic measure is its logarithm.
Testing at any fixed \(1<x<\theta\) or \(x>\theta\) gives the brackets proving the one-sided limits.

Write \(\Delta_n=\beta_n^\pm-\theta\).
For \(1<c<\theta\) these brackets, the root equation and \(f'(\theta)=3\theta^2-1>0\) first give \(|\Delta_n|=O(c^{-n})\).
Then \(n|\log(\beta_n^\pm/\theta)|\to0\), so a second use of the equation gives \(|\Delta_n|=O(\theta^{-n})\).
Now \((\beta_n^\pm)^{-n}=\theta^{-n}(1+O(n\theta^{-n}))\).
Taylor expansion of \(f(\beta_n^\pm)=\mp g(\beta_n^\pm)(\beta_n^\pm)^{-n}\) gives the two stated signs and \(K=(\theta^2+\theta)/(3\theta^2-1)\).
Taking logarithms divides the leading coefficient by \(\theta\) and preserves the claimed remainder order.

The low block is \(\pm g\) and the high block is \(f\) shifted by \(n\), and the supports of \(g\) and \(f\) already differ.
The negative sign gives whole polynomials strictly below \(L\) with fixed width three and an \(X-1\) factor.
For the positive sign the cyclotomic conditions in C336v1 are all absent on \(n\equiv9\bmod120\), \(n\ge9\).
The odd residue excludes the even conditions, and the residues \(1\bmod8\) and \(9\bmod30\) exclude the remaining two.
The full positive-sign polynomial is then irreducible Salem, giving the matching irreducible upper limit from above.

### Consecutive coefficients of the negative-sign Salem factor

For sufficiently large \(n\), C336v2 identifies the whole cyclotomic part \(C_n\) of the negative-sign polynomial.
Its bound \(h=\deg C_n\le30\) uses eventual simplicity of the torsion roots and three auxiliary polynomial degrees \(12,12,6\).
The root one belongs to \(C_n\), since the irreducible factor \(S_n\) contains the real exterior root.
Thus \(1\le h\le30\), \(C_n(0)=\pm1\), and \(\deg S_n=n+3-h\).

For \(0\le j<n\), the coefficient of \(X^j\) in \(S_n\) agrees with that of the formal expansion \(-g/C_n\).
For \(j>3\), the nonzero constant coefficient of \(C_n\) expresses that expansion coefficient as a linear combination of its \(h\) predecessors.
Any \(h\) consecutive zero coefficients of the expansion starting at \(j\ge4\) would make all later coefficients vanish by induction.
Then \(-g/C_n\) would be a polynomial and \(g(1)\) would vanish, whereas \(g(1)=-1\).
An interval of thirty consecutive integer exponents in \([4,n-1]\) contains such a length-\(h\) interval if all its coefficients vanish.
Agreement below \(n\) rules this out for \(S_n\).
The two boundary regions have bounded width since \(n-\deg S_n=h-3\) is bounded, and both extreme exponents of \(S_n\) are occupied.
The largest distance to a support exponent on the entire degree interval is consequently \(O(1)\), giving Hausdorff distance \(O(1/\deg S_n)\) after normalization.
