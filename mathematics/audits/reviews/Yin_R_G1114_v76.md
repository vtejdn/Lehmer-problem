# Review of dyadic resultants for reciprocal units

<a id="reciprocal-dyadic-review"></a>
## The trace norm and its prime divisors

At 2026-11-16T17:02 UTC a separate reviewer for Yin inspected the whole [trace-norm argument](../../ledger/Yin_L_G0462_v83.md#trace-norm), including the example at every dyadic scale.
The reviewer had [independently inspected the underlying dyadic resultant](Yin_R_G1114_v75.md#structured-dyadic-review), took no part in developing L462v83 and received its fixed text without development dialogue, author explanations or an earlier verdict on this argument.
The norm identities, splitting conditions, congruences and example all pass.

For any base the odd part \(W\) is the product of norms of prime ideals carrying an element of exact order \(J\), each taken to its full valuation.
Each such ideal has norm congruent to one modulo \(J\), including when several ideals lie over the same rational prime.
For a reciprocal base, \(\alpha^{-1}\in K\) is a conjugate of \(\alpha\) distinct from it by nontorsion, so inversion defines an involution of \(K\).
Since \(\alpha\) is quadratic over the fixed trace element \(\beta\), the fixed field is exactly \(F=\mathbb Q(\beta)\).
Its monic minimal polynomial \(T\) has degree \(D/2\), and \(z^{D/2}T(z+z^{-1})=Q(z)\) by monicity, degree and evaluation at \(\alpha\).
For a Salem number every conjugate trace is real by its reciprocal pair or its conjugate unit-circle pair.
The factorization of \(\alpha^{J/2}+1\) as a unit times the integral trace element \(\alpha^{J/4}+\alpha^{-J/4}\) gives a square after taking absolute norms, hence \(W=w^2\) after taking odd parts.
In characteristic two the function \(z+z^{-1}=(z-1)^2/z\) has a zero of order exactly two at \(z=1\).
This doubles the multiplicity at zero in \(\bar T\), also when that multiplicity is zero.

For two trace conjugates \(t,t'\), substitution of a root \(a\) of \(z^2-tz+1\) into \(z^2-t'z+1\) gives \((t-t')a\).
The product over the two roots is \((t-t')^2\), so the discriminant product formula gives \((\operatorname{disc}T)^2\prod(t^2-4)\) with the stated sign.
The product is integral, hence every odd contributor avoiding \(\operatorname{disc}Q\) avoids \(\operatorname{disc}T\) as well.
The discriminant-index identity then makes the polynomial orders maximal locally at \(\ell\) and ensures unramifiedness.
In particular reduction of \(\alpha,\beta\) generates the appropriate full residue fields.
Every trace prime dividing the indicated trace element gives \(\bar\alpha^{J/2}=-1\) at any prime above it, since \(\bar\alpha\) is a unit.
In odd characteristic this is equivalent to order \(J\) for the power-of-two order under consideration.

The Frobenius orbit of this primitive \(\bar\alpha\) has length \(f=\operatorname{ord}_J(\ell)\).
Equality of two traces of nonzero elements identifies them up to inversion by their common quadratic polynomial, so the trace orbit has length \(g=\min\{k>0:\ell^k=\pm1\bmod J\}\).
For \(J\ge4\) reduction modulo four makes any exponent taking an odd class to \(-1\bmod J\) odd.
Such exponentiation is an automorphism of the finite abelian \(2\)-group of units modulo \(J\), with unique inverse image \(-1\) of \(-1\).
Thus the inert case occurs exactly at \(\ell=-1\bmod J\), with \(f/g=2\).
In every other case \(f=g\), so each returning trace prime splits in the unramified quadratic extension.
For split primes the dyadic valuation formula with \(f\le D/2\) gives modulus \(J/2^{\lfloor\log_2(D/2)\rfloor}=2B\), and this also divides \(J\) for the inert primes.

All trace primes at a fixed rational prime that contribute to \(w\) have the same \(g\), so the exponent of that prime in \(w\) is a multiple of \(g\).
Since \(g\) is a power of two, odd valuation requires \(g=1\), or \(\ell=\pm1\bmod J\).
Split prime powers in \(w\) are one modulo \(J\), while inert primes supply precisely the displayed parity of the sign.
Squaring \(w=\pm1+Jk\) gives \(W\equiv1\bmod 2J\) since \(J\) is even.
Also \(w>1\) gives \(w\ge J-1\), and the uniform estimate and \(Jm(Q)\gg d/\log^3(2D)\to\infty\) ensure this eventually.
This yields the stated logarithmic lower bound, short of the linear lower bound in the gap criterion.

For \(Q=z^2-3z+1\) the trace field is \(\mathbb Q\), the positive trace is three, and doubling the exponent acts by \(b\mapsto b^2-2\).
The trace at \(J/4\) is \(b_{k-2}\), with \(b_1=7\), \(b_2=47\).
If \(b_s=-1+2^{s+2}a\), expansion gives \(b_{s+1}\equiv-1\bmod 2^{s+3}\).
Every odd divisor prime yields an element of order \(2^k\) among the two roots of \(Q\) over the residue field, whose Frobenius preserves their inverse pair.
Thus each prime is \(1\) or \(-1\bmod 2^k\), and the negative product sign forces a negative-class prime of odd valuation.
This applies to the base norm for every \(k\ge3\), and to a lifted input whenever it satisfies the stipulated degree and prime conditions.
Later iterates modulo \(b_s\) equal \(-2,2,2,\ldots\), proving coprimality since the \(b_s\) are odd.

## The signed odd part and recurrence of inert primes

At 2026-11-16T17:05 UTC the same reviewer independently checked all four sections of [L462v84](../../ledger/Yin_L_G0462_v84.md#sign).
The reviewer received the fixed text after reviewing L462v83, without development dialogue, author explanations or a prior finding on this proof.
The signed congruence, its degree-uniform starting index, the local statements without good reduction, and both recurrence assertions pass for the stated hypotheses.

For any reciprocal nontorsion unit, the embeddings of \(F\) index the characteristic-zero inverse pairs.
Multiplying the \(2^{k-2}\)-power traces over those pairs thus gives the signed rational integer \(V_k\), which is nonzero since no conjugate is torsion.
At every root \(x\) the simple-root lift of its nonzero residue is the unique odd-order Teichmüller representative \(\zeta_x\).
In the local field generated by \(x\) the ramification index is at most \(D\), so the positive valuation of \(x/\zeta_x-1\), normalized by \(v(2)=1\), is at least \(1/D\).
The \(n\) successive squarings give valuation at least one in at most \(\lceil\log_2D\rceil\) steps, at least two at the next step, and then at least one additional unit of valuation at every step.
Thus \(k-2\ge1+\lceil\log_2D\rceil\) suffices simultaneously at every root, including in ramified splitting fields.

The \(h\) roots reducing to one form complete pairs, and for each pair \(v(y-1)\ge2\) gives \(v((y+y^{-1})/2-1)=2v(y-1)-1\ge3\).
Each other inverse pair gives a unit congruent modulo four to the corresponding sum of powers of the inverse Teichmüller representatives.
Nontrivial inverse residues are distinct in characteristic two, and each unordered residue pair has exactly the common multiplicity of its two members in characteristic-zero pairs over it.
On a Frobenius orbit of unordered pairs, raising to \(2^n\) preserves the first return and its orientation.
For a nontrivial odd-order lift \(z\) every difference \(z^{2^i}-z^{-2^i}\) is nonzero, so the displayed telescoping is valid.
The product is minus one precisely when an ordinary orbit of length \(2f\) folds onto an inverse-pair orbit of length \(f\).
This accounts once for each copy of a self-reciprocal irreducible factor other than \(X-1\).
The other orbits have product one, giving \(v_2(V_k)=h/2\) and \(2^{-h/2}V_k\equiv(-1)^s\bmod4\).
The approximation in each factor tends to equality \(2\)-adically at fixed \(Q\), and the product of the corresponding Teichmüller factors is exactly \((-1)^s\) for every \(n\), proving the stated convergence.
For \(X^2-4X+1\) the residual factor is \((X-1)^2\), while \(V_3=4^2-2=14\), as claimed.

At an odd trace prime dividing the trace element, the order is \(J\) at each prime above it, so the two residues in \(X^2-\beta X+1\) are distinct.
This argument works even if the discriminant of the full minimal polynomial is divisible by the rational prime.
For residue cardinality \(q_{\mathfrak r}\), Frobenius fixes both roots or interchanges them, giving \(q_{\mathfrak r}=1\) or \(-1\bmod J\).
In the first case Hensel lifts both simple roots locally.
In the second case the residue extension has degree two and already accounts for the full quadratic degree, so it is inert and unramified.
An odd residue degree is necessary to give \(\ell^g=-1\bmod J\), and the odd-power automorphism then gives \(\ell=-1\bmod J\).
Multiplication of the residue norms gives \(w_k=\pm1\bmod J\), with the stated sign from the full rational exponents at the negative class.
The signed odd part therefore also takes only those two residues, which are distinguished modulo four for \(k\ge2\).
This proves the upgrade of its signed congruence to the full modulus.
A negative sign for the absolute odd part requires a negative-class prime to an odd rational exponent.
At least one summand \(g\,v_{\mathfrak r}\) in that exponent is odd, and that prime ideal has negative Frobenius sign and is inert.
For each fixed bad rational prime the order is at most \(\ell^D-1\), so ultimately all contributors have good reduction and the preceding [residue-field argument](#reciprocal-dyadic-review) gives degrees one and two at the inert primes.

For \(k\ge3\) a real inverse pair contributes positively, and each pair off the real axis and unit circle contributes a positive product with its distinct conjugate pair.
A unique circle pair therefore gives the sign of \(\cos(2\pi 2^n x)\).
With no circle pair the sign is positive, so an odd \(s\) forces the negative class at every index covered by the congruence.
With one pair its angle \(x\) is irrational, and its nonterminating binary expansion never lands on a quarter point.
The first two digits after the shift \(n\) determine the cosine sign by equality or inequality.
Eventual constancy of either sign would produce a constant or alternating binary tail, both rational, so each sign occurs infinitely often.
The sign opposite to \((-1)^s\) makes the absolute odd part negative modulo \(J\), giving the claimed inert prime.
Their sizes are unbounded since \(\ell\ge J-1\), and squaring the odd trace norm doubles the odd rational valuation in the full norm.

A run of \(L\) identical signs fixes \(L+1\) consecutive shifted digits to a constant or alternating word.
Its binary interval lies within \(2^{-(L+1)}\) of respectively \(0,1\) or \(1/3,2/3\).
This gives the claimed \(6\pi\,2^{-(L+1)}\) distance bound after multiplying the angle by one or three.
In the [Baker–Wüstholz distance estimate](../literature/Wus02/Yin_U001.md#unit-circle), \(\xi\) has exact degree \(D\) and height \(\log M(Q)/D\), and neither of the exponents \(2^n,3\cdot2^n\) gives one.
The degree-four factor in the two-logarithm constant is reduced to \(D^2\) by the two modified heights, while the coefficient logarithm is \(O(n+1)\).
Thus \(L\ll D^2\log(2D)(1+\log M(Q))(n+1)\), using the source at its recorded statement and application scope.
Choosing \(C_Q\) larger than one plus a fixed-base run coefficient then forces the needed sign in every sufficiently large index interval \([K_0,C_QK_0]\).
This conclusion requires neither equidistribution of the binary shifts nor a new inspection of the source theorem's proof.
