An integer vanishing relation on distinct powers has a minimally rationally dependent subset.
In a rational basis its column matrix has rank \(p-1\), and the invertible square matrix of field embeddings preserves that rank over the algebraic closure.
After common translation to nonnegative exponents, one may therefore compute the unique relation with one coefficient equal to one by ratios of nonzero \((p-1)\)-row minors; all proper subsets are independent.
The [absolute minor bound](../../claims/Yin_C_G0345_v740.md#minors) controls the normalized coefficient heights by \(2(\log((p-1)!)+(p-1)Wh(\alpha))\) regardless of the given integer coefficient sizes.
Every nonzero power lying in \(\mathbb Q\) would equal its inverse by the reciprocal involution and hence be torsion, excluding \(p=2\) here.

For a complex test the explicit threshold in v740 has negative logarithm bounded by a constant depending only on the actual term count and density times \(1+Wh(\alpha)\).
Taking the maximum required \(K\) over \(2\le r\le T+1\) makes the stated exponential test sufficient.
The degree bound there comes from the subspace dimension thresholds and is uniform in \(Wh(\alpha)\), while the coefficient heights grow at most linearly in that quantity.
A monomial cannot meet a threshold below one on its normalized row, and a vanishing binomial in the extracted relation would put a nonzero power in its coefficient field of degree at most \(M\).
The hypothesis on the minimum degree of powers excludes the latter, including negative exponent differences by inversion.
Minimality of the selected vanishing subsum is with respect to its exact coefficients; those coefficients need not equal the test coefficients.

Subtracting one selected moment vector gives \(q=\min(D,p-1)\) independent directions by Vandermonde rank.
There are only finitely many selected index sets, so integral bases of their difference lattices give uniform degree bounds after Laurent translation, with no saturation assumption needed.
Direct expansion of each exponent difference yields the vector \(U_k\) after extracting exactly \(h_0\gcd(n,b)^D y\).
The common divisor \(c\) of its values on a basis divides each selected difference value \(L_j(x,y)\).
For two distinct \(j\ne j_0\) the associated binary forms have no common projective zero.
At \(Y=0\) their values are \(D(j-j_0)X^{D-1}\), and on the other chart the three different numbers \(X/Y+j\) would have the same modulus and lie on one line, which intersects such a circle in at most two points.
Bézout on both affine charts gives fixed integer multiples of powers of \(X\) and \(Y\) in the ideal of the chosen forms.
At coprime \(x,y\), at least one of \(x,y\) is a unit at every rational prime, giving a uniform bound on \(c\) by the two fixed integers.
The nonnegative-quadrant normalized divided difference has fixed nonzero sign for each \(j\ne j_0\), including when \(x=0\) or \(y=0\), and compactness gives the required lower size.
Every \(L_j\) is a fixed integral linear combination of basis values, so division by the bounded \(c\) gives \(\|v\|_\infty\gg H^{D-1}\) with \(v\) primitive.
The extracted common scalar is positive, which keeps \(\xi\) nontorsion.

Its dot product with \(U\) gives a nonzero binary form since the \(D\) monomials of degree \(D-1\) used here are linearly independent.
Finitely many such forms have finitely many projective roots, bounding the height of their nonnegative rational roots in lowest terms.

If the exact coefficients generate a subfield \(E\) of degree at most \(M\), the conjugate inverse equation has coefficients in \(\iota(E)\), so the two equations are defined over one number field of degree at most \(M^2\).

The full system has bounded polynomial degree after Laurent shifts and boundedly many coefficients.
Individual height bounds \(O_T(1+Wh(\alpha))\) also bound the ordinary coefficient-vector heights by \(O_{D,T}(1+Wh(\alpha))\) by summing the individual local maxima; shifting a Laurent support changes no coefficient.
For a hypothetical common geometric factor the other factor of the first equation is the \(\iota^{-1}\)-conjugate of its inverse, with negated support up to translation.
