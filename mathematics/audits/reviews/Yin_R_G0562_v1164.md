## Real-root signs, negative norm obstruction and the signed divisors

At 2026-11-25T01:12 UTC an agent outside the proof development independently reviewed for Yin the [statement](../../claims/Yin_C_G0901_v104.md#statement) and all three proof sections of C0901 v104, including the clarified conditions on the real-root exclusions, at SHA-256 881A768C7EAA36420221055ADF5964353B01283DBCBAF7109FB892DAFA0E246B.
The reviewer received the fixed proof and its mathematical inputs without development discussion or earlier findings on v104.
The coefficient criterion, all five residue classes, the prime-power classification and the large-\(q\) alternatives for \(u=5,6\) hold.

With \(b/a=-sp/q\), direct substitution gives \(Y(p-qT)=pT-q\) for \(T=s\beta^u,\ Y=-\epsilon s\beta^v\).
The determinant \(L=p^2-q^2\ne0\) excludes the pole and the graph recovers both signed powers from \(T\).
Bézout gives \(\mathbb Q(T)=\mathbb Q(\beta)\); in particular every real conjugate of \(T\) corresponds to an actual real conjugate of \(\beta\).
For \(q\ge5\) the cited strict measure bound and Smyth force reciprocity of the unit minimum, which also gives reciprocity of \(T\) since \(s=s^{-1}\).
The pole norm from v877 is an ordinary signed integer divisor of \(L^u\) congruent to one modulo \(pq\).
For \(q=3,4\) the conclusion concerning a reciprocal factor uses this same norm interface with reciprocity explicitly assumed.

If \(T=-r<-1\), the magnitude of the graph is \((pr+q)/(p+qr)<r<r^{v/u}\).
If \(T>p/q\), its magnitude is \((pT-q)/(qT-p)\), with negative derivative \(-L/(qT-p)^2\) and endpoint limits \(+\infty,p/q\).
There is a unique intersection with the increasing \(T^{v/u}\).
The two powers determine at most one actual root there, so its presence in a unit factor produces exactly one negative real term \(p-qT_i\) in the norm, all remaining factors pairing positively or real positive.
For odd \(u\) a real exterior root must have sign \(s\), giving \(Y\) sign \(-\epsilon s^{v+1}\).
When this sign is negative the positive graph on \(1<T<p/q\) is impossible, and the real root from the unique intersection is exactly \(sT^{1/u}\).
This proves existence and uniqueness at the parent level for the indicated sign.
The additional subgroup obstruction rules that root out of the reciprocal unit factor, whose real interior and neutral possibilities are respectively excluded by its exterior inverse and by nontorsion.
Taking a positive power at a common dilation preserves exteriority and reality and gives the appropriate primitive unit input.
The corresponding reduction to v877 at \(u=1\) has the same sign.

The prime support of \(|K|\) lies above rational primes dividing \(L\), all prime to \(M\).
For \(K<0\) the residue of \(|K|\) must be \(-1\), and conversely any expression for this residue in the generated subgroup can be made with nonnegative exponents by finite multiplicative orders.
This produces a negative signed integer satisfying the two arithmetic constraints for all sufficiently large \(u\), independently of realizability as a norm.
The example \(q=37,\ L=75\) gives \(3^9=14\cdot1406-1\) and \(3^9\mid75^u\) for \(u\ge9\).

When \(4\mid M\), write \(-M\) as a square times a squarefree negative integer.
The odd part of its fundamental discriminant divides \(M\), and its 2-part divides 4 if \(v_2(M)=2\) and divides 8 otherwise.
Thus its primitive quadratic character inflates to modulus \(M\) and is odd.
At every prime \(\ell\mid L\), the nonzero square \(-4M\equiv1\pmod\ell\) shows its value is one, since all such primes are odd and prime to \(M\).
The condition \(4\mid q(q+1)\) gives exactly \(q\equiv0,3,4,7\bmod8\).
For \(q\equiv6\bmod8\) the squarefree part \(a_1\) of \(q/2\) is \(3\bmod4\), so \(-a_1\equiv1\bmod4\) is the negative fundamental discriminant in the squareclass of \(-2q\).
Its conductor divides \(M\) and \(-2q=1-L\equiv1\bmod\ell\) makes all the required character values one.
In both constructions the value at \(-1\) excludes that residue from the subgroup.
If \(L=\ell^a\), the order of \(\ell\) divides \(2a\) by \(L^2=4M+1\), and any proper divisor would be at most \(a\), where \(1<\ell^j\le L<M-1\) for \(q\ge3\).
The unique element of order two in the cyclic subgroup is \(L\), different from \(-1\bmod M\).
The positive signed divisor therefore has \(\ell\)-exponent divisible by \(2a\), yielding exactly the stated necessary powers \(L^{2j}\).

For \(u=5,6\) the smaller signed divisor \(J\) satisfies \(|J|\le L^3\), \(J\mid L^6\).
If \(J=1+hM\), reducing \(hL^2=h(4M+1)\) gives \(hL^2\equiv h-4\bmod J\), hence \(J\mid(h-4)^3\).
For bounded \(h\ne0,4\) this is impossible along \(q\to\infty\).
If \(|h|\to\infty\), then \(|h|\ll L,\ |J|\asymp |h|L^2\), so \((h-4)^3/J\) is a bounded positive integer even for large negative \(h\).
Its residue modulo \(h\) is \(-64\), forcing it to be \(-64\) eventually, a contradiction.
The surviving cases in residue one are \(J=1,L^2\).
For \(u=6\) both signed complementary divisors have this residue, so restoring their order gives the four powers listed.

For \(u=5\) a smaller divisor of residue \(L\) is \(J=K'\).
At each prime of \(L\), the valuation of \(d=\gcd(|J|,L)\) is the smaller of the two valuations.
Consequently \(J_0=J/d\) is prime to \(h_0=L/d\) and divides \(d^4\), including negative \(J_0\), by \(J\mid L^5\).
Division of the congruence modulo \(M\) gives \(J_0=h_0+Mz\).
If \(z\ne0\), then \(|J_0|\asymp L^2|z|\), because \(h_0\le L\).
The inequalities \(d|J_0|\le L^{5/2}\) and \(|J_0|\le d^4\) imply \(d\asymp h_0\asymp\sqrt L\), bounded \(z\ne0\) and bounded integer \(e=d^4/J_0\ne0\).
Substitution of \(4M=d^2h_0^2-1\) forces \(d^2\mid e(4h_0-z)\), whose right side is nonzero of order at most \(d\) for large \(L\).
Thus \(J=L\), giving \(K=L^4\), in addition to \(K=1,L^2\) from residue one.
All these conclusions concern signed divisors allowed by the norm constraints.

As a supplemental check I factored \(2q+1\), enumerated every signed divisor of \(L^u\), and selected those congruent to one modulo \(q(q+1)\) for \(3\le q\le2500\).
At \(u=5\) there was no additional integer value, and at \(u=6\) the only additional values in this finite range were \(729,15625\) at \(q=7\).
These are arithmetic candidates and the finite calculation is separate from the proof for all sufficiently large coefficients.
