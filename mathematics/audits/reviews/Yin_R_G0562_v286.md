### The family is the actual Salem minimal polynomial

The cubic has discriminant \(-23\), no rational root and one real root \(\theta\in(1,4/3)\).
Its other roots are a complex conjugate pair of modulus \(\theta^{-1/2}<1\), since its constant coefficient gives product of roots one.
Thus it has no root on the unit circle.
For \(0<t<1\), Rouché applied to \(Z^Nf-tf^*\) gives precisely \(N+2\) interior roots and one exterior root.
Passing to \(t=1\) leaves at most one exterior root with multiplicity.
The inequalities \(F_N'(1)=7-N<0\) and \(F_N(\theta)>0\) give an exterior root in \((1,\theta)\), which is consequently simple.
Anti-reciprocity gives its inverse, and every remaining root lies on the circle.
The outer and inner roots must belong to the same irreducible monic factor: otherwise the factor containing the outer root would have constant-coefficient modulus \(\beta_N\in(1,4/3)\), which is not an integer.
All other factors are cyclotomic by the accepted Kronecker theorem.
The degree-two exception is excluded because its integral trace would lie strictly between two and three.

The three eliminants in C345v131 exhaust the cases of odd order, order divisible by four, and order congruent to two modulo four.
For the last case, \(-\zeta^2\) remains primitive and the evenness of \(N\) removes the sign from its \(N\)-th power.
The eliminant factorizations leave only orders \(1,3,5,12,30\), and the residue tests give the displayed classes.
At \(N=60h+2\), only orders one and five survive.
The quotient identity in (1), the nonzero endpoint \(1-12h\), and the two positive values \(1+12h(3\pm\sqrt5)/2\) show that each of these cyclotomic factors occurs only once.
Hence division by \(Z^5-1\) leaves exactly the irreducible Salem factor, of degree \(60h\).
The terms \(Z^{5j+5}-Z^{5j+3}-Z^{5j+2}\), together with the initial constant one, occupy distinct exponents and have coefficients of modulus one.
Finally \(F_{N+1}(\beta_N)<0\) gives strict increase, and the completion equation gives the limit \(\theta\).

### The norm formulas and local conclusions hold uniformly

For every \(m\mid60h\) except one and five, the geometric sum vanishes exactly at each primitive \(m\)-th root, so the value of \(P_h\) is exactly one.
The value at one is \(1-12h\).
At order five the two real values each occur twice, and their product before squaring is
\[
             1+36h+144h^2.
\]
These computations give all three cases of (2), including \(P_h(-1)=1\).
When \(h\) is divisible by the stated least common multiple, every tested order divides \(60h\), so for \(J\ge5\) the unweighted total is exactly the two exceptional logarithms.
Its expansion \(5\log h+O(1)\) has a constant independent of \(J\), and division by \(60h\) tends to zero.

For a prime above \(p\mid h\), reduction removes the \(p\)-primary part of the root of unity.
If the surviving order divides \(60h\), then either the reduced denominator \(\bar\zeta^5-1\) is nonzero and the geometric sum is zero, or it is zero and the sum is \(12h=0\).
This proves residue one at every such prime, including \(p=2\), \(p=3\), \(p=5\), orders one and five, and arbitrary further powers of \(p\) in the order.
An algebraic integer reducing to one has valuation zero, independently of the choice of normalization.
Arbitrarily large multiples of the prescribed divisibility conditions retain all these properties.

For the quadratic trace extension, the integral basis candidate \(1,\beta_h\) has discriminant \(t^2-4\), where \(t=\beta_h+\beta_h^{-1}\).
The field relative discriminant ideal therefore divides this principal ideal.
Taking ideal norms strengthens the displayed inequality in C345v73 to the divisibility used here:
\[
       \operatorname N_{F_h/\mathbb Q}\mathfrak d_{A_h/F_h}
                    \mid |P_h(1)P_h(-1)|=12h-1.
\]
Every rational prime dividing \(h\) is coprime to this norm, so no prime above it ramifies in the relative quadratic extension.
This statement concerns relative ramification only.

### The growing window and both degree costs are justified

The identity \(\log\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,H)=\psi(H)\) follows by counting each prime power once.
For even \(H=2n\), each term in the Legendre formula for \(v_p\binom{2n}{n}\) is zero or one.
Their number is at most the exponent of \(p\) in the least common multiple, so the binomial coefficient divides it.
Since the central binomial coefficient is the largest of the \(H+1\) terms summing to \(2^H\), this proves
\[
       \psi(H)\ge H\log2-\log(H+1).
\]
Each power \(n<p^a\le2n\) contributes one to the same valuation, giving
\(\psi(2n)-\psi(n)\le2n\log2\).
Summing dyadically proves \(\psi(H)\le2H\log2\) for powers of two.
These bounds establish exactly the claimed \(H\asymp\log d_h\), without a prime-distribution theorem.

In each finite ring \(R_i\), monicity gives \(A_i^{g_i}\) residue classes and the unit constant coefficient of \(G_i\) makes \(Z\) invertible.
Thus the order \(e_i\) exists and is at most \(A_i^{g_i}\).
The condition \(A_ie_i\mid12h\) makes the number of identical length-\(e_i\) geometric blocks a multiple of the characteristic \(A_i\), so their sum vanishes.
It is equivalent to divisibility of \(h\) by \(A_ie_i/\gcd(12,A_ie_i)\).
Taking a least common multiple and then bounding it by the product proves the stated \(H_0\) and \(H_0\le\prod_i A_i^{g_i+1}\).
Replacing a testing polynomial by a power preserves monicity and the unit constant coefficient, so the stated fixed orders of vanishing are included.
The allowable tests are countable, \(H_r\mid H_{r+1}\), and \(h_r=r!H_r\) tends to infinity while eventually satisfying each fixed test.

For a real reciprocal polynomial with \(P(\zeta_m)=1\), conjugation and reciprocity give \(1=\zeta_m^d\), hence \(m\mid d\).
For \(J\ge10\), omitting order five does not remove a factor from the least common multiple because order ten is tested.
The necessary degree is therefore divisible by \(\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,J)\), and the constructed degree is exactly sixty times that quantity.
In the finite-ring version, invariance of the Laurent ideal under inversion makes inversion a well-defined ring automorphism.
Reducing the reciprocal identity then gives \(1=Z^d\), proving the local period obstruction as well.
