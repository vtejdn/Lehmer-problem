## The largest forbidden-orbit resultant gives the full rate

For an admissible measure \(\mu\), each allowed logarithm has finite integral because it is bounded above and its integral is nonnegative.
Consequently no allowed primitive root is an atom.
When \((j,L)=1\), powering by \(j\) permutes the primitive roots at each order of \(Q\), and its nonzero derivative at these points preserves their multiplicities.
Thus \(Q(X^j)/Q(X)\) is a monic integral product of cyclotomic factors.
It has no forbidden factor because powering also permutes the roots at each forbidden order.
Integration of the logarithm of the quotient gives the required lower bound on \(\int\log|Q(z^j)|\,d\mu\), including for repeated factors of \(Q\).

For an infinite-order point \(z\), every nonzero Fourier mode on \(z^j\) has mean zero as \(j\) ranges along one residue class modulo \(L\), by the geometric series with ratio \(z^{Lk}\ne1\) for mode \(k\ne0\).
All unit classes occur with equal asymptotic frequency.
For a point of order \(b\in B\), reduction from units modulo \(L\) onto units modulo \(b\) is surjective by lifting at each prime power and applying CRT.
Each fiber has the same size, so the limit distributes each individual atom uniformly on that primitive orbit.
The allowed torsion points form a countable set of zero measure.
Integration against \(\mu\) and bounded convergence on continuous tests therefore give exactly the stated mixture, without restrictions on the initial distribution within a forbidden orbit.
For each truncation from below the continuous logarithm majorizes the original one, so the inequality for the power averages passes to an upper bound by the limiting mixture.
Taking the truncation level to \(-\infty\) gives (2), because each forbidden orbit is disjoint from the zeros of \(Q\) and the Haar integral is zero.
This also handles \(B=\varnothing\) and \(Q=1\).

To obtain the matching upper coefficient rate, take any fixed positive margin above the maximum \(A\).
For a finite list of allowed tests truncated along with the objective, strict separation of the compact moment set from the indicated orthant would yield a normal with nonnegative entries.
The objective entry is strictly positive, since the test entries at Haar measure are nonnegative.
Dividing by that entry, testing point masses and removing the lower truncations yields a nonnegative finite combination with the claimed supremum bound.
If no such combination existed, growing finite lists and increasing truncation depths would instead give a sequence of feasible moment vectors at the chosen margin.
Weak compactness, followed by passage first at each fixed truncation and then to the untruncated logarithms, would give an admissible measure with objective at least \(A+\epsilon\), contradicting (2).
The argument uses only the allowed tests and their zero Haar integrals.
Rounding the finitely many positive weights upwards costs a bounded additive amount in the log supremum and \(O(a)\) cofactor degree for each fixed margin.
Cauchy–Schwarz and Parseval cost at most \(\tfrac12\log(1+\deg(Q^aC))\) in the logarithm when passing from circle maximum to coefficient one-norm.
These losses are \(o(a)\), and every allowance with \(W(a)/a\to\infty\) eventually admits that construction.

For any nonzero integer cofactor avoiding \(B\), its resultant with every \(\Phi_b\) is a nonzero integer, of absolute value at least one.
At the \(\varphi(b)\) primitive roots, \(|Q^a C|\le\|Q^a C\|_1\), giving the lower bound \(A_b(Q)\) on the normalized log norm independently of degree or cofactor type.
The coefficient norm of every nonzero integral polynomial is at least one.
Cyclotomic cofactors attain the matching limiting upper bound, so the same rate holds for arbitrary integral cofactors under the stated coprimality.

For a fixed general integer input \(P\) coprime to the forbidden factors, repeating any one block \(P^{a_0}C_0\) and adjoining \(P^{a\bmod a_0}\) works at every sufficiently large \(a\) with cofactor degree \(O(a)\).
This proves existence of the limit and equality with the defining infimum, including the divergent normalized cofactor budgets.
Absorbing \(Q^a\) into the cofactor proves \(\gamma_B(P)\le\gamma_B(PQ)\).
Multiplying near-minimal witnesses at the same exponent proves the opposite inequality with the added cost at most \(A\).
Thus every allowed mandatory product \(Q^a\) whose individual forbidden resultants have absolute value one preserves the limiting rate for \(P\).
The formula evaluating the rate solely from these resultants applies to cyclotomic inputs.

For the substitution \(X\mapsto X^N\), a primitive \(m\)-th root maps to order \(m/\gcd(m,N)\).
Every forbidden preimage order divides \(Nb\) for some \(b\in B\), so \(B^{[N]}\) is finite, including the empty case.
Substitution of a witness preserves its coefficient norm and introduces only allowed cofactor orders relative to \(B^{[N]}\).
For the reverse bound the product \(\prod_{\omega^N=1}C(\omega X)\) has integral coefficients by Galois invariance and algebraic integrality, and rotation invariance forces exponents divisible by \(N\).
Its leading coefficient is \((-1)^{(N-1)\deg C}\).
After normalizing that sign, each root \(r\) of \(C\) supplies one factor \(Y-r^N\) in the descended polynomial, as can also be seen by multiplying its \(N\) rotated linear factors.
This proves the multiplicity assertion even when distinct roots become equal after powering.
The descended polynomial is a monic cyclotomic product avoiding \(B\).
The corresponding product of the \(N\) rotated witnesses has norm at most \(\|F\|_1^N\), since rotation preserves coefficient magnitudes.
It gives a witness for \(P^{aN}\) upon descending to \(Y\), so division by \(aN\) gives the reverse inequality with the correct normalization.
For \(B=\{1\}\) the forbidden preimage orders are exactly the divisors of \(N\).
The proof covers the full substituted polynomial without requiring it to be irreducible.
