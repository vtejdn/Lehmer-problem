### Scope of the bounded-character theorem

I inspected the published Bombieri–Masser–Zannier article, the field convention on p. 2 and Theorem 4.1 across pp. 20–21.
The theorem requires ambient dimension at least two, an irreducible variety over the algebraic numbers of dimension at most the ambient dimension minus two, and a point whose coordinates are powers of a single nonzero complex number multiplied by roots of unity.
It gives a nonzero integral character bounded in maximum norm in terms of that variety and taking value one at the point.
The field in this statement is the field of algebraic numbers, as identified by the authors at the beginning of the article.
In particular geometric components defined over a number field qualify without invariance under the absolute Galois group over the rationals.
There is no exclusion of points on a coset contained in the variety, and the bound does not depend on the parameter, the exponents or the point.
For the untwisted point \(\alpha^u\), evaluation of the character is \(\alpha^{b\cdot u}=1\), so the infinite multiplicative order gives the exact integer relation needed in the descent. [BMZ07, published article](https://doc.rero.ch/record/291088/files/rnm057.pdf#page=20)

I also checked Theorem 3.1 on p. 11 and Algorithm 4.2 and Theorem 4.3 on pp. 15–16 of the local ALS15 author edition.
The multivariate gcd there specializes to a divisor of the original gcd leaving only torsion zeros in the quotient.
This is an accurate comparison and the proof here does not assume that every irreducible constituent of an inversion-stable gcd is itself inversion-stable. [ALS15, author edition](../../literature/pdf/ALS15--author.pdf#page=11)

### Rational factors, geometric intersections and descent

For a noncyclotomic primitive irreducible rational polynomial with nonzero constant term, its roots are nonzero and none is a root of unity.
Reciprocity implies that \(\alpha^{-1}\) is a conjugate of any chosen root \(\alpha\), without requiring that the root be a unit.
At a node the specialized product is the fixed nonzero \(P\), so each specialized factor is nonzero and at least one of them vanishes at \(\alpha\).
Its rational coefficients pass this vanishing to \(\alpha^{-1}\), also for negative coordinates of \(u\).
The two factors of interest in the Laurent ring are \(G\) and \(G(Y^{-1})\), both rationally irreducible.

If a geometric hypersurface divided both, choose its irreducible equation normalized by a monomial shift and a specified leading coefficient one.
The product of its distinct Galois-conjugate equations has rational coefficients, has positive support dimension, and divides both in the Laurent ring.
Divisibility descends to the rationals, since division by this invariant nonzero product gives a Galois-invariant quotient.
Thus rationally nonassociate irreducibles have no common hypersurface over the algebraic closure.
Every component of their intersection has codimension at least two and is defined over the algebraic numbers since this field is algebraically closed and the intersection is defined there.
In dimension one a common torus point would itself be a common geometric hypersurface, so this case never needs a bounded-relation invocation.

Dividing the bounded relation by its integral content retains orthogonality to \(u\).
The character lattice quotient by that primitive vector is free and has rank one less.
Evaluation of exponents by \(m\mapsto m\cdot u\) factors integrally through it, so a basis on the quotient gives an exact specialization back to the original \(P\), irrespective of collisions or cancellations at the restriction.
Passing the whole polynomial to the quotient preserves the bound on its number of terms.
At any one node the factor list, its component lists and the sets of bounded primitive relations are finite.
The exponents of each next polynomial are determined by the node, the relation and its fixed basis, independently of the input specialization within that choice.
Together with finitely many initial coefficient lists and strictly decreasing rank this proves finiteness of all leaves.

### Intrinsic support, normalization and divisibility

An associate relation \(G(Y^{-1})=cY^wG(Y)\) gives \(c^2=1\) on applying inversion twice.
The vertex pairs of its Newton polytope span its direction space about the center, and that center cannot be a vertex in positive dimension.
This gives at least \(2q\) vertices in dimension \(q\).
For any selected vertex of \(N(G)\), an exposing functional can be chosen generic within the interior of its normal cone to expose a unique vertex of the other summand \(N(B)\) at the same time.
This applies also to polytopes in proper affine subspaces.
The sum is then a vertex of the product polytope, because the two singleton exposed faces persist in the product of leading forms.
No other selected vertex of \(N(G)\) can occur in a decomposition of this same sum, since both terms of any such decomposition must maximize the first functional on their respective summands.
Consequently \(2q\le\#\operatorname{supp}(L)\le p\).

After shifting a support point to zero, the saturated support lattice is an integral direct summand of the original lattice.
A basis extension gives a Laurent ring in independent additional variables, in which a nonunit of the \(q\)-variable subring stays a nonunit.
Thus irreducibility of the shifted ambient polynomial forces irreducibility in the chosen coordinates.
If its support is \(U\) with \(0\in U\), the identity \(-U=w'+U\) gives \(w'\in -U\) by taking the image of \(0\) from the right.
This places the new translating exponent in the saturated support lattice and preserves the reciprocal identity for \(Q\).
Signs, scalars and the removed monomial act as units under univariate Laurent specialization.
Nonzero specialization of every factor gives \(Q(X^v)\mid P\), and its vanishing at \(\alpha\) gives \(S\mid Q(X^v)\) after clearing negative powers, since \(\alpha\ne0\).
Each coefficient after specialization is a sum over a subset of the fixed finite support, giving the claimed length bound on the height.
For a fixed polynomial, noncollision is ensured when the shortest orthogonal relation exceeds the largest norm of a nonzero support difference.

### One-variable specialization and escape

A positive-measure divisor in a shrinking sequence has a positive-measure irreducible factor with no larger logarithmic measure once integer content is excluded.
The leading and constant coefficients are units in absolute value when the measure is below \(\log2\), and the [Smyth threshold](../literature/Smy71/Yin_U001.md#smy71-u001) gives reciprocity.
For a fixed one-variable Laurent polynomial, clearing monomials gives a finite set of possible nonzero values \(\beta=\alpha^v\), where \(v\ne0\) by nonzero specialization and vanishing at \(\alpha\).
The norm \(\gamma\) from \(\mathbb Q(\alpha)\) to \(\mathbb Q(\beta)\) satisfies \(\gamma^v=\beta^e\).
Both are nontorsion units and the power height identity gives \(h(\gamma)=e h(\alpha)\) also for negative \(v\).
Multiplying by the degree of \(\gamma\), at most \([\mathbb Q(\beta):\mathbb Q]\), gives \(0<m(\gamma)\le m(\alpha)\).
The degrees of the \(\gamma\)'s are bounded and coefficient bounds on monic polynomials give finitely many with measure at most one, which excludes the shrinking sequence.
In rank two, a bounded shortest orthogonal relation on an infinite subsequence fixes a primitive relation on a further subsequence.
Its integral kernel is generated by one primitive vector and the corresponding fixed univariate Laurent pullback is nonzero since the subsequent power substitutions are nonzero.
The same argument excludes this subsequence and gives divergence of the minimum as stated.

The external inputs were checked at statement and applicability depth, with the existing Smyth source inspection used at its documented scope.
The claim provides a finite reduction for individual factors and does not assert a uniform gap in rank two.
