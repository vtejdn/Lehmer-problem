## Packing, energy and the full-dimensional determinant

The inequalities (1)–(4), the three asymptotic regimes and the identity (5) follow under the stated hypotheses.

If both tails vanish, Parseval gives \(Q_a=PB\) on the circle for a polynomial \(B\) of degree less than \(q\), with \(B=0\) for \(q=0\).
Equality on the circle gives the polynomial identity, whence \(\deg Q_a<d\) forces \(a=0\).
The positive square root of \(H_q\) therefore maps \(\mathbb Z^k\) onto a full Euclidean lattice.
Reducing the first coordinate to a half-open lattice parallelepiped fixes a unique lattice translation, and its simultaneous translation of the torus preserves Haar measure.
Integrating over the entire torus gives covolume \(\sqrt{\det H_q}\).
Only finitely many lattice points have bounded first coordinate, so the shortest nonzero distance is attained.

Nearest-integer rounding in the \(q\) middle coefficients realizes the squared product distance by Parseval.
Clearing a Laurent denominator in a hypothetical divisibility \(P\mid Q_a\) would give \(P\mid X^N Q_a\) in \(\mathbb Q[X]\).
Since \(P(0)\ne0\), this would give \(P\mid Q_a\), impossible for nonzero \(a\).
The minimizing numerator \(Q_a-PB\) is therefore admissible.
The hypotheses make \(P\) the minimal polynomial of a nontorsion algebraic unit, so the [integral quotient estimate](../../claims/Yin_C_G0345_v620.md#quotient) gives \(K(m)\le\mathcal E(P)\le E_q\).
The dependency's coefficient compression and integer maximization have an [independent review](Yin_R_G0562_v757.md#finding).

Two points of the open ball of radius \(\sqrt{E_q}/2\) have distance strictly below \(\sqrt{E_q}\), so a nonidentity lattice translation cannot identify them.
Partitioning the ball among translates of the fundamental domain preserves its volume in the quotient.
For the shortest torus representative \(y\), the real fiber has radius \(\sqrt{E_q/4-\|y\|^2}\) whenever its interior is nonempty.
Faces of the cube have measure zero for \(q>0\), and the empty product for \(q=0\) has measure one.
These facts give (1) with \(E_q\), hence with \(K(m)\).

For \(u=E_q/(k+q)\) the centered box fits in the closed ball and has volume \(u^{k/2}\min(1,u)^{q/2}\).
Its intersection with the sphere has measure zero, as the real fibers have spherical boundary and \(k\ge1\).
Taking the \(2/k\) power of the volume comparison gives \(D_q\ge u\min(1,u)^{q/k}\).
The function on the right equals \(u^{(k+q)/k}\) below one and \(u\) above one, giving the second upper bound for \(E_q\) by inversion.
For \(t=K(m)/k\), the lower bound dominates \((t/2)\min(1,t/2)\) since \(q<k\) and \(K(m)/(k+q)\ge t/2\).
Splitting at \(t=1,2\) gives the constant \(1/4\) in (2).
The Hermite bound for squared length uses the Euclidean covolume to the power \(2/k\), exactly \(D_q\).
Rounding the corresponding torus coordinate costs at most \(q/4\), which proves the other upper bound for \(E_q\).
The minimizing vector supplies one admissible numerator satisfying both bounds.
For \(q=0\), \(k=1\), the torus is a point and \(E_0=D_0=\|1/P\|_2^2\), consistent with both comparisons.

When \(km\to c\in(0,\infty)\) and \(m\to0\), the quotient asymptotic gives \(K(m)/(2k-1)\to1/(4\mathrm e c)\), and \(q/k=1-1/k\to1\).
Continuity at one includes the crossover value in (4).
A common determinant upper bound strictly below the displayed limit inferior excludes the sequence.
If \(km\to0\), then \(m\to0\) and \(t\to\infty\), giving divergence by (2).
If \(km\to\infty\) and \(m\to0\), then \(t\to0\), and \((km)^2t^2/4=(mK(m))^2/4\to1/(16\mathrm e^2)\).

The [Laurent tail expansions](../../claims/Yin_C_G0345_v623.md#tails) also follow directly by multiplying each absolutely convergent simple-pole expansion by \(\sum_{j=0}^q a_jX^j\).
For the indices \(-h-1\) and \(q+h\) this gives the factors \(\rho^h\) and \(-\rho^{-q-1}(\rho^{-1})^h\), respectively, times \(Q_a(\rho)/P'(\rho)\), even with \(a_q=0\).
These are the only formulas needed from v623, and their signs, indices and degree convention also agree with the [earlier tail review](Yin_R_G0562_v760.md#finding).

For \(k=d\), the squared absolute Vandermonde determinant is \(|\operatorname{disc}P|\), and the squared derivative weights contribute \(|\operatorname{disc}P|^{-2}\).
