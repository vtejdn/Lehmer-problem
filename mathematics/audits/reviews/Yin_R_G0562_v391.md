## Independent finding on repeated residues

The local estimate and its finite-window consequence hold under the stated hypotheses.
In a powered residue class the unit ratio is \(-\alpha_i^k\), so two labels agree precisely when the original residues agree, since their quotient is a \(p\)-th root of unity and \(p\nmid k\).
For endpoints of different labels, the Gauss valuation of \(U-\lambda V\) relative to the minimum of the row valuations is positive near the endpoint with ratio \(\lambda\) and zero near the other.
At a boundary between these sets proportional reduced rows would give the same degree and the same order in the incident residue directions for \(U-\lambda V\) and a generic pencil.
Their slopes would therefore agree on both incident segments, contradicting the boundary.
The Taylor coefficient valuations lie in a rational value group and the slopes are integers, so the breakpoint can be realized after finite extension even for ramified centers.
The selected disk on that path lies inside the original disk and contains at least one endpoint.
Two uncovered marks of different labels would contradict their own path selection.

On a mixed disk there is at least one maximal selected disk.
If all are separable, the critical polynomial counts distinct powered marks as integral roots even when their scaled reductions coincide.
Adding the bounds over these disjoint disks gives \(2n_D\ge m-M+2\).
If one is inseparable, \(n_D\ge\ell\) gives the same conclusion when \(2\ell\ge m-M+2\).
For \(M=1\) this bounds \(\binom m2\) by \(2n_D^2\).
For \(M\ge2\), the estimate \((2n_D+M-2)^2/2\le4n_D^2+(M-2)^2\) and \(2W_D\ge M(M-1)\ge(M-2)^2\) give the claimed allowance \(J_D\le4n_D^2+W_D\).

Each unordered pair occurs through exactly its final common powered depth.
For equal original residues the divided power difference is a unit since its reduction is \(p\bar\alpha_i^{p-1}\).
Their common depths thus total half the valuation of the original discriminant, while the different-label depths give the power index.
The two generic pencils pay for the squared root counts over the full tree, so nonnegativity also permits every stated restriction of the disk sum.

On the included discriminant primes the local coefficient of the index is \(1/4\) with a discriminant deduction of \(1/8\).
Weakening \(1/4\) to the good-prime coefficient \(2/9\) and extending the deduction to the entire discriminant gives the stated logarithmic bound.
Hadamard's inequality applied to the squared absolute Vandermonde determinant costs \(d\log d+2(d-1)h\).
The adjacent rows have degrees at most \(16d+1\) and \(16d\), respectively, before stripping.
Using the nonnegative part of the resultant lower bound therefore permits replacing the positive degree sum by \(32d+1\), including when the displayed bracket is negative.
After moving its \(h\) term to the left the coefficient is \(2p+2+(1-4/p)(d-1)/(4(32d+1))\).
Dividing by \(p\), the cost from \(d\log d\) tends to zero because \(p\asymp(\log(3d))^4\), and the logarithmic degree error tends to zero as well.
The coefficient of \(\log I_p^\dagger/(pd)\) on the right tends to \(1/144\), giving \(\liminf h\ge\kappa/288\).
The cited Salem family has \(3\mid\operatorname{disc}P\), \(6<d+1\) and an index equal to a power of three for its prescribed primes.
The positive index-density hypothesis for a general input remains open.
