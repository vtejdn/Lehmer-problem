## Independent proof finding

The stated conclusion holds simultaneously for every factor at both poles outside the asserted exceptional set.
At a repeated prime ideal, the maximizing direction ensures that both pole congruences hold to the full exponent of the other occurrence.
Dividing the powers of the local unit \(\rho\) gives \(\rho^a=1\) modulo this prime power, where \(a=|k-k_0|\), hence \(\beta^a=(\rho^a)^k=1\) there.
For each prime ideal and fixed distance there are at most two other directions, including when several directions attain the maximum.
The absolute norm of the nonzero integer of \(K\) given by \(\beta^a-1\) has logarithm \(O(a+1)\), so summation gives \(O(M^2)\) independently of the centers.
At each retained direction the derivative estimate bounds the sum of all nondominant exponents weighted by log norms by \(O(L)\).
Keeping the dominant factor's own exponent gives a divisor of its individual pole value.
Conjugation fixes each nonsplit prime ideal, so its full exponent occurs in the gcd with the conjugate, whose norm is bounded by \(|c^{2k}-(-1)^l|^2\).
This costs \(O(k+1)\) per direction.

A split rational prime has at most two surviving branches at each pole, each with valuation \(O(pL)\), so Chebyshev's estimate gives the total cost \(O(Y^2L)\) below the size cutoff.
At a surviving branch, \(\rho=(-c)\beta^{-h}\), \(\beta=\rho^k\) and \(-c=\rho\beta^h\) modulo the prime ideal identify the generated subgroup with \(\langle\bar\rho\rangle\).
Reducing the projected product at an extension to a splitting field gives a root with \(z^k=\bar\beta\), and the reduced section at that pole gives \(z^l=-\bar c\).
Since \(l-hk=1\), this root is \(\bar\rho\) and belongs to the split residue field itself.

Taking absolute norms in an integral multiplicative relation forces the exponent of \(-c\) to vanish, after which \(\beta>1\) forces the other exponent to vanish.
At a split place the integral ideal valuation has \(v(p)=1\), the residue degree is one and the source parameter is \(D=2\).
With both logarithmic height majorants equal to \(C\log p\) for a fixed sufficiently large \(C\), one has \(b'=(l+k)/(2C\log p)\).
Thus the first entry of the theorem's maximum is \(O(L)\), its full maximum is \(O(L+\log p)\), and its prefactor including heights is \(O(g_{\mathfrak p}/(\log p)^2)\).
A collision in the stated box yields a nonzero exponent difference of size \(O(\sqrt T)\) in each coordinate.
Multiplying the difference of the monomial and one by \(c^{\max(0,-b')}\), with \(b'\) here the signed exponent of \(-c\), gives a nonzero algebraic integer divisible by the prime ideal.
Its logarithmic absolute norm is \(O(\sqrt T)\) because both conjugates of the unit and its inverse are fixed.
Summation over \(O(T)\) possible differences bounds the sum of distinct ideal log norms by \(O(T^{3/2})\).
Each prime ideal is retained only once, and its exponent for \(g_{\mathfrak p}\le T\) is \(O(TL^2)\), yielding the claimed \(O(T^{5/2}L^2)\) loss.

At \(Y=M\) and \(T=M^{4/5}\) the nonnegative losses summed over all directions, factors and both poles are \(O(M^2(\log M)^2)\).
Division by \(M(\log M)^{5/2}\) gives \(O(M/\sqrt{\log M})\) exceptional directions.
Every individual loss in a remaining direction is bounded by that direction's total.
Since \(l\asymp M(\log M)^6\) and \(\gcd(k,l)=1\), the uniform degree bound makes the ratio of this loss to any factor degree \(O((\log M)^{-1/2}(\log\log M)^{-3})\).
The uniform starting norm asymptotic therefore survives for every factor, and its positive leading coefficient ensures a prime witness at each pole.
The assignment gives disjoint ideal support at each pole and at most two assigned ideals above any rational prime there.
Finally \(\log M/\log l\to1\) uniformly in this interval, giving both claimed exponents in terms of \(l\).
