For every conjugate \(D\) of \(B>1\) one has \(D\ne1,-1\), since otherwise irreducibility of the minimal polynomial would force \(B\) to be that rational number.
Both \(Dt-1\) and \(t-D\) are nonzero in a real neighborhood of \(t=1\), also when \(D\) is nonreal.
The displayed logarithm of their modulus ratio is differentiable with bounded derivative there, takes value zero at one, and is bounded in absolute value by \(M\log t\) on a common right neighborhood by finiteness of the conjugate set.
Taking moduli in (4) therefore excludes that neighborhood for both signs and every real \(s>M\).
The denominator \(t-D\) cannot vanish at a solution because it would leave \(D^2=1\).
For large positive \(t\), the quotient is uniformly bounded while \(t^s\ge t\), giving a common upper bound for all roots in the stated range.

Any sequence of solutions at \(D=B\) and \(s\to\infty\) now has \(t\to B\) by the equation and the common lower bound \(t\ge1+\delta\).
If the positive prime root \(w=t^{1/p}\) also solves a conjugate equation, unbounded primes admit a subsequence with \(w>1\) and \(w\to1\), contradicting the exclusion.
For bounded primes one fixes the prime and the conjugate on a subsequence.
Then \(w\to B^{1/p}>1\) and \(|w-D|\to0\), forcing \(D=B^{1/p}\).
This conflicts with \(h(D)=h(B)>0\) and \(h(B^{1/p})=h(B)/p\).
This contradiction gives a threshold uniform in the real slope and the prime, for both signs and without any dependence on \(u,v\) individually.

The separation applies to \(x=\beta^{1/p}\) because \((\beta^{1/p})^u=(\beta^u)^{1/p}\), while (1) gives a zero at \(\beta\).
