For \(g=\gcd(|a|,|b|)>0\), integral Bézout coefficients recover \(z^g\) from the two nonzero coordinates \(z^a,z^b\), which are themselves powers of \(z^g\).
Thus the orbit has exactly \(D=\deg(\alpha^g)\) distinct points of equal weight, including when one exponent vanishes or either is negative.
Reciprocity of the original minimal polynomial implies invariance of this set under simultaneous inversion.
The interpolation conditions at the given orbit zeros are linear over the number-field splitting field.
Their complex solution space arises by scalar extension, so excluding coordinate zeros and the kernel of specialization leaves a rational point over that infinite field whenever it leaves the given complex vector.
This keeps the entire prescribed support and can only increase the number of orbit zeros.

Products add Newton polygons by nonvanishing of products of initial forms in every direction, giving additivity of the two widths and their sum.
A two-dimensional centrally symmetric summand has at least two independent pairs of parallel edges.
In each pair of opposite normal directions the exposed face of the sum contains a translate of the original edge, and the two resulting edges are distinct since the polygon has positive width in that normal direction.
Thus the assumption on the original polygon rules out every such irreducible factor polygon.
A one-dimensional irreducible Laurent factor has the two terms of a primitive-direction binomial after removing a monomial, by complete splitting of its univariate polynomial over the algebraic closure.
If its projected exponent is nonzero, equating that power of \(z\) to a fixed value gives mass at most \(1/e_*\), also for a negative exponent by inversion.
If the exponent vanishes the factor specializes to a nonzero scalar times a monomial since the entire specialization is nonzero.
Each such factor thus stays within its assigned positive integer width cost; a monomial contributes zero.

For an absolutely irreducible factor of dimension two, no coefficient conjugate can share a torus component with the inverse curve, since equality up to a Laurent unit would make its polygon a translate of its negative.
After separate monomial shifts the two polynomials have no coordinate factors and have total degrees at most the common width sum \(n_f\).
Their affine curves have no common component, so the plane Bézout bound gives at most \(n_f^2\) common torus points regardless of intersections on the boundary or at infinity.
Take a finite Galois extension containing the orbit and the coefficients of this factor.
If its zero set on the distinct orbit has size \(M\), exactly \(M\) orbit points lie on the inverse and each belongs to a uniform Galois translate of the original curve with probability \(M/D\) by transitivity.
Linearity of expectation gives \(M^2/D\le n_f^2\) without independence assumptions.
Summing over the irreducible factors uses the union bound and has total cost at most \(N\), since widths add even with multiplicity.
This proves (1) for arbitrary complex coefficients and every allowed specialization.

Choosing one source representative per distinct projected exponent keeps the specialization of every nonzero hyperplane functional nonzero, even if the original projection had collisions.
Every retained subset has width sum at most \(N\) and satisfies the polygon hypothesis in (2).
After translating the true minimum to zero, the monomial row entries are algebraic integers and their norms are at least one.
The integer product over all nonzero raw determinants bounds the normalized total cost by \(r d^r(\log\sqrt r+Wh)\).
Sequential sampling among the original labeled embeddings gives independent small rows with probability at least \(\delta(\delta-L)^{r-1}\) if the small-value mass is \(\delta>2L\).
The unitary column argument of [v745](../../claims/Yin_C_G0345_v745.md#volume) bounds each such determinant by \(rt\), giving precisely (2) also when the rows have repetitions elsewhere.
On a shrinking positive-measure sequence Smyth's gap gives eventual reciprocal units and norm descent gives \(e_*\to\infty\), so the stated critical-width limit follows.

For a subset of the parabola the slopes of the consecutive lower edges strictly increase, while the upper hull has only the chord of the two endpoints.
This accounts for all two-dimensional subsets, and segments and singletons have already been treated.
Their common width-sum bound is \(N_T=T+T^2\).
Integer factors can be shifted to ordinary polynomials with nonzero constant term and factored into content and primitive irreducibles.
All integer factors have nonnegative logarithmic measure, and content or either extreme coefficient of modulus at least two gives at least \(\log2\) in the indicated irreducible or content factor.
Smyth deals with nonreciprocal units.
For every remaining nontorsion reciprocal unit factor the projected parent vanishes on the whole orbit, independently of coefficient sizes and of any other factor.
Hence \(e_*\le N_T^2\); projection to a single exponent instead leaves only content as a possible positive contribution.
For a power of minimum degree, the ordinary norm unit \(\gamma\) satisfies \(\gamma^q=(\alpha^q)^{[K:\mathbb Q(\alpha^q)]}\).
It is nontorsion, belongs to a field of degree at most \(N_T^2\), and its logarithmic measure is at most the original factor measure by the resulting height identity.
The bounded-degree enumeration is effective: one can enumerate all monic integer polynomials within the coefficient bounds, remove torsion factors, and obtain positive rational lower approximations for the remaining positive measures by algebraic root isolation.
This supplies an effectively computable \(c_T>0\) for every indicated positive-measure integer Laurent factor.

An explicit constant also follows directly from [Voutier, Corollary 2](../literature/Vou96/Yin_U001.md#height).

Here \(\theta<2\), so the content and nonunit bound already exceeds this minimum.
