### Noncancellation in the confluent determinant

Discard any nodes with prescribed row count zero.
Let \(\Gamma\) be the finitely generated group of the remaining \(g_i\), and take its quotient by torsion.
Two distinct active nodes give distinct lattice points there by hypothesis.
A real linear functional separates these finitely many points, and ordering by its values gives distinct real weights \(w_i\).
In a determinant term, its value on the class of the base is the sum of \(w_i j_b\) over the column assignments, where node \(i\) occupies \(v_i\) columns.
Exchanging an out-of-order pair increases this sum by the product of the two positive differences in weights and exponents.
The unique maximizing assignment of *sets* therefore puts the smallest \(v_i\) exponents at the node of smallest weight, then the next block at the next node, in that order.
All permutations within a block contribute the same actual base.
Their signed sum is, up to the sign of the block ordering, the product of determinants of the matrices of powers \(j_b^\ell\) on the respective blocks.
Each is a nonzero ordinary Vandermonde determinant since its exponents \(j_b\) are distinct, including a possible exponent zero with \(j_b^0=1\).
This also proves noncancellation for one active node.

Every other allocation has strictly smaller value on the torsion quotient, so none has a base differing by torsion from the maximizing one.
On grouping the finitely many bases with torsion ratios along any residue class, that surviving term only acquires a nonzero fixed factor.
All remaining distinct bases after grouping have nontorsion ratios and complex modulus one.
Xiao's Lemma 4.1 therefore gives eventual nonvanishing and a lower bound \(\exp(-o(n))\) on each class.
There are finitely many classes, and the determinant is bounded above by a constant for fixed nodes and columns.
Together these prove the claimed \(\exp(o(n))\) rate as an assertion about its modulus.
The empty minor contributes one separately.

### Coordinate supports, jumps and sharpness

In the finite truncation at a fixed exterior radius, take subsequential limits for the logarithmic size of each of the two coefficient coordinates individually.
If a coordinate has finite limiting slope then it is eventually nonzero there, by integrality and extraction.
Every discarded coordinate in the finite block has a strict slope deficit, while the differentiated tail is exponentially small for each fixed jet order.
After dividing by the largest retained contribution the remaining entries therefore still satisfy the jet equations with exponentially small residual and have maximum norm one.
Reversing and removing a common initial monomial preserve the spacing of nonzero indices within each coordinate.

Write \(m=\sum_c u(c)\) at this radius.
If the two retained sets \(J_A,J_B\) both had size at most \(m\), one could choose initial derivative segments of lengths \(v_i\le u(c_i)\) totaling \(|J_A|\) and, independently, another choice totaling \(|J_B|\).
The two-center jet basis at formal \(X=0\) separates the \(A\) and \(B\) columns.
Up to permutations and nonzero row factors, including signs and the original \(c_i\), the resulting square determinant is the product of the two confluent minors just checked, at \(g_i=R/c_i\).
Their pairwise ratios are nontorsion by the original joint-root hypothesis.
This includes configurations in which one retained set is empty.
Both sets cannot be empty by the normalization.

For the determinant with the true formal parameter, fix a modulus killing the torsion among its finitely many phase bases.
In each residue class one can take a positive representative sufficiently far out that both specialized minors at \(X=0\) are nonzero.
Their nonzero product ensures that not all grouped polynomial coefficients vanish identically on the progression.
The local recurrence bound applies with polynomial coefficients at \(X=n\), and all its cofactors grow at most polynomially.
Its inverse of size \(\exp(o(n))\) would turn the exponentially small residual into a vector tending to zero, contradicting the retained normalization.
Consequently one coordinate itself has at least \(m+1\) tight indices.

Those indices span at least \(rm\) positions when consecutive nonzero coefficients have gaps at least \(r\).
The upper-envelope identity counts the extreme maximizing indices by their distance at each positive breakpoint, with weight \(\log R\).
The required jumps alone give \(r\sum_R m_R\log R=rS\), also for unbounded formal widths.
Any violation of the lower limit would give a bounded-slope subsequence to which the argument applies.
At slope \(S\) the required jumps for \(r=1\) exhaust the budget, so the tight indices of one coordinate fill the entire interval of \(m_R+1\) integers at each exterior radius.
The coordinate is chosen separately at each radius.
Since \(S>0\), infinitely many rows having no adjacent terms in either coordinate would already contradict the lower bound with \(r=2\).

For attainment, all exponents \(rn\) separate the original roots.
Choose the genuine signed rows at exponent \(rn\), width \(b(n)\), and limiting slope \(S\) relative to \(rn\) from the even-order theorem.
A width specified only on multiples of \(r\) extends to a diverging width sequence on all exponents, if needed.
For the substituted row \(C(Y)=A(Y^r)\), one has \(C^*_{rb}(Y)=A_b^*(Y^r)\), so at exponent \(n\) the graph and all genuine powered values are exactly the originals.
The one-norm is unchanged, the supports are \(r\)-separated and the slope relative to \(n\) is \(rS\).

### Finite arithmetic of the reduced dilation

Substitution \(Z=Y^r\) is injective on rational functions, so \(h(Z)h(1/Z)=1\).
The finite nonzero value \(h(0)\) implies the same property at infinity, whence the reduced numerator and denominator of \(h\) have equal degree \(d_h\) and nonzero constant coefficients.
Their compositions with \(Y^r\) stay coprime, and multiplying by \(Y^s\) adds \(|s|\) to the larger degree without cancellation.
This gives \(\deg f=|s|+rd_h\).
For the genuine node \(\beta=c^n\), \(c=f(\beta)\) yields \(\mathbb Q(c)=\mathbb Q(\beta)\).
From \(c=\beta^s h(z)\) one obtains \(\beta^{ns-1}=h(z)^{-n}\), where \(z=\beta^r\).
For \(g=\gcd(r,ns-1)>0\), Bézout gives \(\beta^g\in\mathbb Q(z)\), also when \(s\le0\).
It follows that \(e=[\mathbb Q(\beta):\mathbb Q(z)]\le g\) by the polynomial \(T^g-\beta^g\) over the latter field.

The cleared polynomial in \(Z\) is
\[
 (ns-1)L_h+nr\sum_F k_F ZF'(L_h/F).
\]
The sum of the signed multiplicities weighted by \(\deg F\) is zero, so the leading coefficient is \((ns-1)\operatorname{lc}(L_h)\ne0\).
The reciprocal pairing gives \(\deg L_h=2\deg R_h\) and \(m(L_h)=2m(R_h)\).
At \(\beta\), \(nY f'/f-1\) has exact order \(t-1\) by the local graph identity, and \(Y\mapsto Y^r\) is unramified at this nonzero point.
Neither \(h\) nor \(L_h\) has a zero or pole at \(z\), since the graph value is finite and nonzero there after the monomial is removed.
The primitive minimum of \(z\) to order \(t-1\) thus divides the cleared integer polynomial by Gauss's lemma.
This minimum has degree \(D_c/e\), and its log measure is \(nr\,m(c)/e\) by the absolute-height power identity.
Taking degree and log measure of the divisor gives the asserted bounds, using the integral cofactor and Jensen's inequality.
Indeed the mean of \(|(ns-1)+nr Z h'/h|\) on the unit circle is at most \(1+n|s|+nr d_h=1+n\deg f\), by the logarithmic derivative estimate for \(h\).
The power-field theorem at \(M(c)\le53/50\) applies to the positive power \(z=c^{nr}\) even if the degree drops, and gives the factor \(86(t-1)\).
I used that theorem at its proved statement scope.

Finally take the reduced integer presentation of a genuine signed row with a dilation \(r_n\ge r\).
Coprimality after composition and the monomial at zero put the reduced numerator and denominator in one residue class each modulo \(r_n\).
Genuine values make every canceled common factor nonzero at the selected powered nodes.
The reduced two-row graph therefore retains the required orders.
Removal of a primitive gcd and common scalar content yields integral divisors of the original numerator and denominator, giving each reduced row log measure at most the original log one-norm.
For each reduced row of degree at most \(b\), the elementary symmetric bound adds at most \(b\log2\) in passing from log measure to log one-norm.
Applying the fixed-gap lower bound to this reduced pair and using \(b=o(n)\) gives the stated lower slope for the original norms, even when \(r_n\) varies.
