## Discriminant precision and the prime cutoff

At 2026-11-21T09:50 UTC an independent reviewer for Sheng Yin recommends acceptance of the entire [v819 claim](../../claims/Yin_C_G0345_v819.md#statement).
The reviewer took no part in developing the argument and checked the four proof sections against the fixed dependencies below.

Over the open slope locus of the octic reconstruction, the generic algebra has the basis formed by the first eight powers of the octic coordinate and their products with its quadratic generator.
After inverting the leading coefficient and the finitely many reconstruction denominators, its multiplication tables specialize and the sixteen characteristic-zero points give the eigenvalues with their multiplicities, even when a projection takes equal values.
Cayley–Hamilton in the quotient puts each characteristic polynomial evaluated at its linear form in the Laurent ideal generated by the two original equations.
Each of the finitely many multiplier coefficients is rational in the slope, so evaluation at a reduced fraction has a denominator dividing a product of fixed polynomial values in the two exponents and a fixed power of the denominator of the slope.
The additional division by \(k\) in rewriting \(H_s\) is of the same size.
Their common multiple can be chosen at most a fixed power of \(N\).
Only vanishing in characteristic zero excludes a direction.

At a root, both Laurent variables are units and every integral Laurent multiplier has nonnegative valuation.
The derivative term evaluates to \(zP'(z)\), giving the asserted lower bound for the valuation of each product after subtracting the denominator loss.
A factor with at least the average valuation exists even if another factor has negative valuation, with a vanishing factor itself serving if the product is zero.
Among seventeen such choices for sixteen characteristic-zero points two indices select the same point.
Their difference bounds the second coordinate with a loss at most \(\log_p16\), and substitution into either form bounds the first with the same loss.
This check uses no distinctness of reduced points, so primes dividing the exceptional binary form are included.
Positive precision implies that the critical coordinates are units, and the binomial factorization of differences of positive powers preserves that precision.
If their critical value were one, the Bézout inverse of \(z\mapsto(z^k,z^l)\) would give a repeated characteristic-zero root, contrary to squarefreeness.
Nonpositive precision already bounds the root derivative by the elementary losses.

The integral octic has coefficient norm \(O_F(N^2)\), so every irreducible factor has Mahler measure at most that norm up to a fixed factor.
The height inequalities for the quadratic generator and the explicit rational coordinate formulas give both heights \(O_F(\log N)\) in a common field of degree at most sixteen.
I checked the parameter dependence directly against Yu's introductory bound on printed page 337, as recorded in the [source inspection](../literature/Yu99/Yin_U001.md#fixed-input-logarithmic-bound).
For two inputs its ramification factor cancels exactly on dividing the ideal valuation by the ramification index.
For odd \(p\) with \(p^f\equiv1\pmod4\), lifting a residue square root of minus one inside the original completion permits the global adjunction of \(i\) at a place of unchanged residue degree.
The other odd-prime case already satisfies Yu's field hypothesis.
At two the possible doubling of the residue degree gives \(2^f\le 2^{32}\) after adjunction, an absolute factor times \(2^{16}\).
The remaining global degree factors are bounded since the enlarged degree is at most 32.
Each height factor divided by \(f\log p\) is \(O_F(\log N)\), and the exponent parameter \(B=N\) gives the third logarithm.

Every singleton residue root has unit derivative by the product of root differences.
The modular gcd bound gives at most 32 roots counted with multiplicity in the other classes, since a multiplicity \(m\ge2\) contributes at least \(m-1\ge m/2\) to the gcd degree.
Summing the normalized root valuations and applying the monic discriminant product formula gives (1) and (2), including the fixed finite prime sets.
On steep sequences the root counts give eventual squarefreeness and every excluded rational slope is eventually avoided.
Taking a single root with exponent one in the degree law gives \(d=l^{1+o(1)}\) uniformly over the factors.
For fixed positive \(\varepsilon<1/17\), the small-prime upper bound divided by \(d\) is therefore \(l^{-17\varepsilon+o(1)}\).
The derivative average and the individual discriminant bound in v805 give a total logarithmic resultant of at least \(d(\log(l/d)+L+o(1))\).
As \(l/d\to\infty\), the remaining primes give (3), and positivity for each such fixed \(\varepsilon\) gives the stated largest-prime lower bound.
