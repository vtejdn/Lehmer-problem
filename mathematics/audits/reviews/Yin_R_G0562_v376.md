**Reciprocal seed and its lifts.**
Reversal interchanges the two summands of \(A_n\) in formal degree \(3n+1\), and normalization by \(-1\) makes the polynomial monic with unit constant.
At each root \(z\) of the monic cubic \(g\), \(g^*(z)=-z^4\), so the product over its roots has modulus one.
The integral resultant identity then yields Bézout coefficients in \(\mathbb Z[X]\).
At \(\beta=\alpha^n\), the resulting unit \(g^*(\beta)\) has its inverse in \(\mathbb Z[g^*(\beta)]\) by its monic unit minimal polynomial.
This gives \(\alpha\in\mathbb Z[\beta]\), including when \(A_n\) is reducible.
The angular mean in the second variable equals \(\log|g|\) by the linear Jensen formula.
The specialization vector \((n,1)\) has relation lattice generated by \((1,-n)\), so the checked fixed-polynomial Lawton theorem gives \(m(A_n)\to\log\theta\).
This uses the source at its recorded theorem-statement scope, including unit-torus zeros.
Since \(n\ge5Y/4\), the tail of this single sequence works uniformly in the choice of list.
Positivity supplies a nontorsion factor.
A nonreciprocal monic unit factor would occur along with its distinct normalized reverse, and Smyth's bound on each contradicts \(m(A_n)<2\log\theta\).

The same argument applies to \(P_0(x^{2^k})\), which remains reciprocal and has measure \(M(P_0)\) by invariance of the circle mean.
The chosen root \(\gamma_k\) is a nontorsion unit.
Both \(\gamma_k^{2^k}\) and \(\gamma_k^n\), together with their inverses, lie in \(\mathbb Z[\gamma_k^n]\).
The first membership uses the established identity of orders for \(\alpha\), and inverses again follow from monic unit equations.
Since \(n\) is odd these two powers generate \(\gamma_k\) multiplicatively.
The intermediate order for every \(p\mid n\) thus agrees with \(\mathbb Z[\gamma_k]\).
This yields degree preservation and index one simultaneously.
The square-root tower consists of distinct elements since repetition would imply torsion.
Bounded measure and bounded degree would bound all integral coefficients and leave finitely many roots.
Its degrees are therefore unbounded and increase by at most a factor of two in a single step.

**Location of the prescribed list and the trinomial input.**
For the first tower degree at least \(T\), either it is less than \(2T\) after a crossing or the initial degree is already at most \(3n+1=O_C(T)\).
For the other two constructions the ceiling choices give \(T\le d<T+n\) and \(T\le d<T+6n\), respectively.
In every case \(\log(3d)=Y^{1/4}+O_C(1)\) from above, hence \(Y\le y(d)\le Y+O_C(Y^{3/4})<5Y/4\) eventually and \(7Y/4<2y(d)\).
The listed primes apply to one polynomial per construction, throughout that construction.
The asserted cardinality example gives \(\log n\le Y^{1/4}/3\), sufficient for every fixed \(C>0\) at large \(Y\).

For the trinomial, a reciprocal pair \(z,z^{-1}\) would force \(z+z^{-1}=-1\).
Then \(z^d\) has order dividing three whereas \(z+1=-z^2\) has order six.
There are no reciprocal or cyclotomic factors.
Parseval gives \(M(P)\le\sqrt3\), and \((33/25)^4>3\) together with \(g(33/25)<0\) makes this strictly less than \(\theta^2\).
Smyth's bound now permits exactly one irreducible factor with multiplicity one.
Selmer's recorded theorem applies to this same sign choice and every integer \(d\ge2\).
The indices \(0,1\) ensure primitive support differences, and \(\alpha=(\alpha^p)^{d/p}-1\) proves equality of orders for every listed prime.
Index one implies \(E_p=0\), since each isolated length is nonnegative and bounded by the corresponding pair valuation at each discriminant-good prime.

**Full Salem index.**
The cited irreducibility and root distribution apply to every chosen multiple of six, and give \(h(S_d)\to\log2\) and the adjacent support indices.
Include the additional simple root \(1\) in \(F=(x-1)S_d\).
For \(p\mid d\) and \(1\ne\zeta\in\mu_p\), direct subtraction from \(F(\xi)=0\) gives \(F(\zeta\xi)=(\zeta-1)\xi(\xi^d+2)\).
None vanish, because \(F=3\) on the roots of \(x^d+2\).
Thus distinct roots of \(F\) remain distinct on powering, so the degree of the Salem root is preserved.
The absolute resultant of the monic polynomials \(F,x^d+2\) is \(3^d\) by the same evaluation.
In the product of the rotated values the \(d+1\) factors from \(\zeta-1\) contribute \(p^{d+1}\), and the factors from \(\xi^d+2\) contribute \(3^{d(p-1)}\).
In the linear-factor expansion the diagonals contribute \(p^{d+1}\), while each unordered pair contributes the square of its absolute power-difference quotient.
Pairs involving the extra root \(1\) have combined unsquared product \(\prod_{\zeta\ne1}|S_d(\zeta)|=3^{p-1}\).
Removing these pairs leaves the full order index \(3^{(d-2)(p-1)/2}\).
Modulo three, \(F=(x+1)(x^{d/3}-1)^3\), so dividing by \(x-1\) still leaves a repeated root at one.
This excludes the entire index from the good-prime sum defining \(E_p\).
Along these varying inputs \(\log I_p/(pd)\to(\log3)/2\).
The absolute log-radius differences for one exterior and one interior root and \(d-2\) circle roots sum to \(2(d-1)h(S_d)\), giving the stated different fixed-input expression.

**What the collision bound controls.**
When \(I_p=1\), the nonnegative contributions from distinct original residue groups in the local index decomposition vanish, so \(B_0=0\).
At a good support prime, double vanishing with two rows gives divisibility of both reductions by the degree-\(d\) powered polynomial, with its nonzero constant preserving this divisibility after monomial removal.
Primitivity ensures that some row has nonzero reduction, whence \(N\ge d\).
If the support prime were bad in the index-one case, the specified \(B\) would already be zero, with \(N>0\) by the nontorsion input.
For the Salem case the single-root witness has adjacent residues and rows \(-2X^{d/p}-1,\ X^{d/p}+2\) with absolute resultant \(3^{d/p}\).
The multiplicity-one bound therefore bounds the sum of the input's two-group weights by \((d/p)\log3\), before the multiplier four for double roots.
At the support prime \(F'\equiv x^d+2\pmod p\) since \(p\mid d\), and \(F\equiv3\) wherever this derivative vanishes.
It follows that \(p>3\) avoids the discriminant of \(S_d\).
This gives \(B/N\le\log p+4\log(3)/p\), compared to \(\log p\) in the two index-one cases.
Both bounds are \(o(p)\).
The finite two-row comparison supplies witnesses at every listed prime because each preserves degree and lies in the actual window.
The calculation limits only this choice of \(B\), and supplies no upper bound on the full resultant of such a witness or on the contributions at other choices of the powering prime.

**The exact integer endpoint.**
For the separate ledger consequence let \(Y\) be integral and take \(K\) as there.
The dyadic prime count dominates \(K\), and the product of any \(K\) selected primes satisfies \(n\le\exp(Y^{1/4}/3)\).
For the floor choice, \(d/T=1-u\) with \(0\le u<6n/T\), and \(6n/T\le18\exp(-2Y^{1/4}/3)\).
Thus \(0\le Y^{1/4}-\log(3d)=-\log(1-u)\le2u\) eventually.
The mean value theorem bounds \(0\le Y-(\log(3d))^4\le8Y^{3/4}u<1\), including the case \(u=0\).
The ceiling is exactly \(Y\).
The Salem proof uses \(6\mid d,\ p\mid d,\ p>3\) for its arithmetic assertions, so its use here is valid even at primes outside the inner interval of the v371 headline.
Taking the first \(K\) primes of \((Y,2Y]\) makes them the first \(K\) admissible primes as well, since all of them preserve degree.
The measures in this strengthening tend to \(\log2\), leaving the case of inputs with shrinking logarithmic measure open.
