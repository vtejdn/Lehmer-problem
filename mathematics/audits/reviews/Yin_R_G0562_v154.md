**Angular discrepancy and radial movement.**
In v108 the product of the pairwise distances is controlled through the nonzero integral discriminant, so the same proof applies to a separable product with constant term one.
For \(k\le d\), removing \(\rho^{2k}\) from the energy costs at most \(e^2\).
Testing against the enlarged and contracted smoothed arc indicators costs the square root of that normalized energy times \(O(\sqrt{\log(2/h)})\).
The unimodular product of the root moduli gives \(\sum_i|u_i|=2L\), so the phase replacement through \(d\) costs \(O_A((1+\log d)/(dh))\).
The tail of the smoothed indicator costs \(O(1/(dh))\) even using only the trivial normalized moment bound.
Together with the \(O(h)\) shift of the means at \(h=d^{-1/2}\), these estimates give the required discrepancy for arcs of every length.
The at most \(2s\) zero arguments then exclude a proportion \(O_A(sD_d)\) of the roots, including any phases on the excluded boundaries.
On the remaining rays the distance from \(1\) to the ray containing \(\xi/z\) is at least \(\sin\kappa\) or one, so each logarithmic-derivative summand has modulus \(O(1/\kappa)\) throughout the radial segment.
The sum of the integrals of \(|dJ/J|\) on the retained paths, divided by the full degree \(d\), is \(O(sL/\sqrt d)\).

**Peak height and span.**
The zeros and critical points of the nonconstant real trigonometric polynomial \(J\) divide the circle into \(O(s)\) intervals on which \(h_0(J)\) is monotone.
This bounds the discrepancy contribution by \(O_A(sQD_d)\).
The bad points cost at most their fraction times \(Q\), while at the actual roots the mean of \(n|u_i|\) equals \(L\).
These facts give (4).
For \(r=e^{-y}\), the polynomial exterior maximum principle gives \(\max_{|z|=r}|K|\ge r^{2s}H\).
At the specified \(y\) this leaves \(\log|K/2|\ge Q/2\) somewhere on that circle.
Poisson majorization costs \(O(1/y)\) and hence gives \(I\gg Q\min(1,Q/s)\), including the zero case by continuity.
For \(sD_d\) sufficiently small the \(sQD_d\) term is absorbable when \(Q\ge s\) and \(sL/\sqrt d=O_A(L)\).
When \(Q\le s\), solving the resulting quadratic inequality gives \(Q\ll_A\sqrt{sL}+s^2D_d\).
At a maximal root either \(nb\le1\) or \((n-s)b\le\log H+O(1)\), proving \(nb\ll1+Q\) throughout.
For shrinking irreducible inputs the gap \(q=n-s>d/3\) supplies \(db\gg1/L\) from v152 and forces \(d\to\infty\) by v151.
When \(s^2D_d\le1\), one has \(sD_d\le\sqrt{D_d}\to0\), so (1) applies eventually and its bounded terms can be absorbed in \(Q\gg db\).
This gives \(sL\gg(db)^2\) and \(s\gg L^{-3}\) on that branch.
On the other branch \(s>d^{1/4}/\sqrt{\log(2d)}\) dominates \((\log(2d))^3\gg L^{-3}\) eventually.

**Quadratic transfer.**
Jensen's formula in \(Y\) gives the circle mean of \(g(J)\), and the two roots \(-\alpha_i^{\pm n}\) give \(g(J(\alpha_i))=n|u_i|\).
Reciprocity gives \(|u_i|\le b\), so \(nb\ll1+Q\) controls the root values on the omitted rays, while the real-line formula for \(g\) bounds its circle values by \(Q+\log 2\).
If the path of nonzero \(w\)-values has logarithmic length \(\lambda\le1/10\), every point lies within \((e^\lambda-1)|v|\) of the starting point \(v\).
For \(|v|\le4\) choose the sign of one square root to minimize the difference of the two discriminant square roots.
That minimum is at most the square root of the modulus of the difference of their squares, giving \(O(\sqrt\lambda)\) control of both matched roots through the branch values \(2,-2\).
Taking the largest modulus is Lipschitz in this matching, and the logarithm is Lipschitz on \([1,\infty)\).
For \(|v|>4\) the disk containing the path is at distance \(\gg |v|\) from the interval \([-2,2]\), so the larger-root logarithm has derivative \(O(1/|v|)\) and changes by \(O(\lambda)\).
Subdivision handles every longer path with cost \(O(1+\lambda)\), which is \(O(\sqrt\lambda+\lambda)\) for \(\lambda>1/10\).
Applying Cauchy–Schwarz to the short-path square-root term and the discrepancy estimate to the variation \(O(s(1+Q))\) gives (6).
For \(s\le d^{1/3-\epsilon}\), the conditional hypothesis \(sD_d\le c_A\) holds for all sufficiently large \(d\).
The terms \(sD_d,s^{3/2}D_d,s^3D_d^2\) then decay at powers \(1/6+\epsilon,3\epsilon/2,3\epsilon\), respectively, up to logarithms.
The two radial terms decay at least at powers \(1/12+\epsilon/2\) and \(1/6+\epsilon\).
Each permits the displayed \(\gamma\), and the finitely many smaller degrees are covered uniformly since the coefficients of a monic polynomial of bounded degree and measure are bounded.
The v151 degree bound makes the absolute error \(o(L)\) for shrinking irreducible inputs.

**Vanishing and sparse coefficients.**
The integrand \(g(J)\) is continuous and nonnegative, so its integral vanishes exactly for \(|J|\le2\) everywhere.
Equality in the resulting \(L^2\) norm bound \(a_0^2+2\sum a_j^2\le4\) forces the continuous real function \(J\) to be constant.
For strict inequality and \(a_s\ne0\) only one symmetric pair of coefficients \(\pm1\) can occur.
A further constant \(\pm1\) gives peak three, and otherwise the stated factorization expresses \(P\) as two binomials whose roots are all roots of unity.
Thus every admissible \(P\) with a noncyclotomic irreducible factor has strictly positive two-variable measure.
For the general sparse polynomial the maximal-root equation gives \(qb\le\log K_1\), where \(q\ge\delta d\) and \(b>0\).
Integrality then gives \(K_1\ge2\), extending \(L\log K_1\gg_\delta1\) from small to arbitrary positive \(L\).
There are at least two nonzero terms because \(P\) has a leading coefficient and a nonzero constant term.
The recorded DS17 Theorem 1 therefore gives \(H_P\le2^{k-2}e^L\) in its stated support range.
Together with \(K_1\le(k-1)H_P\) this yields both sparse lower bounds, with \(L>1\) immediate for the \(1/k\) bound.

The full submitted argument supports (1)–(3), the positivity and relative asymptotic in their respective ranges, and the two bounds for general sparse units.
