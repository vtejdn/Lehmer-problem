### Integral solutions, dual vectors and the arithmetic bound

Dividing \(f-P\) by \(z-1\) gives the reciprocal polynomial of formal degree \(2m-2\), so the symmetric Laurent basis gives every integral \(R\) without a parity restriction.
Its leading coefficient is \(1-q\), which fixes the constant of \(f\) at \(q\) and leaves every lower coefficient free in \(\mathbb Z\).
The affine translate in (3) therefore represents all integral solutions at the prescribed constant, and the evaluation map is invertible.

The coefficient map obtained by interpolation is the inverse lattice basis matrix, so its rows generate the entire Euclidean dual.
For \(x_j\) a root of \(H\), division of \(Q\) successively by \(x-T\) and \(x-x_j\) gives the stated divided difference for each coefficient row.
The \(A_i\) have distinct successive degrees and unit leading coefficients, so their change-of-basis matrix to \(X,\ldots,X^h\) is unimodular over \(\mathbb Z\).
Consequently (4) gives exactly the dual, including its sign convention, and a nonzero vector has a nonzero representative \(A\).

The degree of \(T\) is \(m=h+1\): its degree is at most \(m\) from \(Q\), and at least \(m\) because \(\sigma\) has degree \(2m\) and is quadratic over \(\mathbb Q(T)\).
Thus a nonzero integral \(A\) of degree at most \(h\) and zero constant cannot have rational \(A(T)\).

### Changing degrees and the converse

Once finitely many initial terms are omitted, a Salem sequence tending to one satisfies \(\sigma\le2\).
In bounded degree its conjugates then have modulus at most two, which bounds every integer coefficient and leaves finitely many minimal polynomials.
Its degrees must therefore tend to infinity.
Condition (2) then forces \(q(d)\ge\lceil B_h\rceil\) eventually and \(B_h/q(d)\to0\), so (1) and \(E\to0\) give \(\theta-q(d)\to0\).
For \(q(d)=d^2\), the additive correction \(\theta-q(d)-E\) is \(O(\sqrt{\log d}/d)\).

For the converse, \(q(d)\ge2\) eventually under (2).
The [positive return](../../ledger/Yin_L_G0462_v32.md#boyd-return) has a positive exterior root because its value at one is negative.
Rouché's theorem for \(zf+t f^*\), \(0<t<1\), and passage to \(t=1\) leave at most one exterior root, counting multiplicity; reciprocity then leaves exactly one interior root as well.
The irreducible factor containing that interior root must also contain the exterior root, since its integral nonzero constant has modulus at least one.
All its other conjugates lie on the unit circle, and the factor is reciprocal.
I rechecked [the two-point calculation](../../claims/Yin_C_G0345_v4.md#two-points): cancellation of the exterior Blaschke factor, evaluation at \(0,1/\sigma\), and the real Schwarz--Pick upper endpoint give
\[
 0<T-2\le(1-1/\theta)(\theta-q)<\theta-q.
\]
A reciprocal quadratic factor has integral trace at least three, so excess below one excludes it.
The resulting Salem roots tend to one, and their degrees may be lower than the Pisot degrees.
