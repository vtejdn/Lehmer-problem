## The coprime power directions determine all the rational equations

Positive \(V\)-degree and rational irreducibility make the coefficient content in \(\mathbb Q[U]\) a unit.
If all coefficients vanished at \(t(x)\), its minimal polynomial would divide that content.
The nonzero specialized equation thus bounds the degree of \(t(y)\) over \(\mathbb Q(x)\) by \(b\), and adjoining \(y\) costs at most a further factor two.
Interchanging the coordinates yields \(D\le2\min(bd_x,ad_y)\).
Both input degrees and the joint degree are therefore comparable to their maximum with constants depending only on the bidegree bounds.
Multiplicative independence would give \(m(x)m(y)\gg d_xd_y/(D(\log(3D))^{212})\) by the rank-two Amoroso–David theorem.
The first degree bound makes this tend to infinity as the maximum degree grows, contrary to \(m(x),m(y)\le M\).

After dependence, the group generated by \(x,y\) modulo its torsion is infinite cyclic.
Choose its generator's orientation and invert \(y\) if necessary to obtain \(x=\omega z^p,\ y=\eta z^q\) with positive exponents.
The exponents are coprime because \(x,y\) generate the group in question.
A representative \(z\) is a product of signed powers of the original coordinates, so \(z,\omega,\eta\) all lie in the joint field, and \(\omega,\eta\) are roots of unity.
Inverting \(y\) changes neither its trace nor its field or height.
The exact absolute-height identities are \(h(x)=p h(z)\) and \(h(y)=q h(z)\).
A nonidentity substituted Laurent polynomial has exponent span at most \(2(ap+bq)\), irrespective of cancellations, and its coefficients lie in the maximal abelian extension.
Since it vanishes at nonzero \(z\), its span bounds \(D'=[\mathbb Q^{\mathrm{ab}}(z):\mathbb Q^{\mathrm{ab}}]\), also bounded by \(D\).
Multiplying the relative height estimate by \(D'\le2(ap+bq)\) and using the stated comparison of logarithmic factors for \(D'\le D\) gives the claimed lower bound for \(a h(x)+b h(y)\).
The left side is \(O_{A,B,M}(1/\max(d_x,d_y))\), which is eventually smaller than that logarithmic lower bound uniformly in all exponents, torsion orders and polynomial coefficients.
This proves that substitution is an identity under one threshold depending only on \(A,B,M\).

On the complex parameter torus, \(U=\omega Z^p+\omega^{-1}Z^{-p}\) has degree \(2p\) as a rational function, and \(Y^p=\xi X^q\) with \(\xi=\eta^p/\omega^q\).
Taking the two traces gives \(T_p(V)=\xi T_q(U)\) when \(\xi=\pm1\).
For the other phases the two expressions \(W+W^{-1}\) and \(\xi W+\xi^{-1}W^{-1}\) satisfy the stated quadratic with constant term \(c^2-4\).
In a generic fiber the two roots for each trace are its coordinate and its inverse.
For two unchanged coordinates one needs \((Z'/Z)^p=(Z'/Z)^q=1\), giving \(Z'=Z\).
For mixed inversion the phase equation forces \(X^{2q}=1\) or \(Y^{2p}=1\), each of which holds at only finitely many parameters.
For simultaneous inversion the two equations for \(S=Z'Z\) have right-hand sides \(\omega^{-2},\eta^{-2}\), compatible precisely when \(\xi^2=1\).
Coprimality gives exactly one such \(S\) by Bézout, and \(S/Z\) differs from \(Z\) outside finitely many parameters.
Thus \([\mathbb C(Z):\mathbb C(U,V)]\) is two in the signed cases and one in the others, by separability and the generic fiber count.
Dividing \(2p\) by those respective degrees gives exactly the degrees in \(V\) of the displayed monic equations.
Consequently they are the geometric minimal equations over \(\mathbb C(U)\), primitive and irreducible in \(\mathbb C[U,V]\).

Distinct conjugates of \(c\) give distinct monic equations because their difference is \(c'-c\) times the nonconstant polynomial \(T_q(U)T_p(V)-c-c'\).
Their full Galois orbit is transitive and its monic product has rational coefficients.
Every rational identity on the parametrization contains each conjugate factor, and no nonempty proper subset of the factors can give a rational factor of that product.
The rational irreducibility of the input \(P\) therefore identifies it up to rational scalar with the indicated product, or with the signed equation in the first case.
The geometric bidegree is \((2q,2p)\) for \(\xi\ne\pm1\), and there are \(\varphi(s)/2\) distinct conjugates at exact order \(s\ge3\).
For the two signed equations the bidegree is \((q,p)\).
All leading degrees add in the products, and \(\varphi(s)^2\ge s/2\) gives finiteness at the fixed bidegree bounds.

For each individual allowed equation take fixed \(\omega=1\) and \(\eta^p=\xi\), and let \(z\) range over the indicated positive radicals of the Lehmer unit.
Their degrees \(10j\) tend to infinity and their total measure is fixed.
Writing \(e_\eta=[\mathbb Q(\eta):\mathbb Q]\), one has \(d_x\ge10j/p\) and \(d_y\ge10j/(q e_\eta)\), since after adjoining \(\eta\) the equation \(z^q=y/\eta\) recovers \(z\) at degree cost at most \(q\).
The degree upper bounds from \(\mathbb Q(z,\eta)\) give \(m(x)\le p\,m(z)\) and \(m(y)\le q e_\eta\,m(z)\).
The inputs are nontorsion units, and any embedding sending \(z\) to a circle conjugate extends to the compositum, with every image of \(\eta\) still on the circle.
Each coordinate then has a circle conjugate, whose complex conjugate equals its inverse, proving reciprocity.
If the unrestricted infimum is zero, the same fixed parameters applied to a shrinking sequence of nontorsion unit measures force both new measures to tend to zero.
Bounded-degree finiteness makes both degrees diverge, and Smyth's nonreciprocal bound makes both coordinates reciprocal eventually.
Thus the unconditional bounded-measure examples and the conditional shrinking examples have exactly the stated quantifiers.
