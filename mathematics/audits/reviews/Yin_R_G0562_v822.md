## The uniform energy budget and the two counts per interval

The proof establishes (1) and (2) with an absolute constant, including the cumulative bound and all three stated subexponential estimates.

Equation (3) of [C345 v108](../../claims/Yin_C_G0345_v108.md#phases) holds for every monic irreducible integer polynomial with constant term of absolute value one.
Its discriminant is taken on the original distinct conjugates, so the two copies of \(\beta^{-1}\) in the reflected list cause no problem.
Indeed the off-diagonal bound uses the nonzero integral discriminant, and the regularized logarithms, including the diagonal, are finite for \(\rho<1\).
For \(\rho=e^{-1/d}\) the right side is at most \(2(d-1)\log B+(d-1)+d\log d\), using \(1-e^{-2/d}\ge1/d\) for \(d\ge4\).
The reflected sum is \(u_j+2\beta^{-j}\) and \(\rho^{2j}\ge e^{-1}\) for \(j\le d/2\).
Subtracting \(2\beta^{-j}\) by the squared triangle inequality costs at most an absolute multiple of \(\sum_{j\le h}1/j\) in addition to an absolute multiple of the reflected budget.
This gives (3) uniformly even when \(\beta\) tends to one.

For \(a=j/h\in(0,1]\), \(g'_j(t)=a t^{a-1}(1-t^{-2a})\) lies in \([0,1]\) for every \(t\ge1\).
A bin of width \(1/8\) therefore controls the variation by \(1/8\), independently of its position, including when its left endpoint is used as a reference but is excluded from the bin.
The preceding Newton sums and coefficients determine \(s_j\bmod j\).
Choosing the nearest member of that residue class to \(g_j(t_0)\), with the specified tie rule, makes the reference \(b_j\) reproducible at every step.
The decoder recovers \(s_j=b_j+jz_j\) and then the exact \(a_j\).
The first \(h\) coefficients determine a monic reciprocal polynomial of degree \(2h\), whose unique exterior root identifies \(\beta\).
If \(z_j\ne0\), its integrality gives \(j/2\le j|z_j|/2\) and \(1/8\le j|z_j|/8\).
Hence \(|u_j|\ge3j|z_j|/8\), and summing the squared estimates with weights \(1/j\) bounds \(\sum jz_j^2\) and thus \(\sum j|z_j|\) as in (5).

The one-coordinate generating series with \(x=e^{-vj}\) is \(1+2\sum_{k\ge1}x^k=(1+x)/(1-x)\le(1-x)^{-2}\).
Extending the product past \(h\) increases it, and expansion of its logarithm gives the bound \(2\sum_{\ell\ge1}\ell^{-2}/v\) for every \(v>0\).
With \(v=E^{-1/2}\) this yields the square-root exponent with an absolute constant.
Alternatively, each decoded prefix admits at most \(1+(2(d-2)+1/8)/j\le3d/j\) choices, by the common residue class and \(|u_j|\le d-2\).
Multiplying these conditional bounds and using \(h!\ge(h/e)^h\) yields \((6e)^h\) in every bin, uniformly for arbitrarily large \(B\).
Taking the smaller exponent and using \(1+8w\le8(1+w)\) gives (1), since \(\min\{d,\sqrt{d\log(2dB)}\}\) has a positive absolute lower bound.

A singleton uses one bin, and at \(B=1\) there are no Salem numbers.
The full interval has \(1+w=B^h\), and each term in the degree sum is bounded by the claimed expression at \(D\) before multiplication by the number of degrees.
Here \(B\ge1\) ensures monotonicity of the prefactor and of both candidate exponents.
For fixed \(B\), \(\sqrt{d\log(2dB)}=o(d)\).
If \(\log B\to0\), the additional logarithmic cost \((d/2)\log B\) is also \(o(d)\).
For a unit-length window the prefactor is two, and \(\log B=o(d)\) already gives \(\sqrt{d\log(2dB)}=o(d)\).
The upper bounds apply also to intervals containing no Salem numbers.
