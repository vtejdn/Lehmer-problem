## The small-value loss and the nontorsion factor

At 2026-11-20T17:47 UTC a separate agent acting for Sheng Yin independently checked the full statement and both proof sections of C0345v602.
The reviewer did not participate in proof development or receive its discussion and consulted R0562v737 for record format.
The transfer and all three consequences hold under the stated hypotheses.

Reciprocity makes the centered Laurent polynomial real on the circle, and the absence of zeros gives a constant sign there.
Its extreme exponents are distinct, with coefficients of absolute value one, so the positive choice of \(u\) is nonconstant and has a strictly positive minimum.
The quadratic \(YF\) has product of roots one and root sum \(2-1/u\).
At \(u=1/4\) the double root is \(-1\), whose Jensen contribution is zero.
Below that level the contribution from the roots is the stated inverse hyperbolic cosine, and above it the contribution is zero.
The positive part of \(\log|F|\) is bounded on the torus, and the fiber integral given by Jensen is bounded below by \(\min\log u\).
Tonelli applied to the negative part thus justifies integration over both variables, including the singular fibers.
The integral of \(\log u\) is \(m(P)\).
Lück's Remark 8.1 and equation (8.2) apply to each fixed nonzero scalar Laurent polynomial in two variables by clearing its negative powers with a monomial.
The multiplicative measure is positive, so taking logarithms preserves the specialization limit.

Since \(\int\log u=m(P)\ge0\), some closed arc of positive length lies in \(u>1/4\).
Each complete mesh interval of size \(2\pi/N\) in that arc gives two distinct zeros, one on each side of its midpoint, by the strict opposite signs at the endpoints and midpoint.
The number of complete intervals grows with \(N\).
The theorem of Beukers and Smyth in Section 4.1 applies to the full complex Laurent curve, including reducible curves.
A binomial component has a parametrization of the stated form after dividing its exponent vector by its gcd and choosing a root of its constant.
When \(b\ne0\), this component meets \(Y=1\), where \(F=1\).
When \(b=0\), \(X\) varies surjectively and the restriction is either one or an affine function of \(u(X)\) with nonzero slope.
Both cases exclude a binomial component, so all torsion pairs form a fixed finite set.
For each \(N\) the map \(X\mapsto(X,X^N)\) injects the specialized torsion zeros into that set.

For \(N>0\) the coefficients at \(\pm(h+N)\) come uniquely from the corresponding endpoints of \(u(X)X^{\pm N}\).
Normalization gives a monic integer polynomial with unit constant, so each monic irreducible factor has unit constant.
Kronecker's theorem makes the factor containing a nontorsion circle root have strictly positive measure, while its measure is bounded by the specialized measure by additivity and nonnegativity.
One may take \(N\) large enough for both this extraction and the prescribed error, and then choose a separate error tending to zero along an input sequence.
Taking the infimum and letting the error decrease gives the stated bound on \(B(P)\).
On the sublevel set the loss integrand is at most \(\log(1/u-2)\le\log(1/u)\), which proves the sufficient condition.
The source check covered the two cited theorem statements and their local applications; the original proofs of the scalar approximation and the torsion-point theorem remain imported inputs.
