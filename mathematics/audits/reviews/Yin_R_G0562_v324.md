### Derivative orders and clipping

With \(D=n/d>1\), the \(k\)-th derivative of the slow term in the fast variable has supremum at most \(HD^{-k}\), since differentiation preserves the Fourier degree and each application of Bernstein costs at most \(d\) in the original variable.
This bound is at most \(1/4\) for both \(k=h,h+1\), including when \(H=0\).
The two fast derivatives are \(2\sin s\) and \(2\cos s\) up to sign and order, so splitting where their absolute values agree gives an absolute number of intervals with the selected derivative of \(f\) of magnitude at least \(3/4\).

Let \(m>0\) be the sublevel length on one closed interval and set \(\delta=m/(2k)\).
Greedy selection from its compact sublevel set gives \(k+1\) points spaced by at least \(\delta\), since otherwise at most \(k\) intervals of length \(\delta\) would cover a set of length \(m\).
At point \(j\), numbered from zero, the divided-difference denominator has magnitude at least \(\delta^k j!(k-j)!\).
The sum of the reciprocal factorials is \(2^k/k!\), whereas the divided difference has magnitude at least \(3/(4k!)\) by Rolle's theorem.
Thus \(m\le 4k(4v/3)^{1/k}\) when \(|f|\le v\) on the selected set.
For \(0<v\le1\), replacing either possible order by \(q=h+1\) gives a normalized period bound \(\min(1,Cq v^{1/q})\) with absolute \(C\ge1\).
Its integral against \(dv/v\) up to one is \(q(1+\log(Cq))\), which accounts for the full dependence on \(h\), even at a multiple zero.

The threshold equations in v320 give \(O(d)\) exceptional periods, since each of the \(O(d)\) relevant arcs has length \(O(1/n)\).
The first- and second-derivative alternatives on each remaining period cost \(O(\sqrt u)\) after clipping at \(u\), and the independent cosine has the same tail bound uniformly in its center.
The clipped logarithm after subtracting \(\log(2+|b|)\) has variation \(O(d\log(6/u))\) in the slow variable, giving that quantity divided by \(n\) on the shifted mesh for each fast phase.
Combining these three normalized bounds with \(u=D^{-2}\) gives (4).
For the optional term-count estimate the enlarged runs in v320 have total normalized length \(O(d/n)\) and supremum at least one on each run.
Its measurable-set estimate then costs \(O(K)\) per unit length for \(K\) nonzero terms, so the better of the two error bounds may be used.
A constant \(b\) gives equality of the two means by periodic substitution.

### Amplitude and the feedback inequality

The quadratic Jensen formula gives \(\log^+|b|\le g(b)+\log 2\) pointwise, including on \(|b|\le2\).
For \(p\ne0\), apply the disk maximum principle to \(w^{2d}p(1/w)\) on \(|w|\le1/r\) to get \(H\le r^{-2d}M_r\).
The harmonic extension of \(\log^+|p|\) dominates \(\log|p|\) even at zeros, and the Poisson kernel on \(|z|=r\) has maximum \((1+r)/(1-r)\) for normalized circle measure.
Thus for \(r=e^{-s}\), \(0<s\le1\), one obtains \(\log H\le2ds+C(1+I)/s\).
Taking \(s=\sqrt{(1+I)/d}\) when \(1+I\le d\) gives \(O(\sqrt{d(1+I)})\), and \(s=1\) in the other case gives \(O(1+I)\).
Allowing \(H\le1\), including \(p=0\), yields (5).

The polynomial \(z^n f\) has leading coefficient \(e^{i\phi}\) and degree \(2n\), since \(d<n\).
Each elementary symmetric coefficient is bounded by its binomial multiplicity times \(e^\mu\) by Jensen's root product.
In particular \(\mu\ge0\) and \(\log(4\max(1,H))=O_A(n)\) for \(\mu\le A\), uniformly in the phase and the complex Fourier coefficients of real \(b\).
For \(n\ge2\) and \(d\le n^{2/3}\), one has \(\log D\ge(\log n)/3\).
Hence \(\log(2h)=O_A(\log(2D))\), and multiplying the ceiling bound \(h\le1+\log(4\max(1,H))/\log D\) gives the order estimate used before (6).
In (6), \(D\ge n^{1/3}\) absorbs the term proportional to \(I/D\) at large \(n\), and \(\sqrt d/D=d^{3/2}/n\le1\) leaves a term \(C_A\sqrt{1+I}\le I/4+O_A(1)\).
This gives \(I=O_A(1)\) before reusing (5).
For the finitely many smaller \(n\), the elementary coefficient bound gives a uniform bound on \(H\), and hence on \(I\).
Substitution into (4) now proves (1), with its two terms tending to zero uniformly along any range \(d=o(n^{2/3})\).

### Width, amplitude and arithmetic scope

For \(H\le H_0n^\alpha\) and \(D\ge\log(2n)\), write \(L=\log(2n)\).
Then \(h=O_{H_0,\alpha}(1+L/\log D)\) and \(\log(2h)=O_{H_0,\alpha}(\log L)\).
Since \(\log D\ge\log L\) and \(D\le n\), (4) is \(O_{H_0,\alpha}(L/D)\), uniformly in \(n,d\) for large \(n\).
This proves convergence if \(D/L\to\infty\) and bounds the error by \(C_{H_0,\alpha}c\) whenever \(D\ge L/c\) with \(0<c\le1\).
At most \(2d+1\le2n+1\) Laurent coefficients contribute to \(H\), which verifies the coefficient-growth consequence.

For integral traces, v156 applies to the symmetric integral Laurent polynomial \(-B(z+z^{-1})\).
Its zero cases yield either a quadratic in \(z^n\) with all roots of unity or the two binomial factors of degrees \(n-j,n+j\) for \(B=\pm C_j\).
Thus \(M(P)>1\) implies \(I\ge c_2>0\), with \(\log M(P)=\mu\) for \(\phi=0\).
In the bounded-diagonal proof one may take \(A=1\), since \(\mu>1\) is already sufficient.
For \(d\le c n^{2/3}\) the second error term in (1) is at most \(C_1 c^{3/2}\) and the first tends uniformly to zero.
In the polynomial-amplitude case one can likewise choose \(c\) with \(C_{H_0,\alpha}c<c_2/2\).
These are gaps for the whole polynomial, with reducibility and repeated roots allowed.
The Taylor comparison in v314 has \(\mu\sim2d\log(n/d)\) on its sublinear-width family, so its square-root obstruction is outside the bounded-diagonal hypothesis.
A monic reciprocal polynomial of degree \(2n\) has the stated wider representation by pairing its Laurent coefficients after division by \(z^n\).
The review covers v324 and the stated interfaces of its inputs, with the original source proofs at their recorded inspection depths.
The range of unrestricted widths up to \(n-1\) and the full Lehmer problem remain open here.
