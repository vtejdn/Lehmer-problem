# Independent review of complete primitive root sets for the six-term section

## Order, exceptional roots and uniform cardinality

At 2026-11-20T12:44 UTC, a separate reviewing agent for Sheng Yin independently checked the full statement and both arguments of [Claim v551](../../claims/Yin_C_G0345_v551.md#statement).
The order alternative, its characteristic-two converse, the uniform count and the application to irreducible factors hold as stated.

For a prime power \(q=\ell^j\Vert n\), fixing the other components of a primitive root leaves every primitive \(q\)-th root available, since the components have coprime orders.
After reducing exponents modulo \(q\), the resulting polynomial is a multiple of \(1+U^{q/\ell}+\cdots+U^{(\ell-1)q/\ell}\), since those roots are distinct in characteristic \(p\).
The degree bound on the quotient makes the coefficient in position \(r+hq/\ell\) precisely its coefficient in position \(r\), independently of \(h\).
Thus the class of terms with exponents congruent to \(r\) modulo \(q/\ell\) vanishes throughout the product of primitive sets.
For \(\ell\ge7\), six formal terms cannot occupy every position in a class, so the common coefficient vanishes and one may split all the way to residues modulo \(q\).
This reasoning applies afresh to each vanishing subsum after every split, even when terms coincide or cancel in the residue field.
The resulting modulus is \(n/\gcd(n,30)\), also for \(n=1\).

A divisor \(h=\gcd(e,a)>1\) separates the triples because \(h\nmid b\).
Each triple is then a vanishing sum, and any further nontrivial partition of its three terms would isolate a nonzero monomial.
For their common root \(y=z^a\), expansion gives \(yB+(1+y)g=-(y^2+y+1)\).
On this quadratic locus the three possible values of \(g\) are \(-y,-1,2y^2\) according to the residue of \(s\).
The third can vanish only in characteristic two, where this quadratic has precisely the two roots of order three.
Conversely either such root gives both zero values when \(s\equiv2\pmod3\), and \(z^a\) has order \(n/\gcd(n,a)\).

If \(a\) is a unit modulo \(e>s\), all three entries of each normalized triple are distinct, so every residue must be matched by an entry of the other triple.
Reflection therefore permutes the three residues and fixes exactly one, because fixing both zero and one would force \(e\mid2\).
Equating the sum of the interchanged residues with twice the fixed residue yields the three stated divisibilities; each divisor is positive for \(s\ge3\) and at most \(2s-1\).

There are at most \(\sum_{n\le30(2s-1)} n\) roots from the bounded orders and at most \(2a\) from the exceptional polynomial, including possible multiplicities in that degree bound.
With \(b\gg as^3\) the ratio to \(b\) is \(O(1/(as)+1/s^3)\), uniformly for positive \(a\).
Every irreducible factor has nonzero roots since \(G(0)=1\), and its distinct Frobenius conjugates share their order.
A factor of degree bounded below by a fixed positive multiple of \(b\) therefore cannot have a complete primitive set of that order in the zero locus for sufficiently large \(s\).
The [earlier cycle bound](../../claims/Yin_C_G0345_v545.md#statement) concerns simultaneous lower bounds along selected progressions and is used only for comparison.
