# The sharp cyclotomic degree cost of matching a Salem polynomial

<a id="statement"></a>
## The completion degree and the last coefficient at the original degree

Let \(P\) be the minimal polynomial of a Salem number \(e^a\), with original degree \(d\) tending to infinity and \(0<a\le A\) bounded.
Write \(D(P,k)\) for the least degree of a monic product of cyclotomic polynomials having the same first \(k\) coefficients below its leading coefficient as \(P\).
The product may have repeated factors and must have degree at least \(k\).
For each fixed \(\delta>0,\ B>1/2+\delta\), uniformly in the integers \(k\) with
\[
          (1/2+\delta)\log d\ \le\ ak\ \le\ B\log d,
\]
one has
\[
          D(P,k)=(1+o(1))\,\frac{e^{ak}}{1-e^{-a}}.                \tag{1}
\]

Let \(K(P)\) be the longest prefix attainable by a monic cyclotomic product of degree exactly \(d\), and put \(t_d=\log(d(1-e^{-a}))\).
There exists \(\eta_d=\eta_d(A)\to0\) such that for all sufficiently large \(d\)
\[
   \left\lfloor\frac{t_d-d^{-1/4}}a\right\rfloor
       \ \le\ K(P)\ \le\
   \left\lfloor\frac{t_d+\eta_d}a\right\rfloor.                     \tag{2}
\]
In particular, along every hypothetical Salem sequence with \(a\to0\),
\[
                   aK(P)-\log(da)\longrightarrow0.                  \tag{3}
\]
For \(a\) bounded away from zero, (2) determines \(K(P)=\lfloor t_d/a\rfloor\) whenever \(t_d/a\) stays outside the \((d^{-1/4}+\eta_d)/a\)-neighborhood of the integers.
The upper estimate uses the full rational cyclotomic factorization of the output.
The coordinator proposed closing the factor of two between the construction and the unit-circle positivity bound in [the preceding prefix theorem](Yin_C_G0345_v138.md#statement).
The contributor supplied the divisor weights below and their application to the minimum degree.

<a id="divisors"></a>
## A divisor weight which charges at most the degree of each cyclotomic factor

Here \(k\to\infty\) is an integer.
Write \(H=\lfloor k^{7/8}\rfloor,\ J=\{k-H+1,\ldots,k\},\ R=k^{1/40}\).
For \(s>0\) put \(f(s)=\max(0,\min(1,1-\log s/\log R))\).
For \(1\le l\le k\) define \(w_l=l f(j/l)\) if some \(j\in J\) is a multiple of \(l\) with \(j/l\le R\), and \(w_l=0\) otherwise.
Such a \(j\) is unique since \(l\ge(k-H)/R>H\) eventually.
In particular \(0\le w_l\le l\), and \(w_j=j\) for every \(j\in J\).
Set \(w_l=0\) for \(l>k\).
Uniformly for all integers \(n\ge1\), the Möbius function and Euler totient satisfy
\[
                 \sum_{l\mid n}\mu(n/l)w_l\ \le\ (1+o(1))\varphi(n). \tag{4}
\]

First suppose \(n\le kR\) and at least one summand has nonzero weight.
For any such divisor \(l\), the rational number \(n/j=(n/l)/(j/l)\) has reduced denominator at most \(R\).
For any two indices \(j,j'\in J\), the difference \(|n/j-n/j'|\) is at most \(kRH/(k-H)^2=o(R^{-2})\).
Distinct rational numbers of denominators at most \(R\) are at least \(R^{-2}\) apart.
Consequently all nonzero weights for this \(n\) use the very same \(j\).
The sum in (4) is precisely \(\sum_{l\mid(n,j)}\mu(n/l)l f(j/l)\), since any divisor of \((n,j)\) with \(j/l>R\) has zero contribution to this expression.
Replacing \(f(j/l)\) by \(f(j/n)\) costs in absolute value at most
\[
                \frac{n}{\log R}\sum_{v\mid n}\frac{|\mu(v)|\log v}{v},         \tag{5}
\]
by the Lipschitz bound in the logarithmic variable.
The resulting sum is \(f(j/n)c_n(j)\), where \(c_n(j)=\sum_{1\le h\le n,\ (h,n)=1}\exp(2\pi i h j/n)\).
Indeed inclusion-exclusion for \((h,n)=1\) gives \(c_n(j)=\sum_{l\mid(n,j)}l\mu(n/l)\).
This real number has absolute value at most \(\varphi(n)\), and \(f(j/n)\in[0,1]\).

For completeness, (5) divided by \(\varphi(n)\) is \(O((1+\log\log(3n))^6/\log R)\).
The divisor sum without the prefactor is exactly \(\prod_{p\mid n}(1+1/p)\sum_{p\mid n}\log p/(p+1)\), with empty sums zero.
Take \(x=\max(3,(\log(3n))^2)\) and \(s=1+\log2/\log x\).
For \(p\le x\), \((1+1/p)^{1/2}\le(1-p^{-s})^{-1}\), and \(\zeta(s)\le1+1/(s-1)\) by integral comparison.
The primes \(p\mid n\) above \(x\) have \(\sum1/p\le(\log n)/(x\log2)\), so \(\prod_{p\mid n}(1+1/p)\ll(1+\log x)^2\).
Also \(n/\varphi(n)\le\zeta(2)\prod_{p\mid n}(1+1/p)\).
The remaining sum of logarithms is \(O((1+\log x)^2)\) by summing \(\log m/m\) over \(m\le x\) and bounding the large-prime contribution by \((\log n)/x\).
Since \(n\le kR\), the relative error in (5) tends uniformly to zero.

If \(n>kR\), the left side of (4) has absolute value at most \(k\tau(n)\), where \(\tau\) counts divisors.
For each fixed \(\epsilon>0\) one has \(\tau(n)^2\ll_\epsilon n^\epsilon\).
Indeed for all sufficiently large primes, \((h+1)^2\le4^h\le p^{\epsilon h}\), and the supremum of \((h+1)^2 p^{-\epsilon h}\) is finite at each remaining prime.
Since \(n/\varphi(n)\le\tau(n)\), the relative bound is \(O_\epsilon(k n^{-1+\epsilon})=o(1)\) uniformly for \(n>k^{41/40}\) on taking \(\epsilon<1/41\).
This completes (4).

<a id="degree"></a>
## Applying the weights to the integral Euler exponents

Let \(p_j\) be the power sums of \(P\).
Its reversed polynomial, normalized with constant one, has formal exponents \(c_l\) through degree \(k\), where \(l c_l=\sum_{e\mid l}\mu(l/e)p_e\).
A comparator with the same prefix has exactly these exponents even when its total degree differs.
For a product with \(b_n\) copies of \(\Phi_n\), the identity for its normalized reciprocal polynomial gives \(c_l=\sum_{n:\,l\mid n} b_n\mu(n/l)\) for \(l\le k\).
This includes the normalized factor \(1-X\) at order one.
The nonnegative multiplicities and (4) show that every such comparator of degree \(D\) satisfies
\[
                    \sum_{l\le k}w_l c_l\le(1+o(1))D.               \tag{6}
\]
The error is independent of its conductor, multiplicities and degree.

The [reflected moment estimate](Yin_C_G0345_v138.md#cost) gives \(|p_j-e^{aj}|\le 1+C_A\sqrt{j d\log(2d)}\) for \(1\le j\le d\).
Indeed the reflected sum replaces just \(e^{aj}\) by \(e^{-aj}\).
The [positive measure degree bound](../audits/literature/Smy08/Yin_U001.md#smy08-u001) gives \(a\gg_A(\log(2d))^{-3}\), so in the range of (1) we have \(k\ll_{A,B}(\log(2d))^4\).
Every proper divisor of \(l\) is at most \(l/2\).
Consequently for all \(1\le l\le k\) one has
\[
      |l c_l-e^{al}|\le
            O_A\!\left(k(1+\sqrt{k d\log(2d)}+e^{ak/2})\right).    \tag{7}
\]
Multiplying by \(w_l/l\in[0,1]\) and summing costs \(o(e^{ak})\) uniformly, since \(e^{ak}\ge d^{1/2+\delta}\) and \(k\) is polylogarithmic.
All the exponential terms are positive, and on \(J\) the weights \(w_j/j\) equal one.
Thus the left side of (6) is at least
\[
          \sum_{j\in J}e^{aj}-o(e^{ak})
             =\frac{e^{ak}}{1-e^{-a}}(1-e^{-aH})-o(e^{ak}).
\]
Here \(aH\gg_{A,B,\delta}\log d/k^{1/8}\gg_{A,B,\delta}\sqrt{\log d}\).
This proves the lower bound in (1).

For the upper bound use the [signed exponent construction and its degree inequality (5)](Yin_C_G0345_v138.md#packets) at the given \(k\).
The top indices cost at most \(e^{ak}/(1-e^{-a})+O_A(k(1+\sqrt{k d\log(2d)}))\) by the same moment estimate.
The small-index term in that inequality is \(O_A(k(1+\log k)^2(1+\sqrt{k d\log(2d)}+e^{ak/2}))\).
Both errors are \(o(e^{ak})\) throughout the stated range.
If necessary one can pad a constructed polynomial of degree less than \(k\) with \(X^{k+1}-1\), which preserves its matched reversal through degree \(k\) at additive cost \(k+1=o(e^{ak})\).
This proves (1).

<a id="rounding"></a>
## Returning to the exact original degree

The [original-degree construction and real moment upper bound](Yin_C_G0345_v138.md#statement) give \(aK(P)\sim\log d\) uniformly for \(a\le A\), including the floor error at bounded \(a\).
For example, both \(\log(1-e^{-a})\) and \(\log(1-e^{-2a})\) are \(O_A(1+\log\log(2d))\) in absolute value by the positive measure degree bound.
Hence \(k=K(P)\) is in the range of (1) with \(\delta=1/4,\ B=2\) eventually.
As its minimum degree is at most \(d\), (1) implies \(aK(P)\le t_d+\eta_d\) with a uniform nonnegative \(\eta_d=o_A(1)\).
The original-degree construction supplies the lower floor in (2) without a fixed degree-padding assumption for the optimizer in (1).
Finally, \(1-e^{-a}\sim a\) and a floor costs at most \(a=o(1)\) on a shrinking sequence, giving (3).
