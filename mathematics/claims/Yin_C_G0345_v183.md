# The unrestricted Lehmer problem on units without short integer relations

<a id="statement"></a>
## Escaping every fixed coefficient length

For a nonzero algebraic number \(\beta\), let \(\lambda(\beta)\) be the least coefficient length \(\sum_i |c_i|\) of a nonzero integer polynomial \(\sum_i c_iX^i\) vanishing at \(\beta\).
Write \(m(\beta)\) for the logarithmic Mahler measure of its primitive minimal polynomial.
Call \(\beta\) power-stable if all its positive powers have its degree over \(\mathbb Q\).

The unrestricted Lehmer gap fails if and only if there is a sequence of power-stable algebraic units \(\gamma\) with \(m(\gamma)>0\) tending to zero and \(\lambda(\gamma)\) tending to infinity.
One may in fact require
\[
            \lambda(\gamma)\gg
          \frac{\log\log(1/m(\gamma))}
               {\log\log\log(1/m(\gamma))}                           \tag{1}
\]
for sufficiently small measure, with an absolute implied constant.
All positive powers of each such unit have coefficient length at least \(\lambda(\gamma)\) in this same minimum-over-multiples sense.
The equivalence also holds within the Salem numbers if the initial sequence consists of Salem numbers.

More precisely, for every fixed integer \(L\ge2\) and every power-stable nontorsion unit \(\beta\) of sufficiently small \(m=m(\beta)>0\), there is a prime
\[
                    p\ll_L 1+\log(1/m)
\]
such that no power \(\beta^{p h}\), \(h\ge1\), is the root of any nonzero integer polynomial of coefficient length at most \(L\).
Its first power in this progression has measure \(m(\beta^p)=p m\).
The theorem gives a restriction of the original problem equivalent to the full question and leaves its truth unsettled.

<a id="relations"></a>
## The exponents of all powers satisfying a short relation

Fix a nontorsion algebraic number \(\beta\).
A relation polynomial at \(\beta\) is nondegenerate here if none of its nonempty proper subsets of whole nonzero monomials sums to zero at \(\beta\).
Normalize it by dividing out its smallest power of \(X\) and choosing the leading coefficient positive.
For each fixed \(L\) the number of nondegenerate normalized relation polynomials of coefficient length at most \(L\) is at most
\[
                 B_L=L(2L+1)^L\exp\!\big(L(6L)^{3L}\big),          \tag{2}
\]
uniformly in \(\beta\) and its degree.
Indeed, order the \(s\le L\) terms by increasing exponent \(0=e_0<e_1<\cdots<e_{s-1}\) and fix their nonzero coefficients \(c_0,\ldots,c_{s-1}\).
A single term cannot vanish.
Rewrite the relation as \(\sum_{j=1}^{s-1}(-c_j/c_0)\beta^{e_j}=1\).
Its tuple belongs to \(\langle\beta\rangle^{s-1}\), a multiplicative group of rank \(s-1\), and no subsum on the left vanishes.
The [theorem of Evertse, Schlickewei and Schmidt](../audits/literature/ESS02/Yin_U001.md#ess-exact-counting-bound) bounds the number of these tuples by \(\exp(s(6(s-1))^{3(s-1)})\).
For \(s=2\) uniqueness of the single variable also suffices.
The tuple recovers its exponents because \(\beta\) is nontorsion, and \(L(2L+1)^L\) bounds all coefficient and length choices.
This is the same direct-product rank used in the [count of normalized signed power relations](Yin_C_G0276_v1.md#statement).

For each polynomial in this finite list let \(g_i\) be the gcd of its positive support exponents.
Then, exactly,
\[
       \{k\ge1:\lambda(\beta^k)\le L\}
                        =\bigcup_i\{k\ge1:k\mid g_i\}.               \tag{3}
\]
In one direction divide the exponents of that polynomial by \(k\mid g_i\).
In the other direction, from a length-\(L\) or shorter relation at \(\beta^k\) choose a minimal vanishing subset, divide its polynomial by its lowest power of \(X\), choose the leading sign positive and substitute \(X^k\).
The resulting normalized relation at \(\beta\) belongs to the list and every positive exponent in it is divisible by \(k\).
This proof covers degenerate input relations and an empty list.

<a id="prime"></a>
## A small prime outside all the exponent divisors

For a power-stable unit \(\beta\), the height identity \(m(\beta^k)=k m(\beta)\) holds because \(h(\beta^k)=k h(\beta)\) and the degree does not drop.
Each \(\beta^{g_i}\) is a root of an integer polynomial of length at most \(L\).
Its minimal polynomial divides this polynomial over the integers.
Additivity and nonnegativity of logarithmic Mahler measure and the circle bound for a polynomial by its coefficient length therefore give
\[
                  g_i m\le\log L .
\]
For \(J=\prod_i g_i\), with the empty product equal to one, it follows that
\[
                  \log J\le B_L\log^+((\log L)/m).
\]
There is a prime \(p\nmid J\) with \(p\le C(1+\log J)\) for an absolute \(C\).
For completeness, \(\binom{n}{j}\) divides \(\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,n)\), since each power of a given prime contributes at most one to its valuation in the binomial coefficient.
The largest of these coefficients is at least \(2^n/(n+1)\).
Passing from this lcm to \(\prod_{\ell\le n,\ \ell\ {\rm prime}}\ell\) costs \(O(\sqrt n(\log(2n))^2)\) in the logarithm, by summing the contributions of powers of exponent at least two.
The prime product thus has logarithm at least \(c n\) for all large \(n\), which proves the assertion.
No multiple of \(p\) divides any \(g_i\), proving the fixed-\(L\) bound by (3).
More generally \(\lambda(z)\le\lambda(z^h)\) follows immediately by substitution in any relation at \(z^h\).

<a id="sequence"></a>
## Preserving the unrestricted failure and choosing the length

If the unrestricted gap fails, take primitive irreducible factors with positive measure tending to zero from a sequence of integral polynomials witnessing failure.
Such factors are eventually units by the bounds from the two endpoints.
The [ordinary norm descent](Yin_C_G0231_v1.md#statement) supplies power-stable nontorsion units with measure no larger.
Indeed, if \(K=\mathbb Q(\alpha)\) properly contains \(F=\mathbb Q(\alpha^n)\), let \(e=[K:F]\) and \(z=\operatorname{Norm}_{K/F}\alpha\).
Then \(z\) is a unit, \(z^n=(\alpha^n)^e\), and \(h(z)=e h(\alpha)>0\).
Its degree is at most \([F:\mathbb Q]<[K:\mathbb Q]\), whence \(0<m(z)\le m(\alpha)\).
Iterating terminates by degree.
For a Salem number \(\tau\) this descent is unnecessary: a conjugate with the same \(n\)-th power as \(\tau\) must have modulus \(\tau>1\), and the defining Salem signature leaves only \(\tau\) itself.
Thus \(\mathbb Q(\tau^n)=\mathbb Q(\tau)\), and the conjugate moduli show that every positive power is still Salem.

For the resulting power-stable unit put \(T=\log(1/m)\) and take \(T\) sufficiently large.
There is an absolute \(C_0\) with \(\log B_L\le\exp(C_0 L\log(2L))\) for all \(L\ge2\), by (2).
For a sufficiently small absolute \(c_0>0\), set \(L=\lfloor c_0\log T/\log\log T\rfloor\).
Then \(L\ge2\) eventually and \(\log B_L\le T^{1/4}\).
The prime just constructed satisfies
\[
         p\le C\big(1+B_L\log^+((\log L)/m)\big)
              \le \exp(T/2)
\]
for all large \(T\).
Taking \(\gamma=\beta^p\) yields \(m\le m(\gamma)\le\sqrt m\) and \(\lambda(\gamma)>L\).
Every positive power of \(\gamma\) still has the same degree.
Since \(\log(1/m(\gamma))\) lies between \(T/2\) and \(T\), this proves (1) along the constructed sequence.
Conversely, such a sequence itself violates the unrestricted gap, or the Salem gap in the Salem case.
