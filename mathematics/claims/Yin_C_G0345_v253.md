# Factor degrees and Galois closures along a fixed plane curve

<a id="statement"></a>
## A degree criterion for small Mahler measure

Let \(F\in\mathbb Z[U^{\pm1},V^{\pm1}]\) be fixed, irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\) in the Laurent ring, with support differences of rank two.
For a primitive pair \((p,q)\in\mathbb Z^2\) put \(N=\max(|p|,|q|)\), and let \(a\ge1\) be any integer.
For every nontorsion algebraic root \(\alpha\) of \(F(X^{ap},X^{aq})\), put \(d=[\mathbb Q(\alpha):\mathbb Q]\), \(m(\alpha)=d h(\alpha)\), with \(h\) the absolute logarithmic height.
Then
\[
               h(\alpha)\asymp_F\frac1{aN},\qquad
               m(\alpha)\asymp_F\frac d{aN}.                            \tag{1}
\]
With \(\lambda(N)=(\log\log y/\log y)^3,\ y=\max(16,N)\), one has
\[
                              d\gg_F aN\lambda(N).                       \tag{2}
\]
Writing \(D_{\rm ab}(\alpha)\) for the degree of the normal closure of \(\mathbb Q^{\rm ab}(\alpha)/\mathbb Q^{\rm ab}\), one also has, for sufficiently large \(N\),
\[
                 \log D_{\rm ab}(\alpha)
                      \gg_F \log N\,(\log\log N)^{1/3}.                 \tag{3}
\]
These are estimates for every individual nontorsion factor, including when the specialization is reducible and the original curve is only rationally irreducible.
In particular the five-term parent in [C345v251](Yin_C_G0345_v251.md#five) has (3) for every irreducible factor, uniformly in its admissible offset and with \(N=n\).

For any fixed nonzero rational Laurent polynomial in two variables and its nonzero specializations, the existence of a sequence of positive factor measures tending to zero is equivalent to the following degree condition.
On a single rational irreducible component with support differences of rank two there exist primitive directions with \(N\to\infty\) and actual nontorsion rational irreducible factors of degree \(o(N)\).
Each such sequence has degrees \(\gg_F N\lambda(N)\), and the normal closures of its root fields satisfy (3).
This equivalence leaves open the existence of those factors.

<a id="cuts"></a>
## Proper subsums at primitive directions

Normalize \(F\) by a monomial to an ordinary polynomial of total degree \(D\), divisible by neither coordinate.
For each nonempty proper subset of its support let \(F_0\) be the corresponding sum.
Since \(\deg F_0\le D\) and \(F_0\) is not a scalar multiple of \(F\), rational irreducibility gives coprimality in \(\mathbb Q[U,V]\).
They also have no common absolute component, since the product of the conjugates of such a component, with normalized leading coefficients, would be a common rational divisor.
By Bézout there are at most \(D^2\) common points for this subset.

At a nontorsion point of the form \((u,w)=(\beta^p,\beta^q)\) with \(\beta\) nontorsion and \((p,q)\) primitive, the kernel of \((i,j)\mapsto u^i w^j\) on \(\mathbb Z^2\) is the line generated by \((q,-p)\).
Thus this point determines at most two primitive directions.
Only finitely many primitive directions have a nontorsion root where any proper subsum vanishes.
Two distinct exponents have the same projection in at most two primitive directions, and there are only finitely many exponent pairs.
No specialization of \(F\) along a nonzero direction vanishes identically, since its primitive monomial image has a binomial defining equation and \(F\) is rationally irreducible with support differences of rank two.

Outside these finite exceptions the support projects to distinct terms of width \(W\asymp_F N\).
Indeed two linearly independent support differences bound \(N\) above by a fixed multiple of \(W\), and every projected difference has absolute value \(O_F(N)\).
Normalize the resulting Laurent polynomial by a monomial and apply the [classical gap estimate](Yin_C_G0345_v250.md#cuts) at each consecutive cut.
For completeness, the estimate also holds for a root that is not a unit.
At each place \(v\) of \(\mathbb Q(\beta)\), bound the nonzero initial sum using its own terms when \(|\beta|_v\ge1\) and the opposite terms when \(|\beta|_v<1\).
For a cut between exponents \(i<j\) this gives \(L_v|\beta|_v^i\) and \(L_v|\beta|_v^j\) respectively, where \(L_v\) is the coefficient length \(L\) at infinity and one at finite places.
The product formula at the value of the initial sum gives \((j-i)h(\beta)\le\log L\).
Summing over the cuts gives \(W h(\beta)\ll_F1\).
For a nontorsion unit the [width lower bound](Yin_C_G0345_v186.md#statement) gives \(W h(\beta)\gg_F1\).
For a nonunit its primitive integral minimal polynomial has absolute leading or constant coefficient at least two, so \(m(\beta)\ge\log2\) and its degree is at most \(W\).
This gives the lower estimate for every nontorsion root.
The finitely many omitted primitive directions have finitely many nontorsion roots of positive height and can be included by adjusting the constants.
Taking \(\beta=\alpha^a\) gives (1).

<a id="twists"></a>
## Twists between different absolute components

There is an integer \(B_F\ge1\) such that for every sufficiently large primitive direction, any two nontorsion roots of \(F(X^p,X^q)\) with a torsion quotient have equal \(B_F\)-th powers.
To see this, factor \(F\) over a fixed number field \(K\) into absolutely irreducible polynomials \(F_i\), normalized up to scalar and divisible by neither coordinate.
Their supports agree because the factors are conjugate after fixing a leading coefficient.
Their support differences have rank two, since otherwise the supports of their product would also lie on a line.
Clear fixed denominators so their coefficients are algebraic integers.

If \(\beta,\zeta\beta\) both belong to the specialization for a root of unity \(\zeta\), the point \((u,w)=(\beta^p,\beta^q)\) belongs to some \(F_i(U,V)=0\) and \(F_j(\zeta^p U,\zeta^q V)=0\).
If the two polynomials are not proportional, the degree over \(K(\zeta)\) of this pair is bounded by \(D^2\) by Bézout.
Bézout's identity in the exponents recovers \(\beta\) from the pair.
Thus \([\mathbb Q(\zeta,\beta):\mathbb Q(\zeta)]\le [K:\mathbb Q]D^2\), and the [relative abelian height bound](../audits/literature/AD07/Yin_U001.md#rational-base) gives \(h(\beta)\gg_F1\).
By (1) at \(a=1\) this bounds \(N\).
If the two polynomials are proportional, their diagonal translations \((s,t)\) form a finite set for the fixed pair \(i,j\).
Coefficient comparison determines \(s^{e_1}t^{e_2}\) for each support difference \(e\); fixing two independent differences gives finitely many solutions in the torus.
Take \(B_F\) to be a common multiple of the orders of all torsion coordinates in these finitely many translation pairs for all \(i,j\).
Both \(\zeta^p\) and \(\zeta^q\) then have order dividing \(B_F\), so \(\zeta^{B_F}=1\).
This proves the assertion, including for distinct absolute components, extending the [single-component calculation](Yin_C_G0345_v251.md#curve).

Put \(\gamma=\beta^{B_F}\).
If two conjugates of \(\gamma\) have equal \(j\)-th powers for some positive integer \(j\), lift them to conjugates of \(\beta\).
Their quotient before taking \(B_F\)-th powers is torsion, so the conjugates of \(\gamma\) were already equal.
Separability gives \(\mathbb Q(\gamma)=\mathbb Q(\gamma^j)\) for every \(j\), also after adjoining \(\mathbb Q^{\rm ab}\).

<a id="galois"></a>
## The normal closure over the abelian field

Let \(e(\gamma)\) be the exponent of the torsion subgroup of the multiplicative group generated by all rational conjugates of \(\gamma\).
The all-powers field equality just proved supplies the hypothesis of [Amoroso and David, Proposition 2.4](../audits/literature/AD22/Yin_U001.md#power-field) at this exponent, regardless of its size.
Writing \(D_0=D_{\rm ab}(\gamma)\), their bound gives
\[
             \log(1/h(\gamma))\le C\frac{\log(16D_0)}
                                                {(\log\log(16D_0))^{1/3}}
\]
with an absolute \(C\).
By (1), \(h(\gamma)=B_F h(\beta)\ll_F1/N\).
It follows first that \(\log(16D_0)\gg_F \log N\), hence that \(\log\log(16D_0)\gg_F\log\log N\).
Substitution gives \(\log D_0\gg_F\log N(\log\log N)^{1/3}\) for large \(N\).
Since \(\gamma=\alpha^{aB_F}\), the normal closure of \(\mathbb Q^{\rm ab}(\alpha)\) contains the normal closure of \(\mathbb Q^{\rm ab}(\gamma)\), which proves (3).
The source proves the height inequality for the root field; the geometric component and torsion checks here allow its use on every nontorsion factor of the specialization.

<a id="dilation"></a>
## Common dilation and components supported on a line

At the primitive root \(\beta=\alpha^a\), [Voutier's estimate and its bounded-degree normalization](Yin_C_G0345_v250.md#cuts) give \(m(\beta)\gg_F\lambda(N)\) since \(\deg\beta\ll_F N\).
For nonunits the bound \(m(\beta)\ge\log2\) already suffices.
Every primitive specialization after a monomial shift has integer coefficient length at most \(L=\sum|\operatorname{coeff}(F)|\).
The [bounded denominator for radicals](Yin_C_G0345_v210.md#statement) gives \(m(\beta)/R_L\le m(\alpha)\le m(\beta)\) for a constant \(R_L\) depending only on \(L\).
Combining with (1) proves (2), with the logarithmic loss depending on the primitive direction alone.

For an arbitrary fixed rational Laurent polynomial, clear denominators and factor over \(\mathbb Q\).
There are finitely many rational irreducible factors up to monomials.
A rank-one factor has the form of a monomial times \(H(U^iV^j)\) with \(H\) a fixed ordinary integral polynomial and \((i,j)\) primitive.
At a nonzero specialization, a nontorsion root coming from this factor satisfies \(\alpha^{|a(ip+jq)|}=\delta\) for a nontorsion root of \(H\) or its reversal and a nonzero exponent.
The same radical inequality gives a uniform positive measure for all such roots, since the possible \(\delta\)'s form a finite set.
A monomial has no root in the torus.
Thus a shrinking sequence must, on a subsequence, come from one rank-two component, and the radical estimate forces the measures of its primitive roots to shrink as well.
Bounded primitive directions produce only finitely many such roots.
By (1) the degrees of the primitive roots are \(o(N)\).
Conversely actual nontorsion factors with degrees \(o(N)\) on that component have measures tending to zero by (1), and divide the corresponding nonzero specializations of the original polynomial except possibly at finitely many primitive directions where another component vanishes identically.
A component vanishing identically has a binomial subtorus as a factor and can account for at most two primitive directions.
This proves the equivalence.
