# All length-six multiples of a small Salem number and of its powers

<a id="statement"></a>
## Uniqueness of the multiple and the possible powers

There is an absolute \(t_0>0\) with the following property.
Let \(\tau=e^t\) be a Salem number with \(0<t<t_0\) whose minimal polynomial has a nonzero integral multiple of coefficient length at most six.
Normalize each such multiple by removing its common monomial and choosing its leading coefficient positive.
The set of normalized multiples consists of either one polynomial or precisely two.
They have length six and the form
\[
 P=X^NQ+\sigma Q^*,\qquad Q=X^a-X^b-1,\qquad
          0\le b<a<N,\qquad \sigma\in\{-1,1\},                     \tag{1}
\]
where \(Q^*(X)=X^aQ(1/X)\).
All have the same \(N\), the same \(\sigma\), and the same monic irreducible factor \(q\) of \(Q\) containing its positive root greater than one.
Two normalized multiples occur exactly when one of their seeds belongs to the pair
\[
 Q_1=X^{3r}-X^r-1,\qquad
 Q_2=X^{5r}-X^{4r}-1=(X^{2r}-X^r+1)Q_1,\qquad r\ge1.             \tag{2}
\]
In this case the second multiple is the first times \(X^{2r}-X^r+1\), ordering them by their respective seeds in (2).

Put \(G=\gcd(N,a,b)\), with \(\gcd(N,a,0)=\gcd(N,a)\).
This integer is independent of the normalized multiple chosen.
For **every** integer \(k\ge1\),
\[
 \tau^k\text{ is a root of a nonzero integral polynomial of length at most six}
                        \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k\mid G.       \tag{3}
\]
The smallness condition is imposed only on the original \(\tau\).
A representation with \(G=1\) therefore precludes such a representation for every proper positive power.
The result describes the multiples and powers of a putative small Salem number without asserting that such numbers exist.

<a id="factor"></a>
## The irreducible factor of the endpoint

For \(Q=X^a-X^b-1\) let \(C\) be the monic gcd of \(Q,Q^*\), and \(q=Q/C\).
The [common-root calculation](Yin_C_G0345_v176.md#elimination) gives squarefreeness of \(Q\) and shows that \(C\) is a product of cyclotomic polynomials.
Since \(Q(1)=-1\), the factor \(X-1\) is absent, hence \(C^*=C\).
At \(b=0\), \(Q=X^a-2\) is irreducible by Eisenstein at two and \(C=1\).

For \(0<b<a\), the assertion that \(q\) is irreducible is the trinomial corollary of Ljunggren's theorem.
We give the reversal argument here for these signs, including the coincident differences. [Ljunggren, pp. 65–66](../audits/literature/Lju60/Yin_U001.md#lju60-u001)

Suppose \(Q=UV\) with monic integral factors and write \(J=\pm UV^*\), choosing the sign to make \(J\) monic.
This is possible since both constant coefficients are units.
For \(T_F=F(X)F(X^{-1})\) we have \(T_J=T_Q\).
Its constant coefficient is three, so \(J\) has exactly three nonzero coefficients, each of absolute value one.
The coefficient at exponent \(a\) is \(-1\), so \(J=X^a+sX^j-1\) with \(s\in\{1,-1\}\), \(0<j<a\).
Writing \(C_\ell=X^\ell+X^{-\ell}\) gives
\[
 T_J=3-C_a+s(C_{a-j}-C_j),\qquad T_Q=3-C_a-C_{a-b}+C_b .
\]
If \(2b\ne a\), the two distinct intermediate positive exponents with their signs force \((j,s)=(b,-1)\) or \((a-b,1)\).
If \(2b=a\), the missing intermediate coefficients force \(2j=a\), with either sign.
In both cases \(J=Q\) or \(J=-Q^*\).
Cancelling the common nonzero factor shows that \(V=\pm V^*\) or \(U=\pm U^*\), respectively.
Now if \(q=AB\) with both \(A,B\) nonconstant monic, apply this observation to \(Q=(CA)B\).
Either \(B\) is reciprocal up to sign or \(CA\) is, in which case so is \(A\) since \(C^*=C\).
Either conclusion puts a reciprocal factor in \(q\), contradicting \(\gcd(q,q^*)=1\).
Thus \(q\) is irreducible, and it contains the unique positive root \(\rho>1\) of \(Q\), since \(Q(1)<0\) and \(Q'(x)\ge a-b\ge1\) on \(x\ge1\).

<a id="separation"></a>
## The Salem root determines the reduced seed and the shift

Apply the [length-six reduction](Yin_C_G0345_v174.md#statement) to every normalized multiple of the same \(\tau\), writing \(d=[\mathbb Q(\tau):\mathbb Q]\).
It gives (1), \(g=N-a\) with \(gt\gg d\), and \(|Q(\tau)|\le3e^{-gt}\), uniformly in the multiple.
The [degree-dependent bound on the seed width](Yin_C_G0345_v176.md#real) gives \(a\ll(\log(2d))^4\), again uniformly.
By the derivative bound on \(Q\), \(|\rho-\tau|\le |Q(\tau)|\le3e^{-c d}\) for an absolute \(c>0\).

Suppose two monic reduced seeds \(q_1,q_2\) were distinct.
They are irreducible and their resultant is a nonzero integer.
All their roots have modulus at most three by the elementary coefficient root bound on the original seeds.
In the product formula for the resultant, one factor is the difference of their positive roots \(\rho_1,\rho_2\), and every other root difference has modulus at most six.
Writing \(a_i=\deg Q_i\) gives
\[
             1\le|\operatorname{Res}(q_1,q_2)|
                  \le 6^{a_1a_2}|\rho_1-\rho_2|
                  \le 6^{a_1a_2+1} e^{-cd}.
\]
Since \(a_1a_2\ll(\log(2d))^8\), this is impossible for all sufficiently large \(d\).
The uniform estimate \(d\ge1/(20t^2)\) from the length-six reduction enforces this by decreasing \(t_0\).
Consequently a single monic reduced seed \(q\) works for every multiple.

The normalized polynomial in (1) equals \(C(X)(X^N q+\sigma q^*)\).
Here \(C(\tau)\ne0\) and \(q(\tau)\ne0\), since otherwise the displayed equation would also force \(q^*(\tau)=0\).
Thus \(\tau^N=-\sigma q^*(\tau)/q(\tau)>0\).
Its absolute value uniquely determines \(N\) because \(\tau>1\), and the sign determines \(\sigma\).

<a id="seeds"></a>
## The two possible trinomials with the same reduced factor

All seeds now have the same rational function \(Q^*/Q=q^*/q\).
At zero this has value \(-1/2\) for \(X^a-2\) and \(-1\) when \(b>0\).
The binomial case is isolated, since there \(Q=q\).
For a trinomial put \(c'=a-b>0\).
One has
\[
                   1+Q^*/Q=\frac{X^b+X^{c'}}{1+X^b-X^{b+c'}}.     \tag{4}
\]
The lowest exponent in its expansion at zero is \(r=\min(b,c')\), and its coefficient is two exactly when \(b=c'=r\).
This settles equal exponents and determines \(r\) in every other case.

Fixing a real \(0<X<1\), write \(z=X^r\) and \(y=X^{\max(b,c')}\).
If \(b=r\), the expression in (4) is \((z+y)/(1+z-zy)\), which increases strictly with \(y\).
If \(c'=r\), it is \((z+y)/(1+y-zy)\), whose derivative in \(y\) has positive numerator \(1-z+z^2\).
Thus two representations with \(r\) in the same position must have identical exponents.
For opposite positions compare \((b,c')=(r,s)\) and \((u,r)\) with \(s,u>r\).
Set \(z=X^r\), \(y=X^s\), \(w=X^u\) as monomials.
Equality in (4) simplifies to
\[
                    y(1+w+z^2)=w(1+z^2)+z^2 .
\]
The lowest exponent gives \(s=\min(u,2r)\), and \(u=2r\) is impossible because its lowest coefficient on the right would be two.
If \(u<2r\), then \(y=w\) and the equality gives \(w^2=z^2\), contrary to \(u>r\).
If \(u>2r\), then \(y=z^2\) gives \(w=z^4\).
The only ambiguity is therefore \((b,c')=(r,2r)\) and \((4r,r)\), precisely (2).

Conversely, the multiplier in (2) is reciprocal and has no positive real zero.
Hence a given \(\tau\) is a root of the parent for \(Q_1\) if and only if it is a root of the parent for \(Q_2\) with the same \(N,\sigma\).
Both are normalized and have length six, since \(N>5r\) for all sufficiently small \(t\).
Indeed the larger seed already has \(N>5r\) in its own representation, and for a representation with the smaller seed \(N\ge g\gg d/t\) whereas \(3r=a\ll\log(2/t)/t\).
This proves the exact number of normalized multiples.

<a id="powers"></a>
## Exponents of represented powers

For (1), the gcd of its support exponents is \(G=\gcd(N,a,b)\).
If \(b>0\), the separated blocks have exponents \(0,a-b,a,N,N+b,N+a\), giving the stated gcd.
At \(b=0\) the four exponents are \(0,a,N,N+a\).
In the exceptional pair (2), both gcds are \(\gcd(N,r)\).
Thus every normalized multiple of \(\tau\) has the same support gcd.

For \(k\mid G\) write a normalized multiple as \(P(X)=R(X^k)\).
Then \(R(\tau^k)=0\) and its integral coefficients have the same length.
Conversely, if any nonzero integral \(R\) of length at most six vanishes at \(\tau^k\), remove its common monomial and normalize its sign.
The polynomial \(R(X^k)\) is now a normalized multiple of \(\tau\) and its support gcd is divisible by \(k\).
This gives \(k\mid G\) and proves (3) without any restriction on \(k t\).
