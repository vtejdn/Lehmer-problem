# Counting reciprocal-unit relations with two unspecified surviving exponents

<a id="statement"></a>
## Completion counts from a common coefficient divisor

Let \(\alpha\) be a reciprocal nontorsion algebraic unit with minimal polynomial \(P\), degree \(d\) and \(m=\log M(P)\).
Fix an ordered tuple of \(r\ge5\) nonzero integer coefficients.
Consider zero relations with these coefficients at distinct integer exponents in this order and with full span at most the positive integer \(W\).
Delete two labeled terms whose coefficients are divisible by a positive integer \(Q\).
Choose two other labels with coefficients \(c,e\), listed in this order, and fix the positions of the other \(r-4\) labels up to translation.
Normalize the smallest fixed position to zero.
We count the possible full relations up to translation for this choice of fixed positions.

Write \(i<j\) for the two unspecified surviving positions and \(S=|c|+|e|\).
Let \(W'\) be the span of an integer interval containing every candidate \(i,j\) for the fixed positions.
One may always use \(W'=2W\), or \(W'=W\) if both labels lie on the same side of zero.
If there are at least \(n\ge2\) distinct completions, then
\[
              \log Q\le\log S+\frac{mW'}d+\frac{\log n}{n-1}.        \tag{1}
\]
Thus if \(Q>S e^{mW'/d}\), the count is bounded by the largest integer \(n\ge2\) satisfying (1), or by one when there is none.
In particular \(Q>2S e^{mW'/d}\) gives at most one completion.

There is also a bound on the number of different positive gaps \(j-i\).
Put \(Q'=Q/\gcd(Q,|ce|)\).
If at least \(n\ge2\) different gaps occur, then
\[
          \log Q'\le\frac{2mW}{d}+\frac{\log n}{n-1}
                                     +\frac{2\log2}{n}.              \tag{2}
\]
These two estimates use the full composite divisor and permit repeated factors of \(P\) modulo its primes.

A second count uses finitely many specified residue roots at primes \(p\mid Q\) with \(p\nmid ce\).
For the selected roots let \(M\) be the least common multiple of their multiplicative orders.
After the other positions are fixed, the [quadratic test](#local) classifies each selected root as ordinary or exceptional.
Let \(M_0\) be the least common multiple of the ordinary orders and \(k\) the number of ordinary tests, using one as the empty least common multiple.
Then there are at most
\[
     2^k\left(1+\left\lfloor\frac{W-1}{M_0}\right\rfloor\right)
         \left(1+\left\lfloor\frac{W-1}{M}\right\rfloor\right)       \tag{3}
\]
completions.
Under either of the following sufficient conditions one can choose tests so that the number of full relations up to translation and with this fixed order is at most
\[
                          W^{o(1)}\binom{W}{r-5}                     \tag{4}
\]
as \(W\to\infty\), with the subpower factor uniform in all the other inputs.

* Among roots at primes also satisfying \(c^2\not\equiv e^2\bmod p\), the least common multiple of the orders exceeds \(W\).
* Among all eligible roots, the least common multiple of the orders exceeds \(2W^2\).

A single eligible root of order exceeding \(2W\) already gives the factor two from the [single-root count](../ledger/Yin_L_G0462_v318.md#count).
The hypotheses permit choosing roots from different primes or several Frobenius orbits at one prime.
Availability of the common divisor or of sufficiently large joint orders in the actual [terminal witness range](Yin_C_G0345_v595.md#statement) remains open.

<a id="vandermonde"></a>
## Distinct two-term sums in one congruence class

Let \(\mathcal O=\mathbb Z[\alpha]=\mathbb Z[\alpha,\alpha^{-1}]\) and write \(\iota(\alpha)=\alpha^{-1}\) for inversion.
Any two-term sum at distinct positions is nonzero, since a vanishing sum would give a positive power of \(\alpha\) in \(\mathbb Q\), fixed by inversion and hence equal to its inverse.
For fixed ordered coefficients, a common two-term sum determines its two exponents uniquely by the [reciprocal trace identity](../ledger/Yin_L_G0462_v313.md#recovery).
Consequently the values \(z=c\alpha^i+e\alpha^j\) distinguish all completions.
Indeed \(z\) determines \(i,j\), and the sum of the two deleted terms is then fixed, determining their positions in the specified order as well.
All \(z\) are congruent modulo \(Q\mathcal O\), since their residues equal the negative sum of the fixed surviving terms.

Translate \(i,j\) by a common integer so that all candidate positions lie in \([0,W']\).
This multiplies the sums by a fixed unit and preserves all their congruences.
For \(n\) distinct sums their nonzero Vandermonde product of pairwise differences belongs to \(Q^{\binom n2}\mathcal O\).
At an embedding sending \(\alpha\) to \(\beta\), every sum has absolute value at most \(S\max(1,|\beta|)^{W'}\).
For \(n\) points in a disk of radius \(R\) centered at zero the absolute Vandermonde is at most \(n^{n/2}R^{\binom n2}\).
This follows by scaling to the unit disk and applying Hadamard's inequality to the power matrix.
Multiplying over the \(d\) embeddings and comparing with the lower bound \(Q^{d\binom n2}\) on the nonzero absolute norm proves (1).

<a id="trace"></a>
## A coefficient-independent archimedean bound for the gaps

Since \(\iota\) preserves \(\mathcal O\), the values \(z\iota(z)=c^2+e^2+ce(\alpha^{j-i}+\alpha^{i-j})\) are congruent modulo \(Q\mathcal O\).
It follows that the traces \(y_u=\alpha^u+\alpha^{-u}\) for the positive realized gaps \(u\) are congruent modulo \(Q'\mathcal O\), by cancellation of the integer \(\gcd(Q,|ce|)\) and invertibility of the remaining coefficient modulo \(Q'\).
Distinct positive gaps have distinct traces since their powers of \(\alpha\) cannot be equal or inverse.

Form the Vandermonde on \(n\) such traces.
Replace its columns by the values of the monic polynomials of degrees \(h=1,\ldots,n-1\) characterized by \(C_h(x+x^{-1})=x^h+x^{-h}\), keeping the first column one.
These polynomials follow from \(C_1(Y)=Y,\ C_2(Y)=Y^2-2,\ C_{h+1}(Y)=YC_h(Y)-C_{h-1}(Y)\) for \(h\ge2\).
The determinant is unchanged by this triangular change of basis.
At the embedding \(\beta\), each positive-degree column has entries of modulus at most \(2\max(|\beta|,|\beta|^{-1})^{hW}\).
Hadamard's bound on the columns gives an absolute determinant at most \(n^{n/2}2^{n-1}\max(|\beta|,|\beta|^{-1})^{W\binom n2}\).
The unit condition gives \(\sum_\beta|\log|\beta||=2m\), and divisibility gives the lower bound \((Q')^{d\binom n2}\) on the nonzero absolute norm.
Taking logarithms proves (2).

<a id="local"></a>
## Combining reciprocal evaluations and exceptional differences

At each selected residue root \(\beta\) of order \(s\), reduce the coefficients into its finite field.
With \(U,V\) the negatives of the fixed surviving sums at \(\beta,\beta^{-1}\), put \(x=\beta^i,\ y=\beta^j\).
The [two reciprocal evaluations](../ledger/Yin_L_G0462_v318.md#residues) give \(cx+ey=U,\ c/x+e/y=V\).
They have at most two ordered solutions in powers of \(\beta\) unless \(U=V=0\) and \(c^2=e^2\) in the residue field.
Call these two types ordinary and exceptional, respectively, according to the test on the fixed data.
For an ordinary test, each solution determines \(i\) and \(j-i\) modulo \(s\).
For an exceptional test, \(y/x=-c/e=\pm1\), giving at most one residue class for \(j-i\) and the necessary divisibility \(s\mid 2(j-i)\).
The tests only use evaluations at roots, so a repeated root has the same meaning as a simple one.

There are at most \(2^k\) ways to select all ordinary solutions.
Compatible congruences fix \(i\bmod M_0\) and \(j-i\bmod M\), including all the exceptional difference residues.
The order relative to the fixed zero puts \(i\) in \([-W,-1]\) or in \([1,W]\), with the same choice for every completion.
Also \(1\le j-i\le W\), so counting representatives proves (3).
For every realized set of positions the least common multiple of the exceptional orders divides \(2(j-i)\le2W\), hence \(M\le 2W M_0\).
In particular \(M>2W^2\) gives \(M_0>W\) for every realized set, while \(M>W\) already suffices when all tests are ordinary.

<a id="selection"></a>
## How many residue roots the count needs

If some eligible order exceeds \(2W\), use that root alone.
No realized set can then be exceptional, and (3) gives at most two completions.
In the other cases every selected order is at most \(2W\).
From the collection in the relevant hypothesis choose an inclusion-minimal subcollection whose least common multiple \(M\) exceeds the threshold \(X=W\) or \(X=2W^2\).
Removing any member leaves least common multiple at most \(X\), so \(M\le2WX\).
Moreover each selected member is the unique contributor of the full power of at least one prime in \(M\), since otherwise removing it would preserve \(M\).
These primes are different for different members, so the number of tests is at most the number \(\omega(M)\) of distinct prime factors of \(M\).
The product of \(a\) distinct primes is at least \((a+1)!\), hence \(\omega(M)=O(\log M/\log\log M)\) for sufficiently large \(M\).
As \(W<M\le4W^3\), the factor \(2^k\le2^{\omega(M)}\) is \(W^{o(1)}\).
The other two factors in (3) are one for every realizable choice under the respective hypothesis.
After translation the \(r-5\) other fixed positions form a subset of \([1,W]\), with labels determined by the order.
This gives (4).
