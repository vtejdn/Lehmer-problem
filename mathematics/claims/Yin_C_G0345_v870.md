# Four-term equations on a reciprocal graph and its power pullbacks

<a id="statement"></a>
## Which four-term identities survive on the curve

Let \(R(U)=\gamma(U-\mu)/(U-\lambda)\), where \(\gamma,\mu,\lambda\in\mathbb C^\times\) and \(\mu\ne\lambda\), and put \(F(U,V)=(U-\lambda)V-\gamma(U-\mu)\).
For every positive integer \(g\), the only nonzero complex Laurent polynomials of at most four terms vanishing identically on \(F(X^g,Y^g)=0\) are scalar monomial multiples of \(F(X^g,Y^g)\).
For the [two-pole curve](Yin_C_G0345_v793.md#statement) in place of this graph, there are no such polynomials for any \(g\).
Both statements persist after multiplying either coordinate by a nonzero constant.
Thus inversion, multiplication and elimination using only the reciprocal graph identity cannot give a genuinely nonreciprocal four-term identity on that graph.
A further four-term equation at a section root imposes an independent point condition, quantified [below](#degree).
The [square-root degree and height estimates](Yin_C_G0345_v868.md#statement) assume a genuinely nonreciprocal rational parent already exists.

<a id="boundary"></a>
## Four extremal exponents on a curve

On a smooth projective complex curve let \(U,V\) be nonconstant rational functions whose zero and pole supports are disjoint from each other's.
In a Laurent identity \(\sum c_{ij}U^iV^j=0\) with distinct exponents and nonzero constant coefficients, each of the minimum and maximum first exponents occurs at least twice.
Indeed, at a zero of \(U\) a unique minimum would give a term of strictly smallest order, and a pole of \(U\) gives the maximum in the same way.
The two extrema of the second exponents likewise occur at least twice.
Neither coordinate of the support can be constant, since the other coordinate function is transcendental over the constants.
With at most four terms there must therefore be exactly four distinct corners of an axis-parallel rectangle.
After a monomial translation the identity becomes
\[
 A+B U^a+(C+D U^a)V^b=0,\qquad a,b\ge1,\quad ABCD\ne0.
\]
Here \(AD-BC\ne0\), since otherwise the left side factors into two nonzero functions of the separate coordinates.
Consequently \(V^b=M(U^a)\) for a degree-one rational function \(M\) with nonzero finite zero and pole.
In particular all \(U\)-values above \(V=0\) have the same \(a\)-th power.

On the graph, \(U\) is a coordinate on the projective line and \(V=R(U)\) has a simple zero at \(\mu\ne0,\infty\).
The zeros of \(M(U^a)\) are simple because its zero lies in \(\mathbb C^\times\) and the power map is unramified there.
Taking the order at \(\mu\) gives \(b=1\), and taking degrees gives \(a=1\).
The resulting bilinear relation is proportional to \(F\), since the numerator and denominator of the reduced fraction \(R\) are unique up to common scalar.
On the two-pole curve the [boundary calculation](Yin_C_G0345_v854.md#critical) gives disjoint supports and simple coordinate zeros.
Already the two values of \(U\) above \(V=0\) satisfying \(U-U^{-1}=-3\) have different absolute values, being real roots of \(U^2+3U-1\) with product \(-1\) and neither of absolute value one.
Their positive integral powers cannot agree.

<a id="pullback"></a>
## Independence of the residue classes of exponents

Write \(K=\mathbb C(U,V)\) for the function field of either curve.
Both curves are geometrically irreducible, with a simple zero of each coordinate away from the divisor of the other.
Adjoin \(X^g=U\) and then \(Y^g=V\).
The first polynomial is Eisenstein at a simple zero of \(U\).
At a simple zero of \(V\), the unit \(U\) has a \(g\)-th root in the completed local field by the binomial series for its principal unit part and algebraic closure of the constants.
The first extension thus embeds into that completion, where \(V\) still has order one and the restricted value group is \(\mathbb Z\).
The second polynomial is Eisenstein for this valuation.
The resulting field has degree \(g^2\), with basis \(X^iY^j\) for \(0\le i,j<g\).
The Laurent coordinate ring of the pullback is free on that same basis over the original coordinate ring by division by the two monic power equations, so it embeds in this field and the pullback is irreducible.
In any Laurent identity on the pullback, group the terms by their two exponents modulo \(g\).
The basis forces every occupied class to give a Laurent identity on the original curve after division by its representative monomial.
Distinct exponent pairs within the class stay distinct after dividing the remaining exponents by \(g\).
The preceding classification uses at least four terms for every occupied class on the graph and forbids even four on the two-pole curve.
This proves the assertions simultaneously for all dilations.

<a id="degree"></a>
## Degree cost of a second equation after specialization

Take either curve over a number field \(E\) and \(\xi,\eta\in E^\times\).
Fix coprime signed integers \(k,l\) and \(g\ge1\), and set \(P(T)=F(\xi T^{gk},\eta T^{gl})\).
Let \(Q(T)=\sum c_i T^{e_i}\) be nonzero with at most four distinct terms over \(E\).
On the graph assume \(Q\) is not a scalar monomial multiple of \(P\).
For any simultaneous nonzero algebraic root \(z\), and **any** integral choices \(ku_i+lv_i=e_i\), put \(A=\max u_i-\min u_i\), \(B=\max v_i-\min v_i\).
Then
\[
 [E(z):E]\le g(A+B)\quad\text{on the graph},\qquad
 [E(z):E]\le g(2A+4B)\quad\text{on the two-pole curve}.
\]
The same bound counts the total number of distinct simultaneous nonzero roots over \(\mathbb C\).
These assertions allow arbitrary secondary exponents, including different residues modulo \(g\).
For an untwisted rational reciprocal or antireciprocal parent the graph hypothesis on \(Q\) holds for every genuinely nonreciprocal rational \(Q\), with reciprocity tested after removing Laurent monomial factors.

Indeed \(G(X,Y)=\sum c_i X^{u_i}Y^{v_i}\) has distinct terms and specializes to \(Q\).
If it vanished identically on the translated pullback, the classification would force a scalar monomial multiple of the translated graph equation, hence the excluded univariate association, or contradict the two-pole conclusion.
Translate the exponent support of \(G\) into \([0,A]\times[0,B]\) by multiplying by a monomial.
On the smooth projective pullback its pole divisor is bounded by \(A(X)_\infty+B(Y)_\infty\).
From the degree \(g^2\) of the covering and the coordinate power equations one gets \(\deg X=g\deg U\) and \(\deg Y=g\deg V\).
The base degrees are \((1,1)\) and \((2,4)\) respectively, the latter from the simple boundary poles.
The nonzero function therefore has at most the stated number of distinct zeros, including at least one branch for every common torus point under consideration.
The map \(z\mapsto(z^k,z^l)\) is injective by coprimality, and all conjugates over \(E\) obey the same two equations.
This proves both counts and permits minimizing the bound over all lifts of the support.

<a id="unit"></a>
## A reciprocal unit admitting no rational tetranomial

Reciprocity and integrality alone cannot guarantee any second sparse equation, even with unrestricted span and coefficients.
For example \(S(T)=T^4-7T^3+13T^2-7T+1=T^2q(T+T^{-1})\), with \(q(t)=t^2-7t+11\), is irreducible and has four distinct positive real roots.
Both roots \((7\pm\sqrt5)/2\) of \(q\) exceed two.
For either root \(t\), the norm of \(t^2-4\) from \(\mathbb Q(t)\) is \(q(2)q(-2)=29\), so \(t^2-4\) is not a square in that quadratic field.
Adjoining a solution of \(T^2-tT+1=0\) therefore gives the asserted degree four and both solutions are positive.
A real Laurent polynomial with \(s\) nonzero terms has at most \(s-1\) distinct positive roots by induction, dividing by its lowest power before differentiating and applying Rolle's theorem.
Every rational annihilator of any root of \(S\) contains all four positive conjugates among its zeros and must have at least five terms.
This example concerns the algebraic hypotheses alone; the existence of a genuinely nonreciprocal tetranomial for an arbitrary hypothetical shrinking sequence remains open.
