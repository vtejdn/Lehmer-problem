# The derived sets of roots of short integral polynomials outside the circle

<a id="statement"></a>
## Exterior roots and the measures of factors

Let \(L\ge2\) be an integer and let \(Z_L\) be the set of distinct complex roots with \(|z|>1\) of all nonzero integral polynomials of coefficient length at most \(L\).
Here length means the sum of the absolute coefficients and a derived set consists of the limits of sequences of distinct points.
In the open exterior of the unit disk these sets are relatively closed and satisfy
\[
                          Z_{L+1}'=Z_L.                              \tag{1}
\]
The positive real roots satisfy the same identities in \((1,\infty)\).
For \(L\ge3\) the last nonempty relative derivative of \(Z_L\) is its derivative of order \(L-3\).

Let \(R_L=\{1\}\cup\{|z|:z\in Z_L\}\) and let \(R_L^{[h]}\) be the set of products of \(h\ge1\) elements of \(R_L\).
It is countable and compact, and its last nonempty derivative in \(\mathbb R\) has order \(h(L-2)\).
Every multiplicative Mahler measure of a monic integral factor with at most \(h\) exterior roots, counted with multiplicity, of a polynomial of length at most \(L\) belongs to \(R_L^{[h]}\).
For an arbitrary integral factor the enclosing set is \(\bigcup_{j=1}^L jR_L^{[h]}\).
Both sets omit a neighborhood of every positive rational whose reduced denominator has a prime factor greater than \(L\).
One can compute a rational open neighborhood from \(L,h\) and the rational point.
The derivative order is asserted for the enclosing product set, whose entries need not be conjugates of the same algebraic number.

In particular a putative bounded-length covering of a positive measure interval for units with uniformly bounded exterior count would already leave a positive-length open subinterval free of their measures.
This applies to the Salem class with \(h=1\).
It identifies a restriction on using the [interval covering criterion](Yin_C_G0345_v185.md#statement) when the number of exterior conjugates can grow.

<a id="roots"></a>
## A disappearing term and the reverse inclusion

At a nonzero root, divide a relation by its highest power of \(z\) and write it as
\[
                 c_0+\sum_{j=1}^{s}c_jz^{-e_j}=0,\qquad
                    1\le e_1<\cdots<e_s,\quad c_0\ne0.
\]
Along a convergent sequence of distinct roots with limit of modulus greater than one and length at most \(L+1\), pass to a single coefficient vector and then to a subsequence on which each exponent stabilizes or tends to infinity.
If all stabilize, one fixed polynomial contains infinitely many distinct roots, which is impossible.
The divergent terms tend to zero, while the stabilized terms include the nonzero constant term and have distinct exponents.
Their Laurent polynomial vanishes at the limit and has length at most \(L\), giving \(Z_{L+1}'\subseteq Z_L\) and relative closedness.

Conversely, if \(F(z_0)=0\) with \(|z_0|>1\) and length at most \(L\), the polynomials \(X^n F(X)+1\) have length at most \(L+1\).
On arbitrarily small circles surrounding \(z_0\) in \(|z|>1\) and avoiding zeros of \(F\), the perturbation \(z^{-n}\) tends uniformly to zero.
Rouché's theorem places roots of these polynomials arbitrarily close to \(z_0\), and different exponents cannot give a common exterior root since \(z^{n_1-n_2}=1\) would follow.
For \(z_0>1\), choose real endpoints immediately to its right where \(F\) has the same nonzero sign.
Choosing the added constant with opposite sign gives a real root between \(z_0\) and any such endpoint for all sufficiently large \(n\).
The same reasoning works on its left inside \((1,\infty)\), allowing a different sign.
This proves both identities, including at multiple roots.
There is no exterior root at length two, whereas \(2\in Z_3\).
Iteration proves the relative derivative count.

<a id="products"></a>
## Compact radii and their products

A nonzero leading coefficient and comparison with the lower terms bound every exterior modulus by \(L\).
Any sequence of distinct radii converging to \(r>1\) therefore has corresponding roots converging on a subsequence to an exterior root of modulus \(r\).
Using (1), \(R_L\) is compact and \(R_L'\subseteq R_{L-1}\) for \(L\ge3\), with \(R_2=\{1\}\).
All the sets are countable since the polynomials and their roots are countable.
The positive real roots \(2^{1/n}\) belong to \(Z_3\) and tend to one.
Iteration of the positive real version of (1), followed by this sequence, shows \(1\in R_L^{(L-2)}\), where parentheses indicate derivative order.
The reverse bound follows from \(R_2'=\varnothing\).

For \(u\in R_L\), write \(i(u)\) for the last derivative order containing it.
There is a neighborhood of \(u\) in which each other point has smaller order because \(u\) is isolated in the derivative of order \(i(u)\).
The \(j\)-th derivative of the Cartesian product \(R_L^h\) can therefore contain only tuples with \(\sum_i i(u_i)\ge j\).
Indeed this holds for \(j=0\), and around a tuple whose sum equals \(j\), product neighborhoods exclude any other tuple with sum at least \(j\).
Under a continuous map from a compact metric space the derivative of its image is contained in the image of its derivative, by taking convergent subsequences of preimages of distinct values.
Iterating for the product map bounds the last possible order by \(h(L-2)\).
Conversely for positive compact sets \(A,B\), \(A^{(i)}B^{(j)}\subseteq(AB)^{(i+j)}\).
For \(j=0\) this follows by fixing the second coordinate and taking \(i\) derivatives of its scaled copy of \(A\), and induction in \(j\) uses that multiplication by a fixed positive number preserves distinctness.
Applying this repeatedly at the point \(1\) proves the exact last order for \(R_L^{[h]}\).

Each exterior conjugate of a monic factor supplies one of its radii, and padding with \(1\) gives the asserted product.
For an arbitrary nonzero factor, the absolute leading coefficient divides that of the original polynomial and is at most \(L\).
The root formula for multiplicative Mahler measure now gives the finite union.

<a id="rational"></a>
## Rational points and effective open intervals

At every finite place above a prime \(p>L\), both nonzero endpoint coefficients of each relation are units after removal of its common monomial.
The polynomial and its reversal show that each nonzero root and its inverse are integral at that place, in every embedding.
For an exterior root \(z\), its complex conjugate has the same property and \(|z|^2=z\bar z\), so its modulus is an algebraic \(p\)-adic unit as well.
This applies to all factors of every element of the enclosing union, including the integer multiplier \(j\le L\).
A reduced rational whose denominator is divisible by \(p\) cannot belong to the union, which is closed.

Here is one termination proof for an interval computation.
For \(n\ge1\) form the finite list of all integral polynomials \(A(Y)\) with nonzero constant term, degree at most \(n\) and length at most \(L\).
Let \(U_n\subset[1,L]\) consist of \(1\) and of the moduli of points \(z\), \(1\le |z|\le L\), satisfying \(|A(1/z)|\le L |z|^{-n}\) for some polynomial on the list.
Truncating the reversed relation after exponent \(n\) proves \(R_L\subseteq U_n\).
Conversely every convergent sequence \(u_{n_i}\in U_{n_i}\), \(n_i\to\infty\), has its limit in \(R_L\).
For a limit above one, choose corresponding \(z_i\) and \(A_i\), pass to convergent \(z_i\) and stabilized coefficients and bounded exponents as in (1), and use \(L|z_i|^{-n_i}\to0\).
The resulting nonzero Laurent polynomial vanishes at the limit point, and the case of radius one is included from the start.
Thus for a rational \(r\) outside the compact enclosing union, eventually \(r\notin\bigcup_{j=1}^L j U_n^{[h]}\), by extracting convergent subsequences in each coordinate.
The sets in this last union are compact real semialgebraic sets defined over the rationals.
For example the displayed modulus test becomes a polynomial inequality in the real and imaginary parts after squaring and clearing the positive denominator.
Real quantifier elimination decides membership and distance inequalities.
Once an \(n\) omitting \(r\) is found, searching positive rational radii gives an open interval disjoint from that entire union.

In every positive-length interval of multiplicative measures above one there is a rational whose reduced denominator has a prime factor greater than \(L\), by taking a sufficiently large prime denominator.
If all the specified bounded-exterior-count units in that interval had a multiple of length at most \(L\), the resulting subinterval would contain none of their measures.
Taking logarithms gives the same conclusion in the logarithmic convention.
For power-stable units the failure of their positive gap produces measures in every positive open interval by taking suitable powers of units of measure tending to zero.
Powers retain the exterior count when the degree is unchanged.
Hence in this class, as well as the Salem class, the existence of a bounded-length covering on some interval and the existence of an empty open interval are equivalent, with the possibly smaller interval just constructed in the forward direction.
