# A finite-place bound on the first nonzero Newton sum at the trace pole

<a id="statement"></a>
## A logarithmic restriction for integral realizations

Let \(q,D\ge2\) be integers, \(p=q+1\), and \(F\) a monic reciprocal polynomial of degree \(2D\) over \(\mathbb Q\), with constant one, satisfying
\[
                     q^{2D}F(p/q)=(2q+1)^2.
\]
Let \(J\le 2D-1\) be the number of consecutive vanishing power sums at the start, or any shorter such prefix.
Equivalently the first \(J\) coefficients below the leading term vanish, by Newton's identities in characteristic zero.
If \(F\) is integral at 2, there is an absolute effective constant \(C\) such that
\[
 (J+1)v_2(q(q+1))\ \le\ C\,\log^2(2q+1)\log(3D).                     \tag{1}
\]
In particular every [genuine integral realization of the first nonunit pole](Yin_C_G0345_v891.md#return) satisfies
\[
                    J+1\ll \log(3D)\,[\log\log(9D)]^2 .                \tag{2}
\]
For a prefix at least \(c q\), with fixed \(c>0\), any such realization must have \(\log(3D)\gg_c q v_2(q(q+1))/\log^2(2q+1)\).
This gives a stronger degree restriction in that subclass than applying the degree-dependent height bound to the exterior root alone.

If \(F\) is integral outside one arbitrary prime \(\ell\), then without assuming integrality at two, root restrictions, or irreducibility, one still has
\[
       J+1\ll \log^2(2q+1)\big(\log(3D)+\log(2q+1)\big)^2 .             \tag{3}
\]
Thus for \(J\ge c q\) and all sufficiently large \(q\), even a one-prime construction requires \(\log(3D)\gg_c \sqrt q/\log(2q+1)\).
The [joint one-prime examples](Yin_C_G0901_v172.md#statement) allow the degree to grow accordingly.
These restrictions leave open the existence of genuine integral examples with short initial zero prefixes or in sufficiently large degrees.

<a id="congruence"></a>
## A precision test at any good prime divisor of two consecutive integers

For \(x=q\) or \(x=-p\) let \(r\) be any prime dividing \(x\) at which \(F\) is integral.
Put \(\alpha=1+x,\ \beta=1+2x\).
Homogeneous evaluation of the pole gives
\[
       v_r\big(\alpha^{2D}-\beta^2\big)\ \ge\ (J+1)v_r(x).              \tag{4}
\]
Indeed for \(x=q\) the leading term gives \(p^{2D}\), and all other terms are multiples of \(q^{J+1}\) in the valuation ring.
For \(x=-p\) the constant gives \(q^{2D}\); reciprocity makes the coefficients of powers one through \(J\) vanish, so all other contributions are multiples of \(p^{J+1}\).
The even exponents remove both signs.
In either case \(|\alpha|,|\beta|>1\) are coprime rational integers in absolute value because \(\beta=2\alpha-1\).
Hence the two numbers are multiplicatively independent and the difference in (4) is nonzero.
Both are principal \(r\)-adic units.

<a id="dyadic"></a>
## Two fixed local logarithms in the dyadic field extension

Take the even choice of \(x\), so that \(v_2(x)=v_2(pq)\).
To apply [Yu's theorem](../audits/literature/Yu99/Yin_U001.md#yu99-u001) at 2 take the fixed field \(\mathbb Q(\zeta_3)\), as required by the dyadic field hypothesis.
It is unramified of residue degree two at 2 since the third cyclotomic polynomial is separable irreducible modulo two.
In particular its integral valuation agrees with \(v_2\) on the rational field.
Use the two rational local units \(\alpha,\beta\) with integer exponents \(2D,-2\) and \(B=3D\).
The field, prime and number of logarithms are fixed; the bases vary with \(q\).
In the explicit bound the dependence on these bases is through the product of their two modified heights, each at most \(\log(2q+1)\) here.
Since \(\beta\) is a local unit, the valuation of \(\alpha^{2D}\beta^{-2}-1\) equals that of (4).
This proves (1).

For a genuine realization, \(F\) is the monic minimum of a Salem number \(\lambda\) of degree \(2D\), with \(0<\log M(\lambda)=\log\lambda<1/q\).
[Voutier's Corollary 2](../audits/literature/Vou96/Yin_U001.md#vou96-u001) gives \(q<(\log(6D))^3/2\).
Consequently \(\log(2q+1)\ll\log\log(9D)\), giving (2).
For comparison the stronger degree-only part of Voutier's theorem gives a scale of order \(q^{1/3}\log q\) for the lower bound on \(\log D\) at growing \(q\); (1) with \(J\ge cq\) is asymptotically stronger.

<a id="varying"></a>
## A prime may vary while its residue order stays one

With a single possible bad prime, choose the member \(x\) of \(q,-p\) prime to \(\ell\); when both are allowable choose either.
At least one is allowable by coprimality and both have absolute value at least two.
Choose any \(r\mid x\).
In the [valuation estimate for two powers of Bugeaud and Laurent](../audits/literature/BL96/Yin_U001.md#bl96-u001) the degree parameter is one and the residue order \(g\) is one, since both rational bases are already principal units.
Take the two height parameters equal to \(\log(2q+1)\), which also exceeds \(\log r\), and take the exponents \(2D,2\).
The prefactor is bounded by an absolute constant times \(\log^2(2q+1)\), using \(r/(r-1)\le2\) and \(\log r\ge\log2\).
The maximum in that theorem is \(O(\log(3D)+\log(2q+1))\), including its \(10\log r\) term.
Combining the resulting bound with (4) and \(v_r(x)\ge1\) proves (3).
This use of the actual residue order keeps the upper bound uniform in which prime of the consecutive integers remains good.
