# Exterior measure remaining at the unit circle for factors of short polynomials

<a id="statement"></a>
## A finite exterior part and a residual contribution

Fix an integer \(L\ge2\).
Suppose \(S_n\) is a nonzero integral factor of a nonzero \(P_n\in\mathbb Z[X]\) of coefficient length at most \(L\), and the multiplicative measures \(M(S_n)\) converge to \(r\).
There is no bound on the degrees or the numbers of exterior conjugates.
After passing to a subsequence, the following data describe the limit:

* There is a single nonzero integral polynomial \(B\) of length at most \(L\) and an integer \(1\le j\le L\).
* A submultiset \(T\) of the exterior roots of \(B\), with multiplicities, is the complete multiset of limiting roots of \(S_n\) away from the closed disk.
  More precisely, one can label \(|T|\) exterior roots of \(S_n\) converging with their multiplicities to \(T\), and all remaining exterior roots have moduli at most \(1+\epsilon_n\) for some \(\epsilon_n\to0\).
* The sum of log moduli of the remaining exterior roots tends to
\[
                       \delta=\log r-\log j-\sum_{\beta\in T}\log|\beta|
                                  \ \ge\ 0.                            \tag{1}
\]

In particular, if a positive rational \(r\) has nonzero valuation at a prime \(p>L\), the residual \(\delta\) is strictly positive on each such subsequence.
The number of exterior roots with moduli in \((1,1+\epsilon_n]\) must then tend to infinity.
For a specified limiting polynomial \(B\) the positive lower bound in (1) can be chosen effectively in terms of \(B,L,r\).

For any family of these factors satisfying
\[
              \lim_{\epsilon\downarrow0}\ \sup_S
                  \sum_{1<|z|\le1+\epsilon,\ S(z)=0}\log|z|=0,        \tag{2}
\]
with multiplicities, the whole measure closure is countable, compact and omits a neighborhood of every such rational \(r\).
This extends the [bounded exterior count case](Yin_C_G0345_v188.md#statement), for example to a uniform bound on \(\sum_{|z|>1}(\log|z|)^s\) for any fixed \(0<s<1\).
For reciprocal units, both orientations additionally constrain the limiting polynomial relations.
In particular, for each integer \(A\ge2\) the [reciprocal factors near \(A\)](#integer) have a sharp minimum length \(2A+2\) whenever a relation of length at most \(2A+2\) exists sufficiently close to \(A\).

<a id="limit"></a>
## Stabilizing the coefficients at the highest exponents

Divide \(P_n\) by its highest monomial \(X^{D_n}\).
After passage to a subsequence, the nonzero coefficients ordered from the top form a fixed tuple, and each exponent distance from \(D_n\) either stabilizes or tends to infinity.
The normalized Laurent polynomials converge locally uniformly on \(|z|>1\) to the nonzero Laurent polynomial consisting exactly of the stabilized terms.
Its constant term in \(z^{-1}\) is the fixed leading coefficient of \(P_n\).
Clearing a monomial gives the polynomial \(B\) of the statement with the same nonzero torus roots.
The absolute leading coefficient of \(S_n\) divides that of \(P_n\) and may be fixed at \(j\) on the same subsequence.
Every root of \(P_n\) has modulus at most \(L+1\) by the coefficient root bound.

Choose a small disk about each exterior root of \(B\), with pairwise disjoint closures contained in \(|z|>1\) and no boundary zeros.
Uniform convergence and Rouché's theorem show that for all large \(n\) the number of roots of \(P_n\) in each disk is the multiplicity in \(B\).
Since \(S_n\mid P_n\), its counts are bounded by these multiplicities and may be fixed by another subsequence.
All the zeros in each disk tend to its center because every compact annulus between the chosen boundary and a smaller circle there is eventually free of other zeros.
On every compact subset of the exterior avoiding the roots of \(B\), the nonzero limit bounds \(X^{-D_n}P_n\) away from zero eventually.
Together with the root bound this makes the maximum modulus of any remaining exterior root tend to one, using one as the maximum when the remainder is empty.
The root formula for \(M(S_n)\) now gives (1).

<a id="mass"></a>
## The obstruction at a rational target

At a prime \(p>L\), the endpoint coefficients of \(B\), after any common monomial is removed, are \(p\)-adic units in all embeddings.
The polynomial and its reversal therefore show that each nonzero root and its inverse are integral above \(p\).
This remains true of \(|\beta|\) because \(|\beta|^2=\beta\bar\beta\).
Thus \(j\prod_{\beta\in T}|\beta|\) is an algebraic unit at every place above \(p\).
It cannot equal the stated rational \(r\), and (1) makes it strictly smaller.
The positive residual requires at least \((\delta+o(1))/\log(1+\epsilon_n)\) remaining roots when \(\epsilon_n>0\).
For fixed \(B\), enumerate the finite products with all allowed multiplicities and all \(j\le L\).
They are positive real algebraic numbers, so comparison with \(r\) determines the largest one below \(r\) and gives an effective positive lower bound for \(\delta\).

This finite-product argument by itself gives no bound independent of \(B\).
Already \(B=X^a-2\) has length three and its submultisets of exterior roots give \(2^{i/a}\), \(0\le i\le a\).
As \(a\) varies these products are dense in \([1,2]\).
They illustrate the range of the unrestricted submultiset argument, without asserting that the products come from actual factors on a common subsequence.

Under (2), the residual tends to zero in the following sense: along every extracted subsequence the remainder in (1) eventually lies in \((1,1+\epsilon]\) for each fixed \(\epsilon>0\), so (2) forces \(\delta=0\).
Each accumulation value is then one of the finite products from some single \(B\), an algebraic unit above every \(p>L\).
There are only countably many such \(B,j,T\), and the closure is bounded by \(L\) since \(M(S)\le M(P)\le\|P\|_1\).
It is compact and avoids the stated rationals, hence omits an open interval about each of them.
The claimed fractional moment bound gives (2) on using \(\log|z|\le(\log(1+\epsilon))^{1-s}(\log|z|)^s\) in the indicated annulus.

<a id="reciprocal"></a>
## Two disjoint relations at an exterior limit

For algebraic \(z\ne0\), let \(\lambda(z)\) be the minimum coefficient length of a nonzero integer polynomial vanishing at \(z\), and put \(\lambda(z)=+\infty\) for transcendental \(z\).
If distinct roots \(z_n\) of reciprocal irreducible unit polynomials converge to \(z\) with \(|z|>1\), then
\[
                         \liminf_n\lambda(z_n)\ \ge\ 2\lambda(z).     \tag{3}
\]
Indeed, if the lower limit is finite, take a subsequence on which it is attained and choose a shortest polynomial for each root, dividing out the common monomial.
Its width tends to infinity after passage to a subsequence, since bounded length and bounded width give only finitely many normalized polynomials.
The chosen polynomial and its reversal both vanish at \(z_n\) by reciprocity.
The stabilized terms seen from the top and bottom each give a nonzero relation vanishing at \(z\) by the local convergence above, with the lower terms read in reverse.
The two groups of stabilized terms are disjoint for large \(n\) because the support width tends to infinity.
Their lengths sum to at most the original length, which proves (3), including that a finite lower limit forces \(z\) algebraic.

<a id="integer"></a>
## The sharp reciprocal benchmark near an integer

Fix an integer \(A\ge2\) and set \(L=2A+2\).
The unique positive-leading shortest relation at \(A\), after removal of a monomial, is \(X-A\), of length \(A+1\).
Indeed for a relation of degree \(a\) the leading term at \(A\) has modulus at least \(A^a\), so the lower coefficients must have total length at least \(A\).
Equality for the total \(A+1\) forces leading coefficient one and every lower contribution to lie at exponent \(a-1\) with negative sign.

There is a neighborhood of \(A\) in the complex plane in which every exterior reciprocal irreducible unit root with \(\lambda\le L\) is a root of
\[
             F_{N,\sigma}=X^N(X-A)+\sigma(1-AX),
                      \qquad N>1,\quad \sigma\in\{-1,1\}.             \tag{4}
\]
In a possibly smaller neighborhood every normalized multiple of such a root of length at most \(L\) has exactly this form.
If a sequence of counterexamples to the latter statement approached \(A\), its roots could be chosen pairwise distinct because \(A\) is not an algebraic unit.
Apply the two-end proof of (3) to the alleged normalized multiples.
Both stabilized blocks must have length at least \(A+1\), so they exhaust the coefficients.
Both outward-facing blocks must be \(X-A\) up to sign by the equality case just proved.
With positive leading coefficient and no common monomial this gives exactly (4), contradicting the choice.
For sufficiently near roots \(z\) of (4), its delay and sign are unique since \(z^N=-\sigma(1-Az)/(z-A)\) and \(|z|>1\).
In particular all such roots have exactly \(\lambda=L\).

Both signs in (4) realize the benchmark by distinct Salem numbers approaching \(A\).
Put \(a_0=1/A\) and \(b(z)=(z-a_0)/(1-a_0 z)\).
On the unit circle (4) vanishes exactly when \(z^N/b(z)=-\sigma\).
The derivative of its argument with respect to \(\arg z\) is \(N-(1-a_0^2)/|z-a_0|^2\), strictly positive for \(N>(A+1)/(A-1)\).
The winding number is \(N-1\) by the argument principle for \(b\), so exactly \(N-1\) simple circle roots occur.
For large \(N\), evaluation at \(A\) and at either side of \(A\) puts a real root tending to \(A\) on the right for \(\sigma=1\) and on the left for \(\sigma=-1\).
Reciprocity gives its inverse and accounts for the two remaining roots.
The exterior root is a unit because the polynomial is monic with unit constant term.
Its minimal polynomial must contain the inverse root, since a unit cannot have all its other conjugates of modulus one and exactly one exterior conjugate.
Degree two is impossible for large \(N\) because its trace would be an integer tending to \(A+1/A\).
Hence the factor is Salem, since its remaining conjugates have modulus one.
The uniqueness of \(N,\sigma\) shows these roots are distinct.
