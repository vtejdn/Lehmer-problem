# A logarithmic-gradient test for radical factors of bivariate specializations

<a id="statement"></a>
## Common components and the primes that can lower a factor's measure

For an integral Laurent polynomial \(F(X,Y)\) and a prime \(p\), put \(\delta_p F=(F(X,Y)^p-F(X^p,Y^p))/p\).
If a geometrically irreducible torus component \(G\) occurs just once in \(\bar F\) and divides \(\overline{\delta_p F}\), its exponent support lies on an affine line modulo \(p\).
More precisely its logarithmic normal direction is constant in \(\mathbb P^1(\mathbb F_p)\).
In particular if the nonzero reduction \(\bar F\) is geometrically squarefree and each geometric component has exponent differences spanning \(\mathbb F_p^2\), the intersection of \(\bar F\) and \(\overline{\delta_p F}\) in the torus is finite, possibly empty.
This test uses only the reduction of \(F\), and applies unchanged to every sign substitution \(F(sX,tY)\), \(s,t=\pm1\).

Suppose now that \(F\) is fixed with two-dimensional Newton polygon, every edge polynomial has only roots of unity, and \(L=\sum|c_{ij}|\) is its coefficient length.
Its vertex coefficients have a common absolute value \(q\), since the product of the root moduli of each edge polynomial is one.
For every prime dividing the [bounded radical denominator \(R_L\)](Yin_C_G0345_v210.md#statement), retain it as a possible exceptional prime unless **both** of these conditions hold:

* \(v_p(q)<p\).
* \(\bar F\) and \(\overline{\delta_p F}\) have no common torus component for each sign substitution, as verified directly or by the logarithmic-gradient test.

Let \(R_{\rm exc}\) be the part of \(R_L\) supported on the retained primes.
For all coprime integer pairs \((a,b)\) with \(\max(|a|,|b|)\) sufficiently large in terms of \(F\), take any nonzero nontorsion root \(\beta\) of \(F(sZ^a,tZ^b)\).
Let \(S\) be its primitive integer minimum, \(g\ge1\), \(\alpha^g=\beta\), and \(e=[\mathbb Q(\alpha):\mathbb Q(\beta)]\).
Then
\[
        \frac{g}{\gcd(g,e)}\mid\gcd(g,R_{\rm exc}),\qquad
        \frac{\log M(\alpha)}{\log M(\beta)}=\frac eg .                  \tag{1}
\]
This includes nonintegral roots and their actual primitive minima.
If \(\gcd(g,R_{\rm exc})=1\), \(S(Z^g)\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\) and every such radical has the same first Mahler measure as \(\beta\).
For the version restricted to algebraic units the edge roots need only have modulus one and the condition on \(v_p(q)\) can be dropped.

One all-prime class of reciprocal parents, of unbounded numbers of terms, is
\[
     F=Y+Y^{-1}-P(X),\qquad
     P=c_0+\sum_{j=1}^h c_j(X^j+X^{-j}),\qquad
     h\ge1,\quad c_0,c_1,c_h\in\{1,-1\},\quad c_j\in\mathbb Z .
\]
Here \(R_{\rm exc}=1\) and no dilation lowers any individual nontorsion factor's measure on sufficiently large primitive directions.
The cutoff may depend on \(P\).
Finite full-rank monomial pullbacks also preserve absence of common residual components, including inseparable pullbacks.
There are parents with cyclotomic edge roots and primitive specializations of arbitrarily large degree for which a prime loss really occurs.

<a id="gradient"></a>
## A common simple component has a Frobenius-fixed logarithmic normal

Work over \(k=\overline{\mathbb F}_p\), and write \(\Theta_1=X\partial_X,\ \Theta_2=Y\partial_Y\).
On the function field of any component of \(\bar F\), differentiation of the integral numerator before reduction gives
\[
                   \Theta_j\overline{\delta_p F}=-(\Theta_j\bar F)^p .
\]
Indeed the two terms before restriction are \(\bar F^{p-1}\Theta_j\bar F\) and \(-(\Theta_j\bar F)(X^p,Y^p)\); the coefficients lie in the prime field.
On a simple component \(G\), the logarithmic gradient of \(\bar F\) is a nonzero scalar times that of \(G\).
Its two entries are not both zero there.
In fact if \(G\mid\Theta_jG\) then \(\Theta_jG=\lambda_jG\) for a constant \(\lambda_j\), allowing zero: its support is contained in that of \(G\), so by addition of Newton polygons any nonzero quotient has polygon contained in \(\{0\}\).
If both divisibilities held, every exponent in \(G\) would have the same two residues modulo \(p\), making \(G\) a monomial times a \(p\)-th power over the perfect field \(k\).
This is impossible for a nonunit irreducible Laurent factor.

When \(G\) divides the reduced defect, its logarithmic gradient there is another scalar multiple of that of \(G\), by the product rule.
The displayed identity therefore makes the projective direction of \((\Theta_1\bar F,\Theta_2\bar F)\) equal to its \(p\)-th power in the function field.
In either affine chart its finite ratio satisfies \(z^p=z\) and hence is in \(\mathbb F_p\).
Choose a nonzero \((i,j)\in\mathbb F_p^2\) perpendicular to this direction.
Then \(G\mid(i\Theta_1+j\Theta_2)G\); the same Newton polygon argument makes this derivative a constant multiple \(\lambda G\).
Comparing coefficients shows \(im+jn=\lambda\) modulo \(p\) at every support exponent \((m,n)\).
This proves the test, without claiming its converse or applying it to a repeated component.

For example at a prime with geometrically squarefree reduction and no binomial component, any common component forces
\[
                              p\le 2\,{\rm area}({\rm Newt}(F)).
\]
An irreducible factor with collinear integer support is a binomial up to a Laurent unit over \(k\).
Any other factor has a triangle of noncollinear support exponents; its nonzero integer determinant is divisible by \(p\) by the test.
Its absolute value is at most twice the component polygon's area, which is at most twice the parent area since a translate of the component polygon is contained in the Newton sum for \(\bar F\), itself contained in the original polygon.

For a full-rank monomial map the Laurent ring of the source is free of rank the absolute lattice index over the image ring of the target, using one monomial for each lattice coset.
The two equations for a monomial pullback are the pullbacks of \(F\) and \(\delta_p F\).
A finite-dimensional torus intersection thus remains finite-dimensional, regardless of \(p\) dividing the lattice index.
A Laurent monomial prefactor does not change this conclusion as its defect multiplier is its \(p\)-th power.
Signed pullbacks use the corresponding signed parent before applying the map.

<a id="primes"></a>
## An effective finite exceptional set from a characteristic-zero parent

For any geometrically squarefree integral parent with two-dimensional polygon and no geometric binomial factor in characteristic zero, the preceding area estimate excludes common components outside an effectively finite set of primes.
To see this, first exclude the primes dividing a vertex coefficient so the Newton polygon is unchanged.
Clear the Laurent monomial so that the resulting polynomial has no coordinate-axis factor, and take a nonzero coefficient of \({\rm Res}_X(F,X\partial_XF)\) over \(\mathbb Z[Y]\).
This resultant is nonzero in characteristic zero: over \(\overline{\mathbb Q}(Y)\) the parent is squarefree and has no factor \(X\).
Outside the prime divisors of the chosen coefficient a repeated torus component with positive \(X\)-degree is impossible, as it would divide both polynomials and make their resultant vanish.

For every primitive edge tangent of the original polygon use its primitive character \(z\) and a unimodular complementary character \(w\).
Write \(F=\sum_j A_j(z)w^j\) in this chart.
The rows \(A_j\) generate the unit ideal of \(\mathbb Q[z^{\pm1}]\): a common nonzero root would give a geometric binomial component.
Clear denominators in a Bézout identity to obtain a nonzero integer \(b_z\) in their ideal over \(\mathbb Z[z^{\pm1}]\).
Outside its prime divisors no binomial component in this direction occurs.
Every binomial factor supplies such an edge direction by the Newton sum with its segment, so this covers all binomials, including components of \(X\)-degree zero.
Outside the union of these finite exceptional primes and those at most twice the area there is consequently no common torus component.
Changing the two signs permutes factors and preserves all support and multiplicity conditions used in this argument.

<a id="factors"></a>
## The defect of a primitive nonmonic minimum and the degree after reduction

Suppose \(\beta=\rho^p\ne0\) in \(K=\mathbb Q(\beta)\), and take their primitive integer minima \(S,Q\) with positive leading coefficients.
Their root fields coincide.
For every primitive \(p\)-th root of unity \(\zeta\), comparison of roots gives
\[
                     \prod_{j=0}^{p-1}Q(\zeta^j Z)=\varepsilon S(Z^p),
                 \quad \varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}.
\]
Indeed the left side has rational integral coefficients by Galois invariance and integrality, and is primitive: modulo any prime ideal in \(\mathbb Z[\zeta]\) each factor is nonzero by primitivity of \(Q\) and the fact that \(\zeta\) is a unit.
The conjugates of \(\rho\) have distinct \(p\)-th powers by equality of the root fields.
This proves equality up to sign, with positive sign for odd \(p\) by the leading term.
In particular the absolute leading and constant coefficients of \(S\) are the \(p\)-th powers of those of \(Q\).

Reduction at \(1-\zeta\) gives \(\bar Q=\bar S\) by Frobenius, including at \(p=2\).
The product omitting \(j=0\) is likewise integral rational, reducing to \(\bar S^{p-1}\).
Thus for some integral \(U,V\) the full product equals \((S+pU)(S^{p-1}+pV)\).
Subtracting from \(S^p\) with the indicated sign and dividing by \(p\) proves \(\bar S\mid\overline{\delta_p S}\), since \((1-\varepsilon)/p\) is integral.
This polynomial argument imposes no monicity or index condition.

The defect product rule then gives the same divisibility in the reduced specialized parent.
For a primitive direction, the map \((X,Y)\mapsto(Z^a,Z^b)\) on the Laurent generators is surjective on Laurent rings.
Consequently the residue Laurent quotient by \(\bar S\) is a quotient of the two-equation intersection for the signed parent, as in the [specialization and Bézout argument](Yin_C_G0901_v161.md#defect).
Its dimension is exactly the span of the exponents surviving in \(\bar S\), including dimension zero for a monomial.
When the two plane equations have no common torus component, this span is at most \(p d_F^2\), with \(d_F\) the total degree of a monomial clearing of \(F\).

At all sufficiently large primitive directions the extreme exponents come from unique vertices: each tie lies on one of finitely many rational lines, each with only two primitive lattice directions.
The absolute endpoint coefficients of \(S\) divide the two parent endpoint coefficients by integral primitive factorization.
If \(v_p(q)<p\), their being \(p\)-th powers implies they are both \(p\)-adic units and their reduced span is the full degree.
For an algebraic-unit input these endpoints are already units at every prime.
The [edge degree theorem](Yin_C_G0345_v171.md#statement) gives \(\deg S\gg_F\max(|a|,|b|)\) for all nontorsion factors when all edge roots are roots of unity, or for all nontorsion unit factors when all such roots have modulus one.
This applies with a common constant for the finitely many signs.
Hence for all sufficiently large primitive directions the bound \(p d_F^2\) rules out in-field \(p\)-th powering for each of the tested primes dividing \(R_L\).
It rules this out for \(-\beta\) too, by substituting \(-Z\) in the signed family.
At primes outside \(R_L\), a degree-one extension for a \(p\)-th root is impossible by the [radical denominator theorem](Yin_C_G0345_v210.md#statement), again for both signs.

For any such nonexceptional \(p\), every \(Z^{p^j}-\beta\) is irreducible over \(K\) by the [binomial criterion](../audits/literature/JP18/Yin_U001.md#jp18-u001); when \(p=2\) the additional possibility \(\beta\in-4K^4\) would make \(-\beta\) an in-field square.
If \(p^j\Vert g\), then \(\alpha^{g/p^j}\) generates a subextension of degree \(p^j\) over \(K\), giving \(p^j\mid e\) regardless of the other prime factors of \(g\).
Combining this with the old denominator divisibility by \(R_L\) proves (1); the ratio of log measures follows from \(h(\alpha)=h(\beta)/g\) and the tower degrees.
When the denominator in (1) equals one, \(g\mid e\) and \(e\le g\) imply \(e=g\), which proves irreducibility of the composed primitive minimum.

<a id="families"></a>
## Reciprocal parents with arbitrarily many terms and an actual prime loss

For the reciprocal family in the statement, multiplying by \(Y\) gives \(Y^2-P(X)Y+1\).
Over \(k(X)\), a root in this rational function field would be integral over \(k[X^{\pm1}]\), as would its inverse by the monic equation with unit constant.
The Laurent polynomial ring is integrally closed and has units \(cX^n\).
Consequently reducibility would give \(\bar P=cX^n+c^{-1}X^{-n}\).
This is impossible in every characteristic because \(\bar P\) has both a nonzero constant term and a nonzero nonconstant term, including in characteristic two.
Monicity also excludes a vertical component.
Thus the Laurent reduction is absolutely irreducible, and its support contains \((0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\) in every characteristic.
The logarithmic-gradient test excludes every prime.
The four polygon edges have primitive difference vectors and endpoint coefficients of absolute value one, so their binomial edge roots are roots of unity and \(q=1\).
This proves (1) with \(R_{\rm exc}=1\) on this family, extending the [five-term case](Yin_C_G0901_v161.md#statement).

For example take \(P(X)=\sum_{j=-h}^h X^j\) and \((a,b)=(1,b)\) with \(b>h\).
At \(Z=1\) the specialization is negative, and it is positive at sufficiently large positive real \(Z\), so there is an actual positive exterior real root.
For all sufficiently large \(b\) its minimum remains irreducible under every dilation and this real root is primitive on its unit line in its embedded field: the torsion there consists only of the two signs, and no in-field prime power is possible for the positive representative.

In contrast, for every prime \(p\) take \(F=X-(1+Y)^p\).
Its edge roots are roots of unity.
Its reduced simple component \(X-1-Y^p\) has two-dimensional Newton polygon of normalized area \(p\) but collinear support modulo \(p\).
Substitution \(X=(1+Y)^p\) into the integral defect gives a multiple of \(p\), since \((1+Y)^p\equiv1+Y^p\bmod p\) and their \(p\)-th powers agree modulo \(p^2\).
Therefore the reduced component really does divide the defect, attaining equality in the area bound.
For any \(a>p\) coprime to \(p\), the primitive specialization \(Z^a-(1+Z)^p\) is monic with unit constant, negative at one and positive at infinity.
Every positive real root \(\beta>1\) satisfies \(\beta^a=(1+\beta)^p\).
Writing \(ia+jp=1\) gives \(\rho=(1+\beta)^i\beta^j\in\mathbb Q(\beta)\) with \(\rho^p=\beta\).
Thus an irreducible factor at dilation \(p\) has log measure exactly \(m(\beta)/p\), where \(m=\log M\).
This is an actual loss at arbitrarily large primitive directions; no general absence of prime loss follows merely from squarefreeness or cyclotomic edges.
