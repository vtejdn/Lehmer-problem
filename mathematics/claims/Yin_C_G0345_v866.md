# An affine relation bounds the least abelian power degree of a four-term root

<a id="statement"></a>
## An absolute gap with unbounded coefficients

Let \(H=\mathbb Q^{\rm ab}\), let \(n_0,n_1,n_2,n_3\) be distinct integers, and let \(a_i\in H^\times\).
Suppose \(z\ne0\) is a nontorsion zero of \(\sum_{i=0}^3 a_i X^{n_i}\).
Take any primitive integer vector \(\lambda\ne0\) with \(\sum_i\lambda_i=\sum_i\lambda_i n_i=0\), except for permutations of \((1,1,-1,-1)\), and write \(L=\|\lambda\|_1\).
If \(z^{-1}\) is conjugate to \(z\) over \(\mathbb Q\), then
\[
              \min_{j\ge1}[H(z^j):H]\le L.
\]
Consequently every such four-term polynomial, with or without reciprocity of its root, satisfies
\[
 m(z)\gg G_3(L),\qquad G_j(t)=\frac{(\log\log(5t))^3}{(\log(2t))^j},
\]
with an absolute implied constant, where \(m(z)=[\mathbb Q(z):\mathbb Q]h(z)\).
In particular any fixed admissible affine relation gives a positive gap for all its exponent tuples, common dilations and choices of nonzero coefficients in \(H\), including arbitrary root-of-unity twists.
If \(m(z)\to0\) along four-term sections, **every** admissible primitive affine relation on their exponents eventually satisfies
\[
 L\ge \exp\!\left(c\,m(z)^{-1/3}\log(1/m(z))\right)
\]
for one absolute \(c>0\).
These conclusions concern individual roots, independently of the Mahler measure of the full polynomial.

For each \(H_0\ge1\) there is moreover a constant \(c(H_0)>0\) for **all untwisted four-term primitive integer parents of coefficient height at most \(H_0\) which are nonreciprocal up to sign and a monomial**.
Every integral factor of such a parent having positive logarithmic Mahler measure has measure at least \(c(H_0)\) in logarithmic units.
Equivalently one may fix any finite set of rational coefficient tuples for the individual-root statement, clearing common denominators and content.
The earlier [coefficient-length theorem](Yin_C_G0345_v555.md#statement) allows any number of terms with coefficient length at most six.
Here the number of terms is four and the primitive coefficient height may have any fixed bound.

The [finite Laurent families](Yin_C_G0345_v236.md#statement) use a coefficient-height bound, and the [inversion obstruction](Yin_C_G0345_v777.md#statement) fixes a rational plane polynomial before varying its sections and twists.
The [polygon incidence estimate](Yin_C_G0345_v752.md#statement) allows complex coefficients and controls the fraction of an ordinary Galois orbit meeting the curve.
Here all points of a relative Galois orbit meet both curves because the coefficients lie in \(H\).
The bound on a power degree depends only on the given affine relation.

<a id="polygon"></a>
## The lattice polygon of a four-term relation

Take \(e_0=0\) and the three standard basis vectors \(e_1,e_2,e_3\) in \(\mathbb Z^3\).
The vector \(u=(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3)\) is primitive since \(\lambda_0=-\sum_{i>0}\lambda_i\).
The lattice \(M=\mathbb Z^3/\mathbb Zu\) is free of rank two.
Write \(p_i\) for the images of the four vectors and \(N\) for their convex hull, with area \(A\) normalized so a fundamental parallelogram of \(M\) has area one.
The functional \(e_i\mapsto n_i-n_0\) descends to \(M\), hence the \(p_i\) are distinct, and their differences generate \(M\).
Their only primitive affine relations are \(\pm\lambda\).
If \(N\) had two distinct pairs of opposite parallel edges it would have four vertices and be a parallelogram.
Opposite vertices would then give the excluded affine relation, so there is at most one pair of opposite edge normals.

The normalized triangle area \(2\operatorname{area}(\operatorname{conv}\{p_j:j\ne i\})\) is \(|\lambda_i|\) for each \(i\).
Indeed the determinant of projected lattice vectors \(v,w\) in a suitable oriented basis is \(\det(u,v,w)\), since \(u\) extends to a unimodular basis.
Applying this to differences of the three vertices of each tetrahedral face gives the three coordinates of \(u\) and their negative sum, up to the alternating orientation signs.
Project the tetrahedron along the direction \(u\).
The faces on its upper boundary in this direction project disjointly in their interiors and cover the polygon up to area zero, since a line parallel to \(u\) over an interior point meets the tetrahedron in a segment with a unique upper endpoint.
In barycentric coordinates these four coordinates change in direction \((\lambda_0,\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3)\), so upper faces are the ones indexed by negative entries, and zero entries project to area zero.
Consequently \(2A=\sum_{\lambda_i<0}|\lambda_i|=L/2\), including the case of three collinear projected points.

<a id="intersection"></a>
## Relative conjugates and common binomial components

Choose a basis of \(M\), denote the descended functional by \((k,l)\), and set \(F=\sum_i a_i X^{p_{i,1}}Y^{p_{i,2}}\).
Then the original polynomial is \(X^{n_0}F(X^k,X^l)\), and \(P=(z^k,z^l)\) lies on \(F=0\).
Choose an automorphism \(\sigma\) of \(\overline{\mathbb Q}\) sending \(z\) to \(z^{-1}\).
The point \(P\) lies also on \(J(X,Y)=(\sigma F)(X^{-1},Y^{-1})\).
Normality of \(H/\mathbb Q\) ensures \(J\) has coefficients in \(H\), so the entire orbit of \(P\) over \(H\) belongs to the intersection.

A geometric component common to \(F,J\) cannot have a two-dimensional Newton polygon \(D\).
In fact the [Minkowski sum rule for factors](Yin_C_G0345_v752.md#polygon) implies each edge normal of \(D\) occurs in both \(N\) and \(-N\).
Two nonparallel edges of \(D\) would therefore give two distinct opposite pairs in \(N\).
Every common component is consequently binomial, of the form \(X^aY^b=\gamma\) with \((a,b)\) primitive, by factoring a univariate polynomial in the primitive character.
If \(P\) lies on one, then \(z^{ak+bl}=\gamma\), with \(ak+bl\ne0\): otherwise \(\gamma=1\) and \(F(X^k,X^l)\) would vanish identically, contrary to its four distinct exponents.
Each conjugate of \(\gamma\) over \(H\) gives a distinct binomial factor of \(F\) with the same character.
Their segments add to a segment of lattice length \([H(\gamma):H]\) in the polygon sum for \(N\).
The face of \(N\) exposed by a perpendicular normal contains a translate of this segment.
Its lattice length is at most \(2A\), since a triangle from it to a lattice vertex off its line has integral lattice height at least one.
This bounds the degree of \(z^{|ak+bl|}\), which generates the same field over \(H\) as \(\gamma\).

Otherwise all relative conjugates of \(P\) are isolated intersection points, since the union of common components is preserved over \(H\).
Remove the entire common divisor from both equations by dividing by their greatest common divisor over the algebraic closure.
The residual equations are coprime and still vanish at every such point.
The [plane torus intersection bound](../audits/literature/BS02/Yin_U001.md#intersection-multiplicities) bounds their number by the mixed area of the residual polygons, at most \(\operatorname{area}(N-N)-2A\).
Indeed each residual polygon is contained in a translate of the corresponding original, by the polygon sum rule, and mixed area is translation invariant and increasing in each argument.
For the latter property one can compare \(\operatorname{area}(tP+Q)\) for nested \(Q\) at large \(t\) and use its linear coefficient in \(t\), then use symmetry.

For completeness, \(\operatorname{area}(N-N)\le6A\).
For \(x\in N-N\) put \(b(x)=\operatorname{area}(N\cap(N+x))\).
Its integral is \(A^2\) by Fubini.
For \(0\le t\le1\) and \(x\in N-N\), choose \(y\in N\cap(N+x)\).
The set \((1-t)N+ty\) lies in \(N\cap(N+tx)\), so \(b(tx)\ge(1-t)^2 A\).
Integrating the resulting lower bound over homothetic shells of \(N-N\) gives \(A^2\ge A\operatorname{area}(N-N)\int_0^1 2t(1-t)^2\,dt=A\operatorname{area}(N-N)/6\).
Thus the orbit of \(P\) has size at most \(4A=L\).
Its field over \(H\) is \(H(z^{\gcd(|k|,|l|)})\) by Bézout, completing the power-degree bound.

<a id="measure"></a>
## Inverting the height estimate

For a reciprocal root put \(s_z=[\mathbb Q(z)\cap H:\mathbb Q]\) and take a power with relative degree \(D\le L\).
The [power compression identity and relative height bound](Yin_C_G0345_v775.md#compression) give \(m(z)\gg s_z G_4(D)\), and \(m(z)\gg s_z G_3(D)\) for integral \(z\) with \(m(z)\le1\).
The [compact-interval and logarithmic derivative comparison](Yin_C_G0345_v864.md#width) permits replacing \(D\) by \(L\) in both estimates.
Every algebraic nonunit has \(m(z)\ge\log 2\), from the leading or constant coefficient of its primitive integral minimal polynomial.
A nonreciprocal algebraic unit has the absolute [Smyth lower bound](../audits/literature/Smy71/Yin_U001.md#smy71-statement-and-conventions).
Since \(G_3\) is bounded above, these two cases and \(m(z)>1\) supply the remaining cases of the uniform cubic estimate.

As \(m=m(z)\to0\), this estimate forces \(L\to\infty\) uniformly over admissible relations.
For sufficiently large \(L\), taking cube roots gives \(\log L\ge c_1 m^{-1/3}\log\log L\) with an absolute \(c_1>0\).
First it yields \(\log L\ge c_1m^{-1/3}\), so \(\log\log L\ge\tfrac13\log(1/m)+\log c_1\).
Substitution gives the claimed exponential lower bound.

<a id="parents"></a>
## Nonreciprocal parents with a bounded coefficient tuple

Fix a rational coefficient tuple in increasing exponent order and remove its initial monomial.
For a reciprocal nontorsion root, the point \((z^{n_1-n_0},z^{n_2-n_0},z^{n_3-n_0})\) and its inverse both lie on \(a_0+a_1U+a_2V+a_3W=0\).
This affine linear polynomial is geometrically irreducible in the torus and is coprime to its inverse, since its three-dimensional simplex of exponents is not centrally symmetric up to translation.
Their intersection thus has finitely many geometric components of dimension at most one.
The [bounded character theorem of Bombieri, Masser and Zannier](Yin_C_G0345_v236.md#source) applied to the component containing the point gives a nonzero integer vector \(b\) bounded in terms of this tuple alone, with \(\sum_{i=1}^3 b_i(n_i-n_0)=0\).
Divide out the common divisor and adjoin \(-\sum_i b_i\) as the zeroth coordinate to obtain a primitive affine relation of bounded length.
If the support admits no parallelogram identity, this relation is admissible, so the first part gives a gap depending only on the coefficient tuple.

Otherwise the ordered support, after translation, is \(0,u,v,u+v\) for \(0<u<v\), because only the least plus the greatest can equal the sum of the other two.
Write \(F=a+bU+cV+dUV\) and take \((U,V)=(z^u,z^v)\).
If \(ad=bc\), then \(F=(a+bU)(1+cV/a)\), so either \(z^u\) or \(z^v\) is rational.
If \(ad\ne bc\), the linear polynomial in \(V\) has relatively prime coefficients \(a+bU,c+dU\) and is geometrically irreducible by Gauss's lemma.
The inverse, after multiplication by \(UV\), has coefficients \((d,c,b,a)\), so the two irreducibles are associates precisely when \(d=\epsilon a,\ c=\epsilon b\) for a common \(\epsilon\in\{1,-1\}\).
This condition makes the original parent reciprocal up to that sign, which is excluded.
For a reciprocal root all relative conjugates of \((z^u,z^v)\) are therefore common zeros of two coprime polynomials with unit-square polygon, of mixed area two.
It follows that \([H(z^{\gcd(u,v)}):H]\le2\).
In both square cases the [compression and height estimate](#measure) gives an absolute gap for reciprocal roots, and the absolute nonunit and nonreciprocal bounds cover all other roots.
Taking the minimum over the finite set of primitive tuples proves the root assertion.
A positive-measure integral factor either has nontrivial content or contains a positive-measure irreducible factor, by multiplicativity, so the same gap with a cap of \(\log2\) works for these factors.
