# Volume of the integral Salem trace slice and the cost of allowing one denominator prime

<a id="statement"></a>
## All separating grids and one prescribed finite place

Let \(q,n\ge2\), \(M=q(q+1)\), \(h=1/M\), and \(N=n-1\).
For \(K>0\) an integer congruent to one modulo \(M\), work in the real affine space of monic degree-\(n\) polynomials with \(M^nH(h)=K\).
Normalize volume \(\nu\) so the integral coefficient lattice in this space has covolume one.
Write \(\Omega\) for the open subset with \(N\) distinct roots in \((-4,0)\) and the remaining root in \((0,h)\).
For \(K=4M+1\) this is the union over all separating grids in the [integral trace construction](Yin_C_G0345_v891.md#return).
For all these \(K\),
\[
         \nu(\Omega)\le \frac{(8/M)^N}{2\sqrt{N!}}<1.                   \tag{1}
\]
The exact integral for the slice is given below.
For a uniform translate of the pole lattice, (1) bounds the probability of any point of the translate lying in \(\Omega\).
If one such random translate is chosen for each \(q,n\), with arbitrary dependence and any prescribed admissible \(K=K(q,n)\), almost surely only finitely many of these translates hit their respective regions.

Now use the original pole \(K=4M+1\), and let \(n\ge3\).
The actual integral pole lattice contains \(P_0=X^{n-1}(X+4)\) in the closure of \(\Omega\).
Fix a prime \(\ell\), a positive integer \(B\) coprime to \(\ell\), an integral polynomial \(H_1\) in the pole lattice, and any neighborhood \(V\) of \(P_0\) in the affine space.
For every sufficiently large \(k\) there is a rational monic \(H\in\Omega\cap V\) satisfying
\[
  H\equiv H_1\bmod B\mathbb Z[1/\ell][X],\qquad
  \min\{c\in\mathbb Z_{>0}:cH\in\mathbb Z[X]\}=\ell^k .
\]
Both \(H\) and \(S(Z)=Z^n H(Z+Z^{-1}-2)\) are irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\).
The unique exterior root \(T\) of \(S\) is real and satisfies \(1<T<1+1/q\).
The primitive integer minimum of \(T\) is \(\ell^k S\), of Mahler measure exactly \(\ell^k T\).
Thus allowing a denominator at any one chosen prime realizes the strict root conditions, the exact pole and every fixed compatible coefficient congruence simultaneously, with an explicit finite-place cost.
For degree three, the original integral problem at this same pole has no solution for any \(q\ge2\).

<a id="volume"></a>
## The affine pole slice in ordered root coordinates

Write \(H=X^n+c_1X^{n-1}+\cdots+c_n\), so the integer linear form defining the pole hyperplane is
\[
                     b=c_1+Mc_2+\cdots+M^{n-1}c_n=(K-1)/M .
\]
The change \((c_1,\ldots,c_n)\mapsto(b,c_2,\ldots,c_n)\) has determinant one and integral inverse.
Therefore Lebesgue measure on \((c_2,\ldots,c_n)\) in a fixed slice is the asserted lattice normalization.

Set \(v=(v_1,\ldots,v_N)\in(-4,0)^N\) and \(\Delta(v)=\prod_{i<j}(v_j-v_i)\).
For ordered distinct negative roots \(v_i\) and one variable positive root \(w\), the absolute Jacobian from roots to coefficients is \(|\Delta(v)|\prod_i(w-v_i)\).
Indeed the columns of the coefficient derivative are the coefficients of the negatives of the polynomials \(\prod_{j\ne i}(X-x_j)\), with \(x=(v,w)\).
Evaluating these columns at all \(x_j\) leaves a diagonal matrix with determinant of absolute value equal to the square of the full Vandermonde, and the evaluation matrix itself has absolute determinant equal to that Vandermonde.
At fixed \(v\), \(b=M^{n-1}(h-w)\prod_i(h-v_i)-1/M\) has absolute derivative \(M^N\prod_i(h-v_i)\) with respect to \(w\).
Dividing the two Jacobians and allowing all \(N!\) orders gives the exact formula
\[
 \nu(\Omega)=\frac1{N!M^N}\int_{(-4,0)^N}
  {\bf1}_{w(v)>0}\,|\Delta(v)|\prod_i\frac{w(v)-v_i}{h-v_i}\,dv ,
 \qquad
       w(v)=h-\frac{K}{M^n\prod_i(h-v_i)} .                            \tag{2}
\]
Here \(w(v)<h\) everywhere and the integrand on \(w(v)>0\) is bounded by \(|\Delta(v)|\).
The use of root coordinates is related to DiPippo and Howe's study of the coefficient volume of real reciprocal polynomials with all roots on the unit circle. [DiPippo and Howe, Section 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/9803097)
The pole slice includes the distinguished exterior pair, and (2) computes its own Jacobian.

Write the determinant \(\Delta(v)\) in the monic polynomial basis \(1,2T_1((X+2)/2),\ldots,2T_{N-1}((X+2)/2)\), where \(T_j(\cos\theta)=\cos(j\theta)\).
The first row is bounded by one and the others by two.
The Lebesgue Gram matrix \(G\) of this basis on \((-4,0)\) thus has \(G_{00}=4\), \(G_{jj}\le16\) for \(j>0\).
Expanding the two determinants and integrating termwise gives \(\int_{(-4,0)^N}\Delta(v)^2\,dv=N!\det G\), by grouping the pairs of permutations according to their relative permutation.
Hadamard's inequality gives \(\det G\le4\cdot16^{N-1}\).
Cauchy–Schwarz and (2) now give (1).
At \(N=1\) the bound is \(4/M\le2/3\); the ratio between successive bounds is \(8/(M\sqrt{N+1})<1\) for \(N\ge1\).

Averaging the number of points in the region over a lattice fundamental cell gives exactly \(\nu(\Omega)\) by translating the cell and summing its indicator integral over the lattice.
Markov's inequality thus gives the probability bound.
The sum of the bounds over \(q,N\) is finite: use \(M\ge6\) and \(\sum_{q\ge2}1/(q(q+1))<\infty\) to bound \(\sum_q M^{-N}\) by a constant times \(6^{1-N}\), then sum the factorial denominator.
The first Borel–Cantelli lemma proves the assertion with arbitrary dependence.
This average alone supplies no upper bound below one on the number in the specified integral coset.

## Strict real feasibility arbitrarily close to an actual integral point

For \(K=4M+1\), choose \(v_1=-4+t^2\) and \(v_{j+1}=-jt\) for \(1\le j\le N-1\).
For all sufficiently small \(t>0\) these roots are distinct and lie in \((-4,0)\), and
\[
             \prod_i(1-Mv_i)=(K-Mt^2)\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}(1+Mjt)>K .
\]
The strict inequality follows from the positive derivative at zero since \(N\ge2\).
Equation (2) gives \(0<w=h(1-K/\prod_i(1-Mv_i))<h\).
The corresponding real polynomial converges coefficientwise to \(P_0\), proving the closure assertion.
Consequently \(\Omega\cap V\) contains a relatively open ball in the pole space.

Parameterize that space by \(H=P_0+(MX-1)G\) with \(\deg G\le n-2\).
The [pole lattice](Yin_C_G0345_v891.md#simplex) corresponds exactly to integral \(G\).
In particular write \(H_1=P_0+(MX-1)G_1\) with integral \(G_1\).
Choose a sufficiently large auxiliary prime \(r\nmid B\ell MK\).
In \(\mathbb F_{r^n}\) there is an element \(u\) of full degree \(n\) with norm \(KM^{-n}\).
Indeed the norm is onto by cyclicity of the finite multiplicative group, and each nonzero fiber has cardinal \((r^n-1)/(r-1)\).
The union of all proper subfields has at most \(n r^{n/2}\) elements, smaller than the fiber for \(n\ge3\) and sufficiently large \(r\).
The monic minimum \(f\) of \(h-u\) over \(\mathbb F_r\), with \(h=M^{-1}\) in this field, is irreducible of degree \(n\) and \(f(h)=KM^{-n}\).
Consequently \(f-P_0\) is divisible by \(MX-1\) modulo \(r\), with quotient of degree at most \(n-2\).
Coefficientwise Chinese remaindering gives an integral \(G_0\) congruent to this quotient modulo \(r\) and to \(G_1\) modulo \(B\).

For \(k\ge1\) take \(G=G_0+Br\,Y/\ell^k\), with \(Y\) an integral polynomial of degree at most \(n-2\) whose constant term is \(1\bmod\ell\).
These coefficient grids have mesh at most \(Br\ell^{1-k}\) in each coordinate, so for every sufficiently large \(k\) they meet the inverse image of the open ball in \(\Omega\cap V\).
They give the desired congruence and reduction \(H\bmod r=f\).
The polynomial \(\ell^k(H-P_0)\bmod\ell\) is nonzero since \(MX-1\) never becomes the zero polynomial and the constant term condition makes \(Y\bmod\ell\ne0\).
Thus \(\ell^k\) is the exact common denominator of the coefficients.
Irreducibility modulo \(r\) proves irreducibility over \(\mathbb Q\) by Gauss's lemma over \(\mathbb Z_{(r)}\); denominators of \(H\) are units there.

In the totally real degree-\(n\) field of the positive root \(w\), the discriminant \(w(w+4)\) of \(Z^2-(w+2)Z+1\) is negative at every other embedding.
Hence this quadratic is irreducible, and its larger positive real root generates the full quadratic extension because \(w=T+T^{-1}-2\).
This proves irreducibility and the claimed root locations for \(S\), using \(h=(1+1/q)+(1+1/q)^{-1}-2\).
The transformation \(H\mapsto S\) preserves the exact common denominator: its coefficients are integral linear combinations of those of \(H\) and the inverse triangular system has integral coefficients and ones on the diagonal, by \(Z^n(Z+Z^{-1}-2)^{n-i}=Z^i(Z-1)^{2(n-i)}\).
Thus \(\ell^k S\) is primitive with positive leading coefficient \(\ell^k\), and only \(T\) contributes an exterior factor to its Mahler measure.

<a id="cubic"></a>
## The integral cubic at the original pole

For an integral \(H=X^3+aX^2+bX+c\) in \(\Omega\), the root product gives \(0<-c<16/M\); the root sum gives \(-1/M<a<8\).
Thus \(M\ge20\) is impossible.
At \(M=6,12\) the pole condition is \(a+Mb+M^2c=4\), so the only admissible integer \(a\) is four.
It follows that \(b=-Mc\ge6\).
The derivative \(3X^2+8X+b\) then has negative discriminant, contradicting the three distinct real roots.
These cases exhaust \(q\ge2\).

At the same pole \(q=2,K=25\), take \(H=X^2(X+4)+(6X-1)/4\).
Its signs at \(-4,-1,0,1/6\) are respectively negative, positive, negative and positive, and its pole identity is exact since \(6X-1\) vanishes there.
The primitive cubic \(4H=4X^3+16X^2+6X-1\) reduces modulo three to \(X^3+X^2-1\), which has no root in \(\mathbb F_3\).
Thus \(H\) is irreducible and its primitive reciprocal lift has measure \(4T\), by the quadratic and denominator arguments above.

For comparison, at \(q=2,n=3\) the different value \(K=109\) does occur integrally with \(H=X^3+6X^2+8X-1\), as the [complete cubic control](../computations/programs/Yin_J_G0185/free_trace_moments.md#census) shows by signs at \(-4,-3,0,h\).
The unweighted ordered-root integral there is \(\frac12\int_{(-4,0)^2}|v_1-v_2|\,dv_1dv_2=32/3\), so even the admissible region containing this integral polynomial has normalized volume at most \(8/27\).
The missing condition for the proposed unbounded-height construction is an integral point in the specified coset at \(K=4M+1\).
