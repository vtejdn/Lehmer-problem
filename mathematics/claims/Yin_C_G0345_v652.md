# Exceptional residue roots in a count with three free surviving exponents

<a id="statement"></a>
## A joint order below the uniqueness threshold

Let \(\alpha\) be a reciprocal nontorsion algebraic unit with minimal polynomial \(P\).
Fix the order of \(r\ge5\) labeled nonzero integer coefficients in a relation with distinct exponents and span at most \(W\), where \(W\ge r-1\) is an integer.
Erase two labels and choose three other labels with coefficients \(a,c,e\), ordering the last two by increasing exponent.
Select a nonempty finite set of residue roots \(\beta\) of \(P\) at primes dividing both erased coefficients and dividing none of \(a,c,e\).
Write \(M\) for the least common multiple of the orders of the selected roots.
Count surviving exponent tuples up to translation that satisfy the reduced relations at every \(\beta,\beta^{-1}\).
The number of these tuples, and hence the number of actual full relations up to translation, is at most
\[
       W^{o(1)}\left(1+\frac WM\right)^2\binom W{r-5}.                 \tag{1}
\]
The subpower factor is uniform in every other parameter as \(W\to\infty\).
In particular \(M\ge W^{1-o(1)}\) gives the subpower multiple of \(\binom W{r-5}\) even when the two coefficients \(c,e\) have equal squares at every chosen prime.
The third coefficient allows one to count the exceptional tests in the [two-position residue calculation](Yin_C_G0345_v645.md#local).

More precisely, for \(r\ge6\), put \(h=r-6\), \(\mu=(W-h)/(h+1)\), and let \(k\) be the number of selected roots.
A finite upper bound is
\[
   3^k\left(1+\frac WM\right)
       \left(3+\frac{2\mu+1}{M}\right)\binom W{r-5}.                 \tag{2}
\]
For \(r=5\) the bound \(2^k(1+W/M)^2\) holds.
The count concerns individual residue evaluations, so it applies also when some roots have multiplicity greater than one.

<a id="exceptional"></a>
## A third exponent determines the exceptional contribution

First take \(r\ge6\).
Fix the other \(r-5\) surviving positions, called the base positions, translating the first of them to zero.
Write \(t,i,j\) for the positions of \(a,c,e\), respectively, and \(u=j-i>0\).
At a root of order \(s\), write \(D,D'\) for the sums from the base positions at \(\beta,\beta^{-1}\).
After fixing \(t\), the two residue equations for \(i,j\) have respective right sides \(U=-D-a\beta^t,\ V=-D'-a\beta^{-t}\).
The ordinary case has at most two choices of \((i,u)\bmod s\).
In the exceptional case \(U=V=0\) and \(c^2=e^2\) in the residue field, so \(u\) belongs to at most one class modulo \(s\).
These assertions follow by solving \(cx+ey=U,\ c/x+e/y=V\) for \(x=\beta^i,\ y=\beta^j\). [Reciprocal residue calculation](Yin_C_G0345_v645.md#local)
In addition, exceptionality requires \(\beta^t=-D/a\), so it allows at most one class of \(t\bmod s\), even before fixing \(t\).

Prescribe which of the \(k\) roots are exceptional and let \(E,O\) be the least common multiples of the exceptional and ordinary orders, respectively, with an empty least common multiple equal to one.
Then \(EO\ge M\).
There is at most one class of \(t\bmod E\).
For each admissible \(t\), if there are \(b\) ordinary tests there are at most \(2^b\) choices of branches.
On each compatible choice the Chinese remainder theorem fixes \(u\bmod M\) and \(i\bmod O\).
This uses the exceptional classes for \(u\) and the ordinary branches at the actual \(t\).

Let \(X,Y\) be the numbers of consecutive integer positions allowed for \(t,i\), respectively, by their order relative to the base positions and by span at most \(W\) with the base alone.
Further constraints between the three free positions can only reduce the count.
As \(1\le u\le W\), the number of triples for this prescription is at most
\[
    2^b(1+W/M)
       \big({\bf1}_{X>0}+X/E\big)\big({\bf1}_{Y>0}+Y/O\big).         \tag{3}
\]

<a id="slots"></a>
## Averaging lengths of the available intervals

Write the positive base positions as \(b_1<\cdots<b_h\) in \([1,W]\).
The \(h\) numbers of vacant sites between successive base positions, starting at zero, together with \(W-b_h\), form a uniform weak composition of \(W-h\) into \(h+1\) parts as the base ranges over all \(\binom Wh\) choices.
For \(h=0\) the sole part is \(W\).
Each internal slot has the length of its corresponding part.
The right exterior slot and the left exterior slot both have length equal to the final part, since a position to the left of zero must be at least \(b_h-W\), taking \(b_0=0\).
Thus \(X,Y\) are specified parts, possibly the same part.

If \(Z\) is such a part, then \(\mathbb E Z=\mu\) and
\[
 \mathbb E[Z(Z-1)]=\frac{2(W-h)(W-h-1)}{(h+1)(h+2)}\le2\mu^2 .
\]
For the factorial moment, sum \(z(z-1)\) over the possible values \(z\) in one part using its generating function \(2x^2/(1-x)^3\) and use \(1/(1-x)\) for each other part.
Hence \(\mathbb E[XY]\le2\mu^2+\mu\) by Cauchy--Schwarz.
In the expected product in (3), the indicator product contributes at most \(\mu\), the two linear terms contribute at most \(2\mu\), and the quadratic term at most \((2\mu^2+\mu)/M\).
Now \(\mu\binom Wh=\binom W{h+1}\), and the sum of \(2^b\) over all prescriptions is \(3^k\).
This proves (2) uniformly even for dense base placements.
When \(r=5\), normalize \(t=0\).
The fixed sum is then \(a\ne0\) at each selected prime, so every test is ordinary and fixes \(i,u\bmod M\) after the branches are chosen.
The order relative to zero confines \(i\) to an interval of at most \(W\) sites, giving the asserted bound.

<a id="orders"></a>
## Selecting roots at the linear span scale

If an individual order exceeds \(2W\), keep that root alone.
At fixed \(t\) and fixed base an exceptional root would have order dividing \(2u\le2W\), since \(\beta^u=-c/e=\pm1\).
The ordinary count gives at most two pairs for each placement of all other \(r-4\) survivors up to translation, hence at most \(2\binom W{r-5}\) tuples.
Otherwise every order is at most \(2W\).
Keep an inclusion-minimal subcollection with least common multiple \(M'\ge\min(M,W)\).
Then \(M'\le2W^2\), and each member is responsible for a distinct prime power in \(M'\) that no other member attains, provided \(M'>1\).
Consequently its size is at most \(\omega(M')=O(\log W/\log\log W)\), since the product of \(l\) distinct primes is at least \((l+1)!\).
If \(M=1\), keep a single root.
Applying (2) or the \(r=5\) bound to those tests proves (1), using \(\mu\le W\).
For an actual relation the deleted terms have a nonzero sum, since otherwise a positive power of \(\alpha\) would be rational and inversion would force it to equal its inverse.
The surviving positions therefore determine the deleted positions by [reciprocal two-term recovery](../ledger/Yin_L_G0462_v313.md#recovery).

For the [actual terminal witnesses](Yin_C_G0345_v595.md#statement) at a neutral input of degree \(d\) and logarithmic Mahler measure \(m\), one can use \(W=O(d\log(2d)/m)\).
If the numbers \(s_p\) of selected distinct roots at their respective primes satisfy \(\sum_p s_p\log p\ge\delta d\) for some fixed \(\delta>0\), the [rank bound for joint orders](Yin_C_G0345_v646.md#orders) gives \(M\gg_\delta d/m\).
Then \(W/M=O_\delta(\log(2d))\), and (1) yields \(W^{o(1)}\binom W{r-5}\) for each full order since one may choose this window of size at least \(d\).
The marked term can occupy at most \(r\) places in the prescribed order of the other coefficients.
The presence of two erased coefficients and three invertible surviving coefficients with enough common residue rank remains an input to this count.
