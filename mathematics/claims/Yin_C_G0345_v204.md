# Cyclotomic resultants of units with short polynomial relations

<a id="statement"></a>
## Bounds on all exceptional orders

Let \(P\) be the monic minimal polynomial of a nontorsion algebraic unit \(\alpha\) of degree \(d\) and logarithmic Mahler measure \(m(P)\ge x>0\).
Write \(\lambda(\alpha)\) for the least sum of absolute coefficients of a nonzero integral polynomial vanishing at \(\alpha\).
For every integer \(n\ge1\), put
\[
 R_n(P)=|\operatorname{Res}(\Phi_n,P)|^{1/\varphi(n)}.
\]
Suppose that \(\lambda(\alpha)\le L\) for an integer \(L\ge2\), and put \(E=(L-1)d\log L/x\) and \(K=\prod_{p\le L,\ p\ {\rm prime}}p\).
There are at most \(L-1\) positive integers \(e_j\le E\) such that every \(n\) with \(R_n(P)>L\) divides some \(K e_j\).
In particular,
\[
 \operatorname{lcm}\{n:R_n(P)>L\}\le K E^{L-1},\qquad
 R_n(P)\le L\max(1,KE/n)^{L-1}\quad(n\ge1).                    \tag{1}
\]
The empty least common multiple is one.
These are necessary conditions, with no converse asserted.
In particular, at bounded measure bounded away from zero, either superpolynomial growth of the exceptional least common multiple for every fixed \(L\), or unbounded \(R_{n_i}(P_i)\) at orders \(n_i\ge c\deg P_i\) for a fixed \(c>0\), precludes bounded minimum length.
Superpolynomial \(|P_i(1)|\) in the degree likewise precludes bounded minimum length.
Here growth beyond every polynomial along the sequence, or divergence of the indicated resultants, forces \(\lambda(\alpha_i)\to\infty\).

If \(\alpha\) is power-stable, meaning that every positive power has the original degree, the exponent \(L-1\) in the pointwise bound of (1) can be replaced by \(\lfloor L/2\rfloor-1\) for all \(n>K\log L/x\).
At exact minimum length four, even without power stability, all odd orders with \(R_n(P)>4\) divide one positive integer at most \(2E\), taking \(L=4\) in \(E\).

For comparison, for every odd \(q\ge3\) and every even \(b\ge6\) there is a power-stable unit \(\alpha\) of degree \((b-1)q+1\), of exact minimum length four, with
\[
  \log\theta\le m(\alpha)\le\log 2,\qquad
  R_n(P)=|\Phi_n(b)|^{1/\varphi(n)}\ge b-1\quad(n\mid q),       \tag{2}
\]
where \(\theta>1\) is the real root of \(X^3-X-1\).
Thus even arbitrarily many distinct prime orders can be exceptional at fixed length and fixed bounded measure: choose \(q\) to be a product of odd primes.
For fixed \(b\) their least common multiple can grow linearly in the degree, attaining the order of the odd-order bound.
As \(b,q\) both tend to infinity, \(R_q(P)\asymp d/q\), so the exponent one in the bound for power-stable inputs of minimum length four cannot be decreased.
The units in (2) do not realize either obstruction in (1) or approach measure zero.

<a id="orders"></a>
## From a large resultant to a divisor of a support exponent

Choose a shortest relation \(F\) at \(\alpha\), remove its common monomial and denote its exponents by \(0=e_0<e_1<\cdots<e_{s-1}=D\).
The [norm bound for each consecutive gap](Yin_C_G0345_v189.md#upper) gives \(D\le(s-1)d\log L/x\le E\), since a shortest relation has no vanishing proper subsum at \(\alpha\).
If \(\zeta\) is primitive of order \(n\) and \(F(\zeta)\ne0\), the factorization \(F=PG\) in \(\mathbb Z[X]\) shows that the nonzero integer \(|\operatorname{Norm} F(\zeta)|\) is a multiple of \(|\operatorname{Norm} P(\zeta)|\), with norms from \(\mathbb Q(\zeta)\) to \(\mathbb Q\).
Since \(|F|\le L\) on the circle, \(R_n(P)>L\) therefore forces \(F(\zeta)=0\).

In a vanishing sum of \(s\) or fewer rational multiples of roots of unity with no vanishing nonempty proper subsum, the ratio of any two of the roots has order dividing \(K\).
This is [Mann's Theorem 1](https://doi.org/10.1112/S0025579300005210), in the homogeneous form stated [before Lemma 1 in Evertse's paper](../literature/pdf/Eve99.pdf#page=3).
That author copy explicitly attributes this rational-coefficient statement to Mann's Theorem 1; the original publisher PDF returned HTTP 403 and its proof was not inspected.
To see the order bound directly, divide through by one root and let \(r\) be the order of the group generated by the resulting roots.
For a prime power \(p^a\) exactly dividing \(r\), express each root as a power \(\xi^j\), \(0\le j<p\), times an \((r/p)\)-th root, where \(\xi\) is primitive of order \(p^a\).
The class \(j=0\) contains the normalized root one, and at least one other class is occupied by minimality of \(r\).
Grouping the sum gives a polynomial of degree at most \(p-1\) annihilating \(\xi\) over \(\mathbb Q(\zeta_{r/p})\); its constant coefficient is nonzero by the proper-subsum condition.
If \(a\ge2\), the relative degree is \(p\), a contradiction.
If \(a=1\), the minimal polynomial is \(1+\cdots+X^{p-1}\), by the relative degree \(p-1\), so all \(p\) coefficients are equal and nonzero.
Thus \(r\) is squarefree with all prime factors at most the number of terms, giving the claim, including coincident roots.

Partition the vanishing terms of \(F(\zeta)\) into minimal vanishing blocks.
The block containing the constant term includes another exponent \(e_j\), so \(n\mid K e_j\).
The least common multiple of the integers \(K e_j\) divides \(K\prod_{j=1}^{s-1}e_j\).
This proves the first bound of (1).

<a id="multiplicity"></a>
## The size of the resultant at its actual order

Write \(h\) for the multiplicity of \(\zeta\) in \(F\).
If \(h=0\) we already have \(R_n(P)\le L\).
Otherwise \(h\le s-1\): the first \(s\) powers of the Euler derivative \(X\,d/dX\), starting with the zeroth, would give a nonsingular Vandermonde system in the distinct \(e_j\) if all vanished at \(\zeta\).
Put \(g=\gcd(n,K)\), \(t=n/g\), and \(\omega=\zeta^t\), of order \(g\).
For \(0\le k<h\), the sum \(\zeta^k F^{(k)}(\zeta)\) vanishes and consists of at most \(s\) nonzero rational multiples of powers \(\zeta^{e_j}\).
In every minimal vanishing block the exponents are congruent modulo \(t\), since \(n\mid K(e_i-e_j)\).
It follows that this sum still vanishes on replacing \(\zeta\) by \(\zeta\eta\) for any \(\eta^t=1\), since each block merely acquires a common factor.
Consequently all \(t\) roots of \(X^t-\omega\) have multiplicity at least \(h\) in \(F\).
Conjugating over \(\mathbb Q\) gives \(\Phi_g(X^t)^h\mid F\) in \(\mathbb Z[X]\), since \(\Phi_g(X^t)\) is monic and its roots are simple.
As \(P\) has no torsion root, \(F=P\Phi_g(X^t)^h A\) with integral \(A\) and \(A(\zeta)\ne0\).

At \(\zeta\) the divided derivative \(F^{(h)}(\zeta)/h!\) is
\[
  P(\zeta)A(\zeta)\bigl(t\zeta^{t-1}\Phi'_g(\omega)\bigr)^h .
\]
The nonzero algebraic integers \(A(\zeta)\) and \(\Phi'_g(\omega)\) each have absolute norm at least one.
At every primitive conjugate, the absolute value of this divided derivative is at most \(L\binom{D}{h}\).
Taking the normalized absolute norm gives
\[
                   R_n(P)\le L\binom{D}{h}t^{-h}
                             \le L(DK/n)^h .
\]
Here only \(g\le K\) and \(\binom{D}{h}\le D^h\) enter the last inequality.
Using \(D\le E\) proves the second assertion of (1), also at \(n=1\), where \(R_1(P)=|P(1)|\).
For comparison, [Dubickas, Theorem 2, printed p. 284](../literature/pdf/Dub01.pdf#page=6) constructs irreducibles with superpolynomial value at one, but their leading prime coefficient and Mahler measure both exceed the degree.
That theorem does not supply units of bounded measure.

<a id="power"></a>
## Power stability and the odd orders at length four

Suppose \(h\ge1\).
The argument with derivatives also shows that the vanishing sum for each \(k<h\) vanishes separately in each residue class of support exponents modulo \(t\).
Indeed it partitions into blocks contained in those classes, including the empty sum if all differentiated terms of a class disappear.
In any occupied class containing \(u\) exponents, if \(h\ge u\) the first \(u\) such identities are a nonsingular system in the \(u\) nonzero coefficients weighted by \(\zeta^{e_j}\).
Its matrix evaluates the falling factorial polynomials of degrees \(0,\ldots,u-1\) at the distinct exponents, so its determinant is Vandermonde.
It follows that each occupied class contains at least \(h+1\) exponents.
If only one class is occupied, it contains zero and \(F=G(X^t)\) with \(\|G\|_1\le L\).
For a power-stable \(\alpha\) we then have \(t x\le m(\alpha^t)=t\,m(\alpha)\le m(G)\le\log L\).
Thus for \(n>K\log L/x\) at least two classes are occupied and \(2(h+1)\le s\le L\).
Using this exponent in the divided-derivative estimate proves the improvement, also allowing the already bounded case \(h=0\).

Now take \(\lambda(\alpha)=4\), without power stability, and split the coefficients of \(F\) into four signed unit terms, with repetitions allowed.
If \(R_n(P)>4\) for odd \(n\), these four unit complex numbers at \(\zeta\) sum to zero and can be paired into opposites.
Indeed if the sum of the first two is zero the assertion is immediate; otherwise two points on the unit circle with given nonzero sum have a unique unordered pair of positions, since their midpoint determines their chord, including coincident endpoints.
Apply this to the first two terms and the negatives of the other two.
Since \(-1\) is not an \(n\)-th root of unity, each opposing pair has opposite coefficient signs and exponents congruent modulo \(n\).
Consequently \(X^n-1\) divides \(F\), so \(F(1)=0\) and \(n\mid F'(1)\).
Write the four terms as \(X^a+X^b-X^c-X^e\).
If \(F'(1)=0\), then \(e=a+b-c\) and \(F=(X^{a-c}-1)(X^c-X^b)\) as a Laurent polynomial.
This is either zero or has no nontorsion unit root, a contradiction.
Hence \(0<|F'(1)|=|a+b-c-e|\le2D\), which bounds the common multiple for all exceptional odd orders, with common multiple one if there are none.

<a id="example"></a>
## Irreducible units with simultaneous large resultants

Take
\[
 H=-1+X\sum_{j=0}^{b-1}X^{jq},\qquad
 F=(X^q-1)H=X^N-X^q-X+1,\qquad N=bq+1 .
\]
First, \(H\) has no reciprocal pair of roots, allowing a root to be its own inverse.
If \(z,z^{-1}\) were both roots, the equations and reversal of the geometric sum would give \(z^{(b-1)q+2}=1\).
Thus \(z\) has odd order since \(b\) is even and \(q\) odd.
Set \(u=z^q\).
The case \(u=1\) would require \(bz=1\), impossible for a root of unity.
Otherwise \(|1-u^b|=|1-u|\) implies that \(u^b\) and \(u\) have the same real part, so \(u^b=u\) or \(u^b=u^{-1}\).
In the first case the sum is one, forcing \(z=1\); in the second it is \(-u^{-1}\), forcing \(z^{q-1}=-1\).
Both are impossible, the latter by odd order.
In particular \(H\) has no torsion roots, since complex conjugation preserves its roots.

To prove irreducibility, consider any integral factorization \(F=UV\) and set \(W=U^*V\), where star reverses the coefficients.
This is the [factor-reversal method of Ljunggren](../audits/literature/Lju60/Yin_U001.md#lju60-u001); we verify the needed four-term case here.
The endpoints of \(W\) are units, its degree is \(N\), and \(WW^*=FF^*\).
The middle coefficient is the sum of the coefficient squares, equal to four.
Thus \(W\) has four unit coefficients.
The six positive support differences of \(F\) are \(1,q-1,q,N-q,N-1,N\), all distinct for \(q\ge3,\ b\ge6\).
At each of them the autocorrelation is \(1\) or \(-1\); the six pairs of terms of \(W\) must therefore give exactly these six distinct differences.
The distance \(N-1\) lets us reverse \(W\) if necessary so that its support contains \(0,1,N\).
The other exponent \(u\) must satisfy \(\{u-1,u,N-u\}=\{q-1,q,N-q\}\).
The only consecutive pair on the right is \(q-1,q\), so \(u=q\).
Each difference from zero now determines the corresponding coefficient multiplied by the constant term from its unique pair.
Thus \(W=\pm F\) or \(W=\pm F^*\) before the optional reversal, forcing \(U\) or \(V\) to equal its own reversal up to sign by cancellation.
If \(H=AB\) with both factors nonconstant, apply this conclusion to \(U=A\) and \(V=(X^q-1)B\).
It would make \(A\) or \(B\) reciprocal up to sign, contrary to the absence of reciprocal root pairs.
This proves irreducibility, in particular excluding repeated factors.

There is a unique positive root \(\alpha\in(0,1)\) of \(H\), since the sum of its positive monomials increases strictly on \([0,\infty)\) and takes the values zero and \(b\) at zero and one.
Every root has modulus at least \(\alpha\), by the triangle inequality, and equality requires all those monomials to be positive real, including \(X\) itself.
Thus \(\alpha\) is the unique conjugate of minimum modulus; an embedding over \(\mathbb Q(\alpha^k)\) must fix it for every \(k\ge1\).
Separability gives power stability.
All roots are units and \(H\) is nonreciprocal, so [Smyth's bound](../audits/literature/Smy71/Yin_U001.md#smy71-statement-and-conventions) gives the lower measure in (2).
Since \(m(H)=m(F)\), Jensen's inequality and the squared coefficient sum four of \(F\) give \(m(H)\le\log 2\).
The multiple \(F\) shows \(\lambda(\alpha)\le4\).
A nonzero multiple of length at most three would have value zero at one, since \(H(1)=b-1>3\) divides that value.
Its length would be even, hence two; a nonzero integral polynomial of length two vanishing at one is a monomial times a signed difference of powers and has no nontorsion unit root.
This proves equality.

Finally \(H(\zeta)=b\zeta-1\) whenever \(\zeta^q=1\).
At primitive order \(n\mid q\) its absolute norm is \(|\Phi_n(b)|\), by factoring over all such roots and replacing them by their inverses.
Each factor has absolute value between \(b-1\) and \(b+1\), and \(d/q=b-1+1/q\).
This gives (2) without discarding any cyclotomic factor or repeated root of an auxiliary polynomial.
