# Coefficient ratios for small factors of four- and five-term polynomials

<a id="statement"></a>
## Two end binomials and an optional middle term

Let \(P\in\mathbb Z[X]\) have four or five nonzero terms, with its lowest exponent zero and degree \(D\).
Write
\[
       K=\frac{\sum_i|[X^i]P|}{\min(|P(0)|,|[X^D]P|)} .
\]
In particular \(K\ge2\), independently of content.
Let \(S\) be a primitive irreducible divisor of \(P\) of degree \(d\) and positive logarithmic Mahler measure \(m\).
There are absolute constants \(c_0,c_1>0\) with the following properties whenever \(m<c_0\).
Then \(S\) is, up to sign, a monic reciprocal unit polynomial.
Put \(t=\max_{S(x)=0}\log|x|\).

For **four terms**, either the normalized \(P\), with content removed and leading sign positive, has the [exceptional reciprocal form](Yin_C_G0345_v182.md#statement)
\[
       X^N B+\sigma X^{a}B(1/X),\qquad
       B=uX^a+\epsilon v,\qquad v>u\ge1,\quad (u,v)=1,\quad N>a>0,
\]
or
\[
                       \log K\ \ge\ c_1\,\frac{dt}{m}
                                      \ \ge\ \frac{c_1}{20m^2}.             \tag{1}
\]
Here \(\epsilon,\sigma\in\{1,-1\}\).
For a Salem factor outside the exceptional form this gives \(\log K\ge c_1 d\).

For **five terms** write
\[
     P=A(X)+c X^n+X^D B(X^{-1}),\qquad
     A=A_0+A_1 X^a,\quad B=B_0+B_1 X^b,\quad
     0<a<n<D-b<D .
\]
All five coefficients are nonzero and \(j=D-2n\) is a signed integer.
In the Laurent rational function field define
\[
      F=A+cY+X^j B(X^{-1})Y^2,\qquad
      F^\dagger=B+cX^jY+X^j A(X^{-1})Y^2 .
\]
Suppose \(j\ne0\) or \(A\ne B\).
Assume also at least one of the following two conditions:

* The common nonzero roots of \(A\) and \(B\) are all roots of unity, allowing no common roots.
* The discriminant \(c^2-4X^j A(X)B(X^{-1})\) is not a square in \(\mathbb Q(X)\).

Then, with an absolute \(c_2>0\),
\[
          \frac{m}{d}\left(\frac{\log K}{t}+|j|\right)\ge c_2,
          \qquad
          20m^2\log K+|j|\frac{m}{d}\ge c_2.                              \tag{2}
\]
For a centered middle term \(j=0\), every positive-measure integral divisor therefore has logarithmic measure at least \(c_3/\sqrt{\log K}\) with an absolute \(c_3>0\).
The same lower bound holds in the nonexceptional four-term case.
This gives a uniform gap whenever the ratio \(K\) stays bounded, even for unbounded primitive coefficients and arbitrary cofactors.
For positive-degree Salem factors of sufficiently small measure with \(j=0\), (2) gives \(\log K\ge c_2 d\).
The reciprocal four-term family and centered five-term inputs with \(A=B\) are outside these alternatives.

<a id="outer"></a>
## End gaps and the four-term case

The endpoint bounds and [Smyth's nonreciprocal theorem](../audits/literature/Smy71/Yin_U001.md#smy71-u001) allow \(c_0\) small enough that \(S\) is a monic reciprocal nontorsion unit polynomial up to sign.
The quadratic logarithmic modulus [bound for every nontorsion unit](Yin_C_G0855_v2.md#statement), part A, gives
\[
             \tfrac1{10}\le d\sum_{S(x)=0}(\log|x|)^2
                 =2d\sum_{|x|>1}(\log|x|)^2\le 2dtm .                   \tag{3}
\]
For a root \(\alpha\) of modulus \(e^t>1\) its height is \(h=m/d\).
If \(a,b\) denote the gaps to the next exponents at the two ends, the leading term at \(\alpha\) gives \(e^{bt}\le K\).
Applying the same argument to the reversed polynomial, which also vanishes at \(\alpha\), gives \(e^{at}\le K\).
Hence \(w=\max(a,b)\le\log K/t\).

For four terms, let \(A,B\) be the two end binomials so that \(P=A(X)+X^D B(X^{-1})\).
The [four-term comparison](Yin_C_G0345_v182.md#trace) shows that outside the displayed exceptional case
\[
                  A(X)A(X^{-1})-B(X)B(X^{-1})
\]
is nonzero and vanishes at \(\alpha\).
Here one may use the absolute smallness threshold in the four-term comparison when choosing \(c_0\).
The Laurent polynomial has at most five terms and width at most \(2w\).
The [sparse width bound](Yin_C_G0345_v186.md#statement) gives \(w h\gg1\) with an absolute constant.
Combining this with the upper bound for \(w\) and (3) proves (1).

<a id="elimination"></a>
## Elimination of the middle power

For five terms set \(W=a+b+|j|\) and let a star on a rational function of \(X\) mean substitution of \(X^{-1}\).
At \(X=\alpha,Y=\alpha^n\), the values of \(F,F^\dagger\) are respectively \(P(\alpha)\) and \(\alpha^D P(\alpha^{-1})\), hence are both zero.
In the field \(\mathbb Q(X)\) both polynomials are quadratic in \(Y\).
They are proportional only when \(j=0\) and \(A=B\): their linear coefficients specify the proportionality factor \(X^j\) from \(F\) to \(F^\dagger\), and the constant coefficients would give \(B=X^j A\).
The nonzero constant terms of the two binomials force \(j=0\).

When the quadratics are coprime their resultant in \(Y\) is a nonzero Laurent polynomial vanishing at \(\alpha\).
All their coefficient exponents belong to \([-W,W]\), with at most two monomials per coefficient.
The determinant of the four-by-four Sylvester matrix gives width at most \(8W\) and at most \(4!\,2^4\) terms.
The sparse width bound yields \(Wh\gg1\), with an absolute constant.

Suppose the nonproportional quadratics have a common factor over \(\mathbb Q(X)\).
It has degree one and gives a unique common root \(R\in\mathbb Q(X)^\times\).
In particular the discriminant of \(F\) is a rational square.
The identity \(F^\dagger=X^jY^2 F(X^{-1},Y^{-1})\) and its inverse show that \(1/R^*\) is another common root.
Thus \(RR^*=1\).
Write \(R=U/V\) with coprime \(U,V\) in the Laurent unique factorization domain \(\mathbb Q[X,X^{-1}]\).
Then \(UU^*=VV^*\), and coprimality gives \(V\) associated to \(U^*\).
Substitution in \(F\) yields \(U\mid A\) and \(V\mid B^*\), so \(U\mid B\) as well.
Under the common-root condition in the statement every nonunit irreducible factor of \(U\) would be a cyclotomic polynomial up to a Laurent unit.
Each such factor is associated to its own reversal and would divide \(V\), contradicting coprimality.
It follows that \(U,V\) are Laurent units and \(R=sX^e\), with \(e\in\mathbb Z\) and \(s=\pm1\) by \(RR^*=1\).

In the identity \(F(X,sX^e)=0\) the three exponent ranges are \([0,a]\), the singleton \(e\), and \([2e+j-b,2e+j]\).
They would be disjoint for \(|e|>2W\), so \(|e|\le2W\).
Divide both quadratics by \(Y-sX^e\).
For a quadratic \(p_2Y^2+p_1Y+p_0\) the resulting linear factor is \(p_2Y+(p_1+p_2sX^e)\).
Its coefficients have at most three terms and exponents in \([-3W,3W]\).
The two linear cofactors are coprime because the original quadratics are nonproportional.

At \(X=\alpha,Y=\alpha^n\) the removed factor is nonzero.
If \(n\ne e\), equality \(\alpha^{n-e}=s\) would make \(\alpha\) torsion.
If \(n=e\) and \(s=1\), \(F(X,X^n)=P(X)\) would vanish identically, contrary to its five distinct nonzero terms; the sign \(-1\) gives no zero.
Thus the resultant of the two linear cofactors is a nonzero Laurent polynomial vanishing at \(\alpha\).
Its width is at most \(12W\), with at most \(2\cdot3^2\) monomials.
The sparse width bound gives \(Wh\gg1\) in this case too.

Since \(a+b\le2\log K/t\), the estimates \(Wh\gg1\) and (3) give both inequalities (2) after decreasing \(c_2\).
For centered inputs this gives the lower bound at small \(m\).
For larger measures decrease \(c_3\), using \(K\ge2\), and for reducible integral divisors apply additivity, with content greater than one contributing at least \(\log2\).
The same reasoning applies to (1) when the normalized four-term input does not have the exceptional form.

<a id="examples"></a>
## Checking the discriminant at the extreme exponents

For \(j=0\) the discriminant has least exponent \(-b\) with coefficient \(-4A_0B_1\) and greatest exponent \(a\) with coefficient \(-4A_1B_0\).
A Laurent polynomial which is a square in \(\mathbb Q(X)\) has even extreme exponents and rational squares as their coefficients, by taking the lowest and highest terms of a ratio of two squares.
Thus if \(A\ne B\), either \(a,b\) being odd, or either of these coefficients failing to be a rational square, is sufficient for the centered gap.
In particular four positive outside coefficients qualify whenever the end binomials differ.

For instance take
\[
        A=1+2X^a,\qquad B=1+3X^b,\qquad c=-8,\qquad
        D=2n,\qquad n>\max(a,b).
\]
The monic polynomial \(P\) has constant one and \(P(1)=-1\), hence has a real root greater than one.
Its noncyclotomic factors have a uniform positive gap for all of these exponent triples.
For a growing-height example take \(A=u+(u+1)X^a,\ B=u+(u+2)X^b,\ c=-(5u+3)\) with \(u\ge1\).
Then \(P\) is primitive, \(P(1)=-u\), and \(K=(9u+6)/u\le15\).
The same gap is uniform also in \(u\).
