# Short numerators and the Gram determinant for hyperbolic unit polynomials

<a id="statement"></a>
## The sharp bound at width less than the degree

Let \(P\) be a monic integer polynomial of degree \(d\ge1\) with \(|P(0)|=1\) and no zero on the unit circle.
Write \(m=m(P)\) for its logarithmic Mahler measure and use normalized Haar measure on the circle.
These hypotheses give \(m>0\), since the product of the root moduli is one.
For a nonzero Laurent polynomial \(A\), define its width as the difference of its largest and smallest exponents and write \(a\) for its coefficient at the smallest exponent.
If \(A\) has complex coefficients and width less than \(d\), then
\[
                 \|A/P\|_2^2\ \ge\ c(m)|a|^2,\qquad
                 c(m)=\frac{1}{2\sinh(2m)}.                         \tag{1}
\]
Equality is attainable over the complex coefficients for every prescribed nonzero \(a\).
In particular, every nonzero integral numerator of width less than \(d\) has energy at least \(c(m)\sim1/(4m)\) as \(m\downarrow0\).
The [bound for unrestricted widths](Yin_C_G0345_v620.md#statement) gives \(K(m)\sim 1/(2\mathrm e m)\) for irreducible \(P\).

Let \(G_d\) be the Gram matrix of \(1,X,\ldots,X^{d-1}\) for the inner product with circle weight \(|P|^{-2}\).
Then
\[
                          (\det G_d)^{1/d}\ \ge\ c(m).              \tag{2}
\]
Thus along any sequence with \(m\downarrow0\), the squared covolume raised to the power \(1/d\) already tends to infinity in this degree-\(<d\) numerator lattice.

For arbitrary width, write \(\mathcal E(P)=\inf_{P\nmid A}\|A/P\|_2^2\), with divisibility and numerators taken in \(\mathbb Z[X,X^{-1}]\).
For every integer \(q\ge1\), one has \(\mathcal E(P(X^q))=\mathcal E(P)\).
The [explicit family below](#substitution) has unbounded degree, all root moduli tending to one and constant positive \(\mathcal E\) at a fixed positive Mahler measure.

<a id="evaluation"></a>
## Evaluation after reflection of the interior roots

Translate the smallest exponent of \(A\) to zero by multiplying by a power of \(X\).
Then \(A(0)=a\), \(\deg A<d\), and the circle norm is unchanged.
For each root \(\rho\) of \(P\) with \(|\rho|<1\), replace \(X-\rho\) by \(1-\bar\rho X\), whose boundary modulus is the same.
Keep the exterior root factors and multiply the resulting polynomial by a constant of modulus one to obtain \(W(0)>0\).
The polynomial \(W\) has degree \(d\), all its roots are exterior, and \(|W|=|P|\) on the circle.
The root product and \(|P(0)|=1\) give \(W(0)=e^m\) and \(|[X^d]W|=e^{-m}\).

Put \(W^*(z)=z^d\overline{W(1/\bar z)}\), \(B=W^*/W\), \(b=B(0)\), and \(f=A/W\).
The function \(B\) is analytic on the closed disk, has modulus one on the circle, and \(|b|=e^{-2m}\).
On the circle \(f\overline B=A/W^*\).
This last rational function vanishes at infinity and all its poles lie strictly inside the unit circle, so its circle average is zero by its Laurent expansion at infinity.
Hence \(f\) is orthogonal to \(B\) in the circle inner product.
Set \(k=1-\bar b B\).
Analyticity gives \(\int B=b\) and \(\int f=f(0)\), so
\[
        \int f\bar k=f(0),\qquad
        \|k\|_2^2=1-|b|^2 .
\]
Cauchy--Schwarz and \(\|f\|_2=\|A/P\|_2\) now give (1), since
\[
                  \frac{1}{W(0)^2(1-|b|^2)}
                  =\frac{e^{-2m}}{1-e^{-4m}}=c(m).
\]
The polynomial \(W-\bar b W^*\) has degree less than \(d\), since \(b=\overline{[X^d]W}/W(0)\) and \(W(0)\) is real.
Its constant coefficient is \(W(0)(1-|b|^2)>0\).
Multiplying it by \(a/[W(0)(1-|b|^2)]\) gives a numerator with constant coefficient \(a\) whose quotient by \(W\) is a scalar multiple of \(k\).
This establishes the asserted equality.

<a id="gram"></a>
## Successive distances in the monomial basis

Apply Gram--Schmidt in the order \(X^{d-1},X^{d-2},\ldots,1\).
Each squared distance to the span of the preceding monomials is the minimum weighted squared norm of \(X^j+\sum_{i=j+1}^{d-1}u_iX^i\) over the complex coefficients \(u_i\).
Every such polynomial has lowest coefficient one and width at most \(d-1\), so (1) bounds each squared distance below by \(c(m)\).
Multiplying the squared distances gives (2).
The Gram matrix is real because \(P\) has real coefficients and the weight is invariant under complex conjugation on the circle.
It therefore also computes the squared covolume for the lattice with integer monomial coordinates.
The usual Hermite upper bound for shortest squared length uses \(\gamma_d(\det G_d)^{1/d}\), where \(\gamma_d\) is the Hermite constant.
Since \(\gamma_d\ge1\) by the cubic lattice, this expression diverges along every shrinking sequence.

<a id="substitution"></a>
## Power substitution and an exact fixed-measure example

Write \(A(X)=\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}X^r A_r(X^q)\) by grouping Laurent exponents modulo \(q\).
Multiplication by \(P(X^q)\) acts on each group as multiplication by \(P\) in its own variable.
Thus \(P(X^q)\mid A\) holds exactly when \(P\mid A_r\) for every \(r\).
The summands \(X^r(A_r/P)(X^q)\) have Fourier supports in disjoint residue classes and substitution preserves the norm of each \(A_r/P\).
If \(P(X^q)\nmid A\), their squared norms sum to at least \(\mathcal E(P)\).
Taking \(A(X)=A_0(X^q)\) and passing to the infimum gives the reverse inequality.

For \(Q(X)=X^2-8X+1\), take \(q=2^j\) with \(j\ge0\).
The polynomial \(Q((X+1)^q)\) reduces to \(X^{2q}\) modulo two and its constant coefficient is \(-6\), so Eisenstein's criterion at two gives irreducibility of \(Q(X^q)\).
Its root moduli are \((4+\sqrt{15})^{1/q}\) and \((4-\sqrt{15})^{1/q}\), each repeated \(q\) times, giving \(m(Q(X^q))=\log(4+\sqrt{15})\).
For \(Q\), the energy of a monomial with coefficient of modulus one is
\[
              \int_0^{2\pi}\frac{dt}{2\pi(8-2\cos t)^2}
                    =\frac{8}{60^{3/2}} .
\]
Indeed the constant Fourier coefficient of \((s-2\cos t)^{-1}\) is \((s^2-4)^{-1/2}\) for \(s>2\), as follows by writing \(s=r+r^{-1}\) with \(0<r<1\) and expanding \(r/|1-r e^{it}|^2\).
Differentiation in \(s\) gives the displayed integral.
Every other nonzero integral numerator has squared coefficient norm at least two, and \(\max|Q|=10\) gives energy at least \(2/100>8/60^{3/2}\).
A monomial is indivisible by \(Q\), so power substitution gives \(\mathcal E(Q(X^q))=8/60^{3/2}\) for the entire family.
