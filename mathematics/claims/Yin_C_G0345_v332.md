# A relative Mahler comparison for separated integral frequency bands

<a id="statement"></a>
## The comparison and the gap above the common factor

Fix \(m\ge1\) and let \(F(X,Y)=\sum_{j=0}^m a_j(X)Y^j\ne0\), with integral Laurent polynomials \(a_j\) supported in \([-d,d]\), \(1\le d<n\).
Write \(h(X)\) for the gcd of the rows in \(\mathbb Z[X^{\pm1}]\), including their integer content, up to a Laurent unit.
Put \(F_0=F/h\), \(I=m_2(F_0)\), and
\[
 \mu_\phi=m_X(F_0(X,e^{i\phi}X^n)),\qquad D=n/d.
\]
There exist \(C_m,D_m\) depending only on \(m\) such that, for \(D\ge D_m\) and every real \(\phi\),
\[
 |\mu_\phi-I|\le \frac{C_m}{D}\bigl(1+I+\log(2D)\bigr).             \tag{1}
\]
The same estimate holds with \(\mu_\phi\) on the right after increasing the constants.
The two measures for \(F\) equal the corresponding measures for \(F_0\) plus \(m(h)\ge0\).
Thus (1) also bounds their difference, either with the primitive-row measure on the right or with the larger whole-polynomial measure.
In particular the difference tends uniformly to zero for bounded diagonal measures and \(d=o(n)\), with no lower bound prescribed for the size of an individual row or their maximum.

For phase zero there are \(\epsilon_m,\eta_m>0\) such that \(d\le\epsilon_m n\) implies
\[
    m(F(X,X^n))=m(h)\qquad\text{or}\qquad
    m(F(X,X^n))\ge m(h)+\eta_m.                                    \tag{2}
\]
When \(m(h)=0\) this gives a positive gap above zero.
It includes every irreducible integral diagonal with at least two occupied rows in this separated range.
An arbitrary common base factor can carry its own Mahler measure into the first case of (2).

The \(X\)-widths of the factors of a Laurent product add, by taking the extreme terms over the integral domain \(\mathbb Z[Y^{\pm1}]\).
We may recenter \(F_0\) by an \(X\)-monomial to put its rows in \([-d,d]\).
For \(n>2d\) its occupied diagonal frequency intervals are disjoint.
Their outermost coefficients have moduli at least one, so the diagonal is nonzero and \(\mu_\phi\ge0\) by Jensen.
Henceforth write \(a_j\) for the rows of \(F_0\).
They have no common zero on \(\mathbb C^\times\), since the minimal polynomial of any such zero would divide all the rows.

<a id="curvature"></a>
## A positive measure controlling zeros in complex neighborhoods of the circle

Use normalized circle measure \(d\nu=dt/(2\pi)\).
Represent twice the real and imaginary parts of all the coefficients on the circle by \(p=2(m+1)\) real trigonometric polynomials \(v_b(t)\).
Their holomorphic extensions in \(t\) are given by the Laurent forms \(a_j(X)+a_j(X^{-1})\) and \((a_j(X)-a_j(X^{-1}))/i\), with \(X=e^{it}\).
They have Gaussian integer coefficients, \(v_b(\bar t)=\overline{v_b(t)}\), and they have no simultaneous zero for complex \(t\), since they recover \(2a_j(X)\).
Let \(A(t)=\max_b|v_b(t)|\) on the real line, so \(A(t)>0\).
For independent uniform phases \(\theta_b\) on the unit circle put
\[
 W(z)=\mathbb E_\theta\log\left|\sum_{b=1}^p\theta_b v_b(z)\right|,
 \qquad
       \log\max_b|v_b(z)|\le W(z)\le\log\max_b|v_b(z)|+\log p.      \tag{3}
\]
To see the lower bound, integrate first in the phase of a maximizing coordinate and use Jensen's formula for a linear polynomial.
For continuity near any \(z\), choose a nonvanishing coordinate and integrate first in its phase.
The resulting logarithm is continuous and uniformly bounded there over the remaining phases because its argument is bounded away from zero.
Conjugation invariance of the phase law gives \(W(\bar z)=W(z)\).
The measure \(\omega=(2\pi)^{-1}\Delta W\) in the complex \(z\)-plane is the average of the zero counting measures of the holomorphic sums, with multiplicities.
Indeed each sum contributes its zeros through the distributional Laplacian of its log modulus.
Testing against a compactly supported smooth function and interchanging the integrals is legitimate since the positive logarithms are locally uniformly bounded and (3) bounds their mean negative parts on compact sets.

Multiply the Laurent forms for \(v_b\) by a common power of \(X\) so their union of exponents starts at zero and ends at \(s\le2d\).
Write \(Q(X,\theta)\) for the resulting sum with the phase variables as algebraic indeterminates.
The common power contributes zero to the boundary logarithm and a harmonic function to the complex logarithm in \(z\), preserving \(\omega\).
If \(s=0\), each nonzero real trigonometric coordinate is supported at a single frequency and must be constant.
The comparison is then exact by integration in the fast phase.
For \(s>0\) almost all phase choices have full \(X\)-degree.
The shifted forms have no common zero at zero by normalization and no common zero elsewhere.
Hence the cross-resultant \(R(\theta,\theta')=\operatorname{Res}_X(Q(X,\theta),Q(X,\theta'))\) for independent parameter vectors is a nonzero Gaussian integer polynomial.
For a full-degree first slice, each of its finitely many roots excludes a proper linear subspace of parameters for the second, and a degree drop excludes another.
The Mahler measure of a nonzero Gaussian integer polynomial is nonnegative by iterating the leading-coefficient Jensen bound down to a nonzero Gaussian integer.
The same induction gives integrability of its logarithm on the parameter torus, including for \(R\).

Reflect every root \(\alpha\) of \(Q\) into the unit disk as \(w=\alpha/\max(1,|\alpha|)^2\).
Let \(\sigma\) be the expectation of the sum of the point masses at these \(w\).
Its mass is \(s\).
The [cross-resultant calculation](Yin_C_G0345_v331.md#energy) applies to the whole parameter vectors.
Indeed at fixed \(0<q<1\), the sum of \(-\log|1-qw\bar w'|\) over independent slices is at most \(s(m_X(Q_\theta)+m_X(Q_{\theta'}))-\log|R(\theta,\theta')|-\tfrac12s^2\log q\), by the pair-distance and leading-coefficient calculations there.
Independence and integration turn the kernel series into nonnegative squared moments.
Monotone convergence as \(q\uparrow1\) and \(m(R)\ge0\) therefore give
\[
 \sum_{\ell\ge1}\frac{|\int w^\ell\,d\sigma|^2}{\ell}
       \le 2s\int W(t)\,d\nu(t)\ll_m d(1+I).                      \tag{4}
\]
Here (3) and the fiber coefficient bound \(\max_j|a_j(e^{it})|\le2^m\exp m_Y(F_0(e^{it},Y))\) give \(\int W\le I+\log(2^{m+1}p)\).

Replace each interior \(w\) in \(\sigma\) by its Poisson probability measure on the circle and each boundary \(w\) by its point mass there.
Call the resulting positive measure \(\tau\).
It has mass \(s\), and its positive angular moments are \(\int w^\ell\,d\sigma\), by the Poisson formula.
In particular every real Lipschitz test \(\psi\) satisfies
\[
 \left|\int\psi\,d(\tau-s\nu)\right|^2
 \ll_m d(1+I)\sum_{\ell\ne0}|\ell|\,|\widehat\psi(\ell)|^2.       \tag{5}
\]
This follows by Fourier Cauchy–Schwarz, first for trigonometric polynomials and then by uniformly convergent Fejér approximation with no increase of the weighted sum.

<a id="packing"></a>
## A cover with bounded local zero mass and a quadratic sum of radii

Let \(B(x,r)\) denote the closed arc of angular radius \(r\), extended to the whole circle for \(r\ge\pi\).
Fix \(\delta=2^{-18}\), set \(r_0=\delta/d\), and take \(K=16/\delta\).
At each \(x\) choose the largest \(r\ge r_0\) with \(\tau(B(x,r))\ge Kdr\), using \(r=r_0\) if the set is empty.
The largest radius exists when needed since masses of closed arcs are upper semicontinuous in their radius and \(\tau(\mathbb T)\le2d\).
All chosen radii are at most \(\delta\).
For \(r>r_0\) the stated lower bound holds, while for every \(C>1\) we have \(\tau(B(x,Cr))\le KCdr\) in all cases.

Choose a finite family \((x_i,r_i)\) with disjoint \(B(x_i,2r_i)\) such that \(B(x_i,8r_i)\) cover.
Repeatedly choose an available radius at least half the remaining supremum and discard all doubled arcs meeting the chosen doubled arc.
Discarded centers lie within \(6r_i\), and disjoint doubled arcs of radius at least \(2r_0\) give termination.
Write \(M_i=dr_i\ge\delta\).
Then \(\sum r_i=O(1)\) and
\[
                           \sum_i M_i^2\ll_m d(1+I).                \tag{6}
\]
The centers with \(r_i=r_0\) cost \(O(d)\) by disjointness.
For every larger radius take a nonnegative tent \(\psi_i\) equal to one on \(B(x_i,r_i)\), linear on each remaining half of the doubled arc, and zero outside it.
Then \(\int\psi_i\,d(\tau-s\nu)\ge cKdr_i\), using \(s\le2d\) and the arc length bound on its Haar integral.
The homogeneous squared norm \(\|\psi\|_*^2=\sum_{\ell\ne0}|\ell|\,|\widehat\psi(\ell)|^2\) of each tent is bounded absolutely.
Indeed \(|\widehat\psi_i(\ell)|\ll\min(r_i,1/(r_i\ell^2))\) by its support and the total variation of its derivative.
For disjointly supported nonnegative tents the cross inner products in this norm are nonpositive.
To see this, its squared norm is a positive constant times
\[
 \iint\frac{|\psi(t)-\psi(u)|^2}{|e^{it}-e^{iu}|^2}\,d\nu(t)d\nu(u).
\]
The identity follows by integrating translations and using \(\int|(e^{i\ell t}-1)/(e^{it}-1)|^2\,d\nu=|\ell|\).
Polarization now gives the sign pointwise for the cross difference products.
Thus \(\|\sum_i M_i\psi_i\|_*^2\ll\sum_i M_i^2\) on the larger radii, and (5) proves (6) by testing with this sum.

We also have
\[
                  \omega\bigl(\{|z-x_i|<256r_i\}\bigr)\ll M_i.    \tag{7}
\]
Each zero there of a phase sum maps under \(X=e^{iz}\) to a slice root with argument within \(256r_i\) of \(x_i\) and \(|\log|X||\le256r_i\).
The disk is too small for two different lifts of the same root, and the map preserves multiplicities.
The reflected root therefore sends at least an absolute positive amount of Poisson mass into \(B(x_i,512r_i)\).
Indeed at logarithmic depth \(a\in(0,256r_i]\) the density \(\sinh a/(\cosh a-\cos t)\) is \(\gg1/a\) for \(|t|\le a\) about its argument, and at depth zero its mass is a point.
Average over slices and use the upper bound on the larger arc to obtain (7).

<a id="green"></a>
## A real normalization on two sizes of disk

On a disk of radius \(R\) centered at a real point \(c\), write \(H\) for the harmonic extension of the boundary values of \(W\).
The Green representation for a continuous subharmonic function gives
\[
   W(z)=H(z)-\int_{|\zeta-c|<R}
     \log\left|\frac{R^2-(z-c)\overline{(\zeta-c)}}{R(z-\zeta)}\right|
                \,d\omega(\zeta).                                  \tag{8}
\]
Equivalently the nonnegative integral solves the disk Dirichlet problem with distributional Laplacian \(-2\pi\omega\).
Both \(W\) and \(H\) are symmetric about the real axis.
There is a holomorphic function \(h_R\) on the disk with real part \(H\) whose imaginary part vanishes on the real diameter, by taking a harmonic conjugate zero at \(c\) and using symmetry.
Multiplication of every real coordinate by \(e^{-h_R}\) preserves real values on this diameter and preserves all real ratios there.
Throughout the disk these normalized coordinates have modulus at most one, by (3) and (8).

If \(S\) is any measurable subset of the real diameter of length at least \(\gamma R>0\), the average on \(S\) of \(H-W\) is \(O_\gamma(\omega(\{|z-c|<R\}))\).
For each pole \(\zeta\), the nonnegative Green kernel is bounded by \(\log(2R/|t-\zeta|)\) on the diameter.
Its integral there is \(O(R)\), by projecting \(\zeta\) to the real line and integrating the logarithmic singularity.
Integrating this estimate over the poles proves the assertion.
In particular, there is some \(t_0\in S\) where the largest normalized coordinate is at least \(p^{-1}\exp(-C_\gamma\omega(\{|z-c|<R\}))\), by (3).

## Counting the fast periods with rapidly varying ratios

Fix \(i\), abbreviate \(x=x_i,r=r_i,M=dr\), and use \(J=[x-40r,x+40r]\) on the real lift.
Apply (8) to the disk of radius \(256r\) about \(x\), and take \(S=[x-r,x+r]\).
By (7) there is a point \(t_0\in S\) where the real normalized vector has Euclidean norm at least \(e^{-C_m M}\), absorbing fixed constants through \(M\ge\delta\).
Choose a single real orthogonal change of the \(p\) coordinates which sends its direction to \((1,\ldots,1)/\sqrt p\).
Write \(u_b\) for the rotated normalized holomorphic coordinates.
They satisfy \(\sup |u_b|\le\sqrt p\) on the disk and \(|u_b(t_0)|\ge e^{-C_mM}\) for every \(b\).
The fixed inverse rotation recovers the original normalized vector with operator norm one.

We detail the application of the [real-coordinate chart argument](Yin_C_G0345_v327.md#charts) with this common point.
For each denominator \(u_b\), the chart where the ratios \(u_a/u_b\) lie strictly between chosen signed thresholds of magnitudes in \([2,3]\) contains every real point of \(J\) where \(b\) maximizes absolute value.
Every real point has a maximizing chart since the vector never vanishes.
Impose also signed thresholds of magnitudes in \([\eta,2\eta]\) for \(n^{-k}(u_a/u_b)^{(k)}\), \(1\le k\le m\), with a small \(\eta=\eta_m>0\).
Write this expression as \(N_k/u_b^{k+1}\), starting with \(N_0=u_a\) at order zero.
Differentiation gives \(N_{k+1}=u_b N_k'/n-(k+1)N_k u_b'/n\).
On the disk of radius \(160r\) about \(x\) all these numerators are bounded by \(O_m(1)\), by Cauchy estimates through order \(m\) on the rotated normalized coordinates \(u_b\) and \(nr=DM\ge1\) for \(D\) large.
At \(t_0\), the values of \(N_k-\lambda u_b^{k+1}\) at the two permitted endpoints of each signed threshold range differ by at least \(\min(1,\eta)|u_b(t_0)|^{k+1}\).
Choose one endpoint for which the absolute value is at least \(e^{-C_m M}\).
Jensen on a circle centered at \(t_0\) of radius between \(100r\) and \(120r\) then bounds its number of zeros within \(50r\) by \(O_m(M)\).
The total number of threshold points on \(J\) is \(O_m(M)\), including the order-zero chart boundaries.

Partition the real line into full fast periods of length \(2\pi/n\).
Among the periods wholly inside \(J\), flag each meeting a threshold point or a portion of a chart where some positive-order normalized derivative lies outside its thresholds.
In the complement of the threshold points chart membership is constant on each component, since a denominator cannot tend to zero with bounded ratios unless every coordinate tends to zero.
Where a violation first occurs at order \(k\) for a fixed ratio, the preceding order stays bounded and has derivative of constant sign and magnitude at least \(\eta n\).
Consequently each such violating component has length \(O_m(1/n)\) and meets \(O_m(1)\) periods.
There are only \(O_m(M)\) components and choices of indices, so at most \(C_mM\) periods are flagged.

On any other full period inside \(J\) a maximizing chart chosen at one point persists throughout, with every ratio bounded by \(3\) and its normalized derivatives through order \(m\) bounded by \(2\eta\).
Divide the diagonal by its chart denominator after the common normalization.
In the fast angle the result has the form \(\sum_{j=0}^m w_j(v)e^{ijv}\), with \(w_j\) recovered by applying the fixed inverse rotation to the real ratio vector and combining the resulting pairs, including the factor \(1/2\).
The Euclidean norm of this vector of complex coefficients is between \(1/2\) and \(3\sqrt p/2\), since one real ratio is identically one.
Each normalized derivative of its coefficients through order \(m\) is \(O_m(\eta)\).
The modulus of the chart denominator is comparable, within constants depending on \(m\), to the largest original normalized coordinate.
The [finite-type estimate on a good period](Yin_C_G0345_v327.md#finite-type) therefore gives, for \(G(t)=F_0(e^{it},e^{i(nt+\phi)})\),
\[
  \frac{|\{t\text{ in the period}: |G(t)|/A(t)\le w\}|}
       {2\pi/n}\ll_m w^{1/m},\qquad 0<w\le1 .                      \tag{9}
\]
Indeed freezing the coefficients changes derivatives through order \(m\) by \(O_m(\eta)\) over a full period.
For the frozen shape the inverse Vandermonde matrix in \((ij)^k\), \(0\le j,k\le m\), bounds some derivative of order at most \(m\) away from zero at each point.
Its real or imaginary part stays separated from zero on a sufficiently short fixed interval, using the bounded next derivative of the frozen shape and the perturbation estimate.
Order zero excludes small values, and the [divided-difference estimate](Yin_C_G0345_v324.md#orders) at positive order gives (9).
In particular at least half of each such period satisfies \(|G(t)|\ge c_m A(t)\).

<a id="blocks"></a>
## Integrating the logarithm where ratios change rapidly

On the doubly infinite grid of period indices mark the flagged full periods inside \(J\), at most \(C_mM\) marks.
Use the ordinary dyadic partitions of this integer grid into intervals of \(2^k\) consecutive indices, \(k\ge0\).
Take the maximal dyadic blocks with marked fraction greater than \(1/4\), pass to their parents, and keep the inclusion-maximal parents.
These final blocks are disjoint and cover the marks.
Each has marked fraction at most \(1/4\), by maximality of the child, and greater than \(1/8\), since it contains such a child.
Their total real length is \(O_m(M/n)\).
If there are no marks, no blocks are needed.
Choose \(D_m\) large enough that every block has length \(l\le r/100\), and each period has length at most \(r/100\).
For any of the blocks meeting \([x-8r,x+8r]\), all its periods lie inside \(J\), so its unmarked periods there satisfy (9).
There is a measurable subset \(S\) of that block of length at least \(3l/8\) on which \(|G|\ge c_m A\).

Reapply (8), now on the disk of radius \(16l\) about the midpoint of this block.
It lies inside the larger disk in (7), so its zero mass is \(O(M)\).
The average Green estimate on \(S\) gives a point \(\xi\in S\) with \(e^{-H(\xi)}A(\xi)\ge e^{-C_mM}\).
The analytic function \(\widetilde G=e^{-h_{16l}}G\) therefore has \(\log|\widetilde G(\xi)|\ge-C_m M\).
On the disk its logarithmic modulus is at most \(O_m(1+nl)=O_m(M)\), since all the normalized coordinates are bounded by one and the fast factors cost at most \(16mnl\) in logarithm.
The disk about \(\xi\) of radius \(8l\) stays within this disk.
The [disk Jensen and Blaschke estimate](Yin_C_G0345_v325.md#clusters) now gives
\[
                      \int_{\rm block}\log^-|\widetilde G|\,dt
                                               \ll_m M l.          \tag{10}
\]
In detail Jensen bounds by \(O_m(M)\) the number of zeros within \(5l\) of \(\xi\).
Choose a concentric disk of radius between \(4l\) and \(5l\) avoiding boundary zeros, and divide out the disk Blaschke factors for the zeros inside it.
The remaining zero-free function has log modulus bounded above by \(C_mM\) and at the anchor bounded below by \(-C_mM\), since dividing by the factors increases its modulus there.
Harnack bounds its negative logarithm on the block by \(O_m(M)\).
Each removed zero has negative logarithm of its factor bounded by \(\log^+(6l/|t-\zeta|)\) there, with integral \(O(l)\).
This proves (10).

On the block \(e^{-H}A\le1\), so the loss from clipping \(\log(|G|/A)\) below at \(\log u\), \(0<u<1\), is bounded by \(\log^-|\widetilde G|\).
Summing (10) over the relevant blocks costs \(O_m(M^2/n)\).
The good periods meeting the cover arc cost \(O_m((r+1/n)u^{1/m})\) by integrating (9) in \(dw/w\) up to \(u\).
Summing these nonnegative losses over centers and using (6), \(\sum r_i=O(1)\), and \(nr_i\ge1\), bounds the full diagonal clipping loss by
\[
                     O_m\bigl(d(1+I)/n+u^{1/m}\bigr).             \tag{11}
\]

## Completing the comparison and removing common content

The common real baseline \(\log A(t)\) cancels in the difference of the two means.
At fixed base point the normalized fiber has bounded coefficients and one of modulus at least \(1/2\), so the constant-coefficient case of (9) gives independent clipping cost \(O_m(u^{1/m})\).
The [clipped grid estimate](Yin_C_G0345_v327.md#quadrature) applies directly to the positive \(A(t)\), since it uses only the normalized ratio and the total variation at fixed fast angle.
Indeed the maximizing-coordinate changes, clipping crossings and extrema of each squared ratio are cut out by \(O_m(1)\) trigonometric polynomials of degree \(O(d)\), ignoring identities and breaking identical ties consistently.
The clipped range has length \(O_m(\log(2/u))\), giving variation \(O_m(d\log(2/u))\).
Integrating the variation error of the shifted \(n\)-point grid over fast angles compares the two clipped means within \(O_m(D^{-1}\log(2/u))\).
Taking \(u=D^{-m}\) and applying (11) proves (1).
If \(C_m/D\le1/2\), the lower bound for \(\mu_\phi\) first gives \(I\le2\mu_\phi+O_m(D^{-1}\log(2D))\), proving the version with \(\mu_\phi\) on the right.
Reinserting \(h\) leaves the difference unchanged and increases the two logarithmic means by the same nonnegative amount.

For the primitive integral row the [bounded partial-degree gap](Yin_C_G0345_v156.md#statement) gives \(I\ge c_m>0\) in the presence of a \(Y\)-dependent geometric component other than a torsion translate of a subtorus.
Choose \(D_m\) so large that (1) then gives \(\mu_0\ge c_m/2\).
If every \(Y\)-dependent component is a torsion translate, the [horizontal factorization](Yin_C_G0345_v156.md#horizontal) gives a product of factors \(\Phi_q(X^aY^b)\), \(b>0\), up to a Laurent monomial and a base factor.
The base factor is a unit because each torsion factor has monomial-unit lowest \(Y\)-coefficient and the primitive rows have gcd one including integer content.
Additivity of width gives \(|a|\varphi(q)\le2d\) for each factor, so \(a+nb\ne0\) for \(n>2d\).
The phase-zero diagonal of every such factor has measure zero logarithmically, giving \(\mu_0=0\).
With a single occupied row, the primitive row is already a Laurent unit and the same conclusion holds.
This proves (2).
For an irreducible phase-zero diagonal with at least two occupied rows, the separated primitive diagonal has at least two nonzero terms, hence is a nonunit.
Thus \(h\) must be a unit by irreducibility in \(\mathbb Z[X^{\pm1}]\).
