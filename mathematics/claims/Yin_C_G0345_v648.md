# Prime-power gains for binomial covers of a reduction

<a id="statement"></a>
## A lower bound for the total cost of a cover

Let \(P\in\mathbb Z[X]\) be monic of degree \(d\ge1\), with nonzero constant and no root of unity among its zeros.
Put \(m=\log M(P)\), \(r_p(q)=\deg\gcd(\bar P,X^q-1)\) in characteristic \(p\), and
\[
                 H(q)=d^{-1}\log|\operatorname{Res}(P,X^q-1)|.
\]
For \(n\ge2\) set \(C_n=\binom n2\), \(D_n=n(n^2-1)/6\), and
\[
               T_{n,p}=\sum_{h=1}^{n-1}(n-h)(1+v_p(h)).
\]
For arbitrary positive periods \(q_1,\ldots,q_k\), \(k\ge1\), one has
\[
 \frac{m}{d}\sum_{j=1}^k q_j
 \ \ge\ \sup_{n\ge2}
 \frac{\displaystyle\sum_p T_{n,p}\log p
                  \sum_{j=1}^k r_p(q_j)/d-\tfrac{kn}{2}\log n}{D_n}.       \tag{1}
\]
Repeated factors of the reductions count with their multiplicities in the gcds.
The [prime-power rank inequality](#lifting) gives a refinement when those multiplicities grow across the successive factors of \(X^{hq}-1\).

If \(\bar P\mid \prod_{j=1}^k(X^{q_j}-1)\) at a prime \(p\), define
\[
          F_{k,p}=\sup_{n\ge2}
           \frac{T_{n,p}\log p-\tfrac{kn}{2}\log n}{D_n}.
\]
Then \(m\sum_j q_j/d\ge F_{k,p}\).
In characteristic two the exact value of this expression at \(k=2\) is
\[
              F_{2,2}=\frac{59\log2-9\log9}{120}=0.1760055\ldots.          \tag{2}
\]
For the same two-binomial hypothesis Hakimoglu-Brown obtains the larger coefficient \(\log(5/2)/4=0.2290726\ldots\). [Corollary 2.7, equation (14)](../audits/literature/HB26/Yin_U001.md#hb26-u001)
For each fixed prime \(p\), as \(k\to\infty\),
\[
             F_{k,p}\sim\frac{3}{4k\log k}
                       \left(\frac{p\log p}{p-1}\right)^2.               \tag{3}
\]
In particular the binary leading coefficient is \(3(\log 2)^2\), four times the one obtained from the [rank bound using only the original periods](Yin_C_G0345_v646.md#cover).
Both constants here refer to the displayed estimates for a supplied cover.
The existence of covers with bounded total period relative to \(d\) on unrestricted inputs remains unresolved.

<a id="lifting"></a>
## Ranks of the additional cyclotomic factors

For \(a\ge1\) put \(r_{p,a}(q)=\deg\gcd(\bar P,(X^q-1)^{p^{a-1}(p-1)})\) in characteristic \(p\).
For every \(h,q\ge1\),
\[
 H(hq)\ge H(q)+\frac1d\sum_{p\mid h}\log p
                              \sum_{a=1}^{v_p(h)}r_{p,a}(q).           \tag{4}
\]
Indeed \(X^{hq}-1\) is the product of \(X^q-1\) with the integer polynomials \(\Phi_e(X^q)\) for \(e\mid h,\ e>1\).
Every resultant with \(P\) in this factorization is a nonzero integer.
For \(e=p^a\), the geometric sum \(\Phi_{p^a}(Y)\) reduces to \((Y-1)^{p^{a-1}(p-1)}\) modulo \(p\).
The [kernel and determinant argument](Yin_C_G0345_v646.md#proof) applied to multiplication by \(\Phi_{p^a}(X^q)\) on \(\mathbb Z[X]/P\) therefore gives a factor \(p^{r_{p,a}(q)}\) in its resultant.
The integers \(p^a\) selected in (4) are different divisors of \(h\).
Multiplicativity of the resultant gives (4), even if \(p\mid q\) or \(\bar P\) is inseparable.

Each \(r_{p,a}(q)\ge r_p(q)\), and the original factor gives \(dH(q)\ge\sum_p r_p(q)\log p\).
It follows that
\[
             H(hq)\ge\frac1d\sum_p (1+v_p(h))r_p(q)\log p.            \tag{5}
\]
For instance \(P=X^2+3\), \(p=2\), \(q=1\) have \(r_2(1)=1\) and \(r_{2,2}(1)=2\).
Here \(H(1)=\log 2\) and \(H(4)=3\log2\).
At \(h=4\), (4) adds at least \(\tfrac32\log2\) to \(H(1)\), whereas replacing both new ranks by \(r_2(1)\) adds only \(\log2\).

<a id="average"></a>
## Summing the gains over all differences

The [consecutive Vandermonde estimate](Yin_C_G0345_v646.md#proof) on the sample \(0,q,\ldots,(n-1)q\) gives
\[
        \sum_{h=1}^{n-1}(n-h)H(hq)
            \le \frac n2\log n+\frac{qm}{d}D_n .
\]
The factor \(n-h\) counts the occurrences of the difference \(hq\), and the maximum of the absolute determinant on the unit circle is \(n^{n/2}\).
One can insert (4) with the actual \(H(q)\) and ranks for each period.
Inserting (5), summing over periods and then taking the supremum gives (1).
Only finitely many primes contribute, by the nonzero resultant at each supplied period.
At one prime the per-period lower bound as a function of \(r_p(q)/d\) is a supremum of increasing affine functions and is convex.
Thus one may optimize separately for each period, or apply Jensen's inequality at their mean rank.
Under the cover hypothesis the sum of their ranks is at least \(d\), by adding the multiplicities truncated at each factor of \(\bar P\).
The bound at the mean rank \(1/k\), multiplied by \(k\), is \(F_{k,p}\).
Silverman's [weighted fixed-period estimate](../audits/literature/Sil12/Yin_U001.md#fixed-period-valuation) retains the valuation mass at \(q\) on every multiple.
Here the factors indexed by the prime powers dividing each multiplier contribute the additional terms in (4).

<a id="optimization"></a>
## Evaluating the two-period bound and its many-period asymptotic

Write \(s_p(t)\) for the sum of the base-\(p\) digits of \(t\).
Summing \(v_p(h)\) first over \(1\le h\le t\) gives \(\sum_{\ell\ge1}\lfloor t/p^\ell\rfloor=(t-s_p(t))/(p-1)\), by expanding \(t\) in base \(p\).
Consequently
\[
 T_{n,p}=\frac{p}{p-1}C_n-\frac{1}{p-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}s_p(t).
\]
For \(k=p=2\), \(T_{n,2}\le n(n-1)\) bounds the objective by \(6\log2/(n+1)\).
For \(n\ge23\) this is at most \(\log2/4<F_{2,2}(9)\), where \(F_{2,2}(n)\) denotes the objective before taking its supremum.
The last strict inequality is \(2^{29}>9^9\).
For \(2\le n\le22\) the successive \(T_{n,2}\) are
\[
 1,4,8,15,23,33,44,59,75,93,112,134,157,182,208,239,271,305,340,378,417.
\]
For these values the integer comparison
\[
 2^{720 T_{n,2}}9^{9n(n^2-1)}
       \ \le\ n^{720n}2^{59n(n^2-1)}
\]
is strict except at \(n=9\).
This comparison is exactly \(F_{2,2}(n)\le F_{2,2}(9)\), proving (2).

For fixed \(p\) let \(b=p\log p/(p-1)\).
The digit sum bound \(s_p(t)=O_p(\log(2t))\) gives
\[
 \frac{T_{n,p}\log p-\tfrac{kn}{2}\log n}{D_n}
  =\frac{3b}{n+1}-\frac{3k\log n}{n^2-1}
                       +O_p\!\left(\frac{\log n}{n^2}\right),
\]
where the error is nonpositive and independent of \(k\).
A positive value forces \(n-1>k\log n/b\).
Thus \(n\) is at least a fixed positive multiple of \(k\), and for sufficiently large \(k\) one has \(n>k\).
Every positive value is then at most \(3b/n-3k\log k/n^2\), whose maximum over \(1/n\ge0\) is \(3b^2/(4k\log k)\).
Taking \(n=\lceil 2k\log k/b\rceil\) in the expansion attains that value up to relative error \(o(1)\), proving (3).
For the original ranks alone the same objective has \(C_n\log p\) in place of \(T_{n,p}\log p\).
Its expansion has \(b=\log p\) and zero digit-sum error, so the same maximization gives \(3(\log p)^2/(4k\log k)\).
