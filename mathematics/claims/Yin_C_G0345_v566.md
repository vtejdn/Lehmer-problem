# Dyadic allocation of the positive exterior root and the short roots at three

<a id="statement"></a>
## A signed congruence for each rational factor

Let \(k=2^s\) with \(s\ge2\), let \(n>k\) be odd, and put \(D=n+k\) and \(F=X^D-2(X^n+X^k)+1\).
Let \(P\) be a monic reciprocal rational irreducible factor of even degree, and let \(N_P\) be the norm of \(2-x^k\) for a root \(x\) in its root field.
Put \(b_P=1\) if \(X^k+1\mid P\bmod3\) and zero otherwise.
Let \(r\) be the unique root of \(F\) above \(2^{1/k}\) on the positive real axis, and put \(\epsilon_P=-1\) when \(P(r)=0\) and \(\epsilon_P=1\) otherwise.
Then \(N_P=\epsilon_P3^{kb_P}\).
Write \(S_P\subset\mu_D(\overline{\mathbb F}_2)\) for the roots of \(P\bmod2\), and \([u]\) for the odd-order root of unity lifting \(u\) in the maximal unramified dyadic extension.
Set \(c_P=\sum_{u\in S_P}u^3\in\mathbb F_2\), interpreted as zero or one.
The dyadic subset determines both allocations by
\[
 B_P:=\prod_{u\in S_P}(1-2[u])\equiv\epsilon_P\bigl(1+4k(b_P+c_P)\bigr)\pmod{8k}.
\]
In particular the product is \(\epsilon_P\) modulo \(4k\), and any subset giving neither sign is excluded.
As \(n/k\to\infty\), all irreducible factors other than \(X+1\) are eventually reciprocal of even degree, and a unique factor receives all \(k\) short roots modulo three.
This applies to the [primitive rough-exponent family](../ledger/Yin_L_G0462_v284.md#exponents).

If \(S_P\) consists of all nontrivial \(m\)-th roots for \(m\mid D\) with \(m\ge s+2\), then \(P\) contains \(r\), and if \(m\ge s+3\) it also receives all short roots modulo three.
Suppose instead that \(S_P\) consists of full sets of primitive roots of orders \(m>1\) whose prime divisors are at least \(s+2\).
Then \(P\) contains \(r\) precisely when the number of squarefree orders in the union is odd.
If all their prime divisors are at least \(s+3\), the same parity determines \(b_P\).

<a id="three"></a>
## The norm and its sign

Modulo three, \(F=(X^k+1)(X^n+1)\).
The first factor has simple roots of order \(2k\), disjoint from the second since \(n\) is odd.
Cubing and inversion act transitively on these roots, since three has order \(2^{s-1}\) modulo \(2k\) by \(v_2(3^{2^i}-1)=i+2\) for \(i\ge1\), and \(-1\) lies outside its subgroup modulo eight.
The valuation formula follows by successive squaring.
Thus the reciprocal factor contains all or none of these roots modulo three.
The full parent has absolute resultant \(3^k\) with \(X^k-2\), since \(F\) evaluates to \(-3\) at every root of that binomial.
A factor contributes a multiple of three exactly when its reduction meets the short roots.
If \(P\) meets them all, its complement meets none because each is simple in \(F\bmod3\).
Multiplicativity gives \(|N_P|=3^{kb_P}\).

For a real root \(z\) with \(z^k>2\), the equation \((z^k-2)(z^n-2)=3\) gives \(z^n>2\), hence \(z>2^{1/k}\) because \(n\) is odd.
On this interval the product on the left increases strictly from zero to infinity.
Only the resulting root \(r\) can contribute a negative factor to the norm, and nonreal conjugate pairs have positive product.
This gives the sign \(\epsilon_P\).

On the unit circle the equation is \(X^n=(2X^k-1)/(X^k-2)\), where both sides have modulus one.
The right side has winding number \(k\) because its numerator has \(k\) zeros inside and its denominator has none.
The difference of continuous arguments gains \(2\pi(n-k)\) in a full turn, so there are at least \(n-k\) distinct circle roots by the intermediate value theorem.
At most \(2k\) roots are off the circle.
The [four-term degree bound](Yin_C_G0345_v250.md#statement) gives degree \(\gg\sqrt{Dk}\) for every nontorsion factor, so each has a circle conjugate and is reciprocal once \(n/k\) is sufficiently large.
Cyclotomic factors are reciprocal as well, and the only possible linear factor is \(X+1\), which misses the short roots.
This proves uniqueness of their rational factor at large slope.

<a id="two"></a>
## Two successive dyadic precisions

Modulo two, \(F=X^D-1\) is separable, so its roots lift individually by Hensel's lemma in the unramified extension splitting \(X^D-1\).
For the root \(x_u\) lifting \(u\), write \(x_u=[u]\,(1+2a)\) with \(a\) integral, and put \(v=u^k\).
Expansion in \(F(x_u)/2=0\) modulo two gives \(\bar a=1+v+v^{-1}\), since \([u]^D=1\), \(D\) is odd and \(u^n=v^{-1}\).
The first squaring gives \((1+2a)^2=1+4(a+a^2)\), and successive squaring gives
\[
 (1+2a)^k\equiv1+2k(a+a^2)\pmod{4k}.
\]
Reciprocity, even degree and constant coefficient one imply \(N_P=\prod_{u\in S_P}(1-2x_u^k)\), by inversion and the product of all roots of \(P\).
Replacing \(x_u^k\) by \([u]^k\) gives \(B_P\) since raising to \(k\) permutes \(S_P\) by Frobenius.
This product is in \(\mathbb Z_2\) by Frobenius invariance.
Each factor ratio \((1-2x_u^k)/(1-2[u]^k)\) is one modulo \(4k\), and the reduction modulo two of its difference from one divided by \(4k\) is
\[
 v(\bar a+\bar a^2)=v^2+1+v^3+v^{-1}.
\]
The even size of \(S_P\) cancels the constant terms, and inversion and squaring each permute the set, so the contributions of \(v^{-1}\) and \(v^2\) cancel.
The remaining sum is \(c_P\), fixed by Frobenius.
Thus \(N_P/B_P\equiv1+4k c_P\bmod8k\), and \(3^k\equiv1+4k\bmod8k\) by the exact valuation above.
These identities give the claimed congruence.

For all nontrivial \(m\)-th roots the product is \(2^m-1\) by the geometric series and inversion, since \(m\) is odd.
The sum of their cubes in characteristic two is one for \(m>3\), again by the geometric series.
For all primitive roots of odd order \(m>1\), the product is \(\Phi_m(2)=\prod_{d\mid m}(1-2^d)^{\mu(m/d)}\) by inversion and the cyclotomic product formula.
Under the prime divisor hypothesis each term with \(d>1\) is one modulo \(4k\), while the \(d=1\) term is \((-1)^{\mu(m)}\).
The stronger size condition gives the same signed residue modulo \(8k\), so \(b_P=c_P\).
Under that condition cubing permutes each primitive set, and its root sum is \(\mu(m)\) by inversion of the geometric series over divisors.
This proves both parity assertions.

## An excluded degree at exponents 64 and 4927

In the [degree-4991 example](../ledger/Yin_L_G0462_v284.md#sample), degree six passes the separate reciprocal degree tests at two and three.
At two the six nontrivial seventh roots form one orbit under doubling and inversion.
At three the primitive roots of order 26 split into two orbits of size six under cubing and inversion, since \(3^3\equiv1\bmod26\) and \(-1\notin\langle3\rangle\).
Each orbit at three divides the reduction since \(13\mid4927\).
At two the seventh roots are the only reciprocal subset of size six, as the orders divide \(4991=7\cdot23\cdot31\), the doubling orders at 23 and 31 are 11 and 5 respectively, and inversion doubles the latter orbit size.
The seventh-root product is 127, which is neither signed residue modulo 256.
This excludes degree six for reciprocal factors and leaves the larger factor degrees on the infinite family open.
