# Bounded linear patterns in critical sparse tests for arbitrary shrinking measures

<a id="statement"></a>
## Restrictions on the actual support of an exact subsum

Fix \(r\ge2,\ C>0,\ 0<\eta\le1\).
Let \(\alpha\) run through nonzero algebraic integers with \(m=\log M(\alpha)\to0^+\), and put \(d=\deg\alpha,\ h=m/d\).
At each index let \(Q\) be a complex Laurent polynomial with at most \(r\) terms, shifted to have true minimum exponent zero and normalized to coefficient norm one, of width \(Wh\le C\) in height units.
There is a fixed \(\epsilon_0>0\) such that if \(|Q(\sigma\alpha)|\le\epsilon_0\) on at least \(\eta d\) original embeddings, the following holds eventually.

Some subset of the actual exponents has size \(p\) with \(4\le p\le r\).
In a fixed ordering its members have the form
\[
        e_0+v\cdot w_1,\ldots,e_0+v\cdot w_p,\quad
        v\in\mathbb Z^q,\quad 2\le q\le\lfloor p/2\rfloor,              \tag{1}
\]
where \(w_1=0,w_2,\ldots,w_p\) span \(\mathbb Q^q\) and this entire tuple belongs to a finite list depending only on \(r,C,\eta\).
The indicated original exponents support an exact relation at \(\alpha\) with no proper vanishing subsum.
Its nonzero coefficients come from a finite list in \(\mathbb Q(\alpha)\) and may differ from those of \(Q\).
In (1) their convex hull in \(\mathbb R^q\), meaning the convex hull of the \(w_j\), has a full-dimensional centrally symmetric polytope as a Minkowski summand.
This bounds the dimension needed for the actual exponents of the subsum, including those outside the selected irreducible factor.

In particular for \(r=4\) the actual support is eventually \(0,a,b,a+b\) with \(0<a<b\), for arbitrary shrinking inputs of any signature.
Let \(\iota\) denote the root-field involution taking \(\alpha\) to \(\alpha^{-1}\).
There are \(\lambda,\nu\in\mathbb Q(\alpha)^\times\) from fixed finite lists such that
\[
  \alpha^b=\nu\lambda\,\frac{\alpha^a-1/\iota(\lambda)}{\alpha^a-\lambda},
       \qquad \nu\iota(\nu)=1,\qquad \lambda\iota(\lambda)\ne1.         \tag{2}
\]
Conversely, a relation (2) with the two parameters and their involution images in a field \(E\subseteq\mathbb Q(\alpha)\) gives an exact four-term polynomial vanishing at at least \(d/[E:\mathbb Q]\) original embeddings.
The [Salem theorem](Yin_C_G0345_v743.md#statement) obtains stronger real and height restrictions when there is a unique positive exterior root.

<a id="descent"></a>
## Finite descent while retaining every term of a minimal subsum

The inputs are eventually reciprocal units by [Smyth's gap](../audits/literature/Smy71/Yin_U001.md#smy71-u001).
Every nonzero power has degree at least \(e_*=\min_{j\ge1}\deg(\alpha^j)\to\infty\) by [norm descent](Yin_C_G0345_v739.md#volume).
Take a common positive threshold from the [critical finite-list theorem](Yin_C_G0345_v740.md#statement) at all term counts \(2,\ldots,r\), smaller than one to exclude monomials.
All evaluation rows contain one, so the absolute smallness hypothesis implies its relative one.
From the exact relation select a nonempty vanishing subsum minimal by inclusion.
Its \(p\) nonzero coefficients belong to a fixed finite list in the root field and all exponents are distinct.
Subtracting its first exponent \(e_0\), lift it first to \(L=c_1+\sum_{j=2}^p c_jY_{j-1}\) in rank \(p-1\), with specialization vector given by the exponent differences.
Write \(u\) for the current specialization vector.
As in the [bounded relation descent](Yin_C_G0345_v236.md#source), keep the entire \(L\) at every restriction.
Its specialization has exactly the original \(p\) distinct exponents and therefore is nonzero.

Factor \(L\) over the algebraic closure and choose an irreducible factor \(f\) through \(\alpha^u\).
There are finitely many choices up to Laurent units at each node.
Choose an extension of \(\iota\) to the algebraic closure.
Then \(f^\iota(Y^{-1})\) vanishes at \(\alpha^u\) as well.
Its normalized coefficient vector has only finitely many conjugates, even though the extension of \(\iota\) can vary.
If the two factors are associates, their common Newton polytope up to translation is centrally symmetric, and the descent at this node stops.
Otherwise they are geometrically irreducible nonassociates, and the common torus zero has an irreducible component through it of codimension at least two.
By [the bounded-character theorem of Bombieri, Masser and Zannier](Yin_C_G0345_v236.md#source), some nonzero bounded integer vector annihilates \(u\), the bound depending on that component.
We use the theorem for a nontorsion \(\alpha\) and no torsion twists.
Restrict \(L\) modulo the primitive vector obtained by removing its gcd, using a fixed basis of the quotient lattice.
The specialization factors through this rank-one reduction and its exponents stay distinct.
Each node has finitely many child nodes and the dimension decreases, so all the terminal configurations form a finite list.
In dimension one two nonassociate geometric factors could not meet in the torus.
A rank zero specialization cannot support two distinct powers with a vanishing sum.

At a terminal node let \(\Lambda\) be the saturated sublattice spanned rationally by the exponent differences of \(f\), of rank \(q\).
Multiply \(f\) by a Laurent unit to put it in the group ring of \(\Lambda\).
In \(L=fB\), group the terms by their coset modulo \(\Lambda\).
Each nonempty group is itself divisible by \(f\), since multiplication by \(f\) preserves each coset part of \(B\).
Each such group vanishes upon evaluation at \(\alpha^u\).
The defining subsum has no proper vanishing subsum and its terms never collide, so **all its terms occupy just one coset**.
Taking that coset's first exponent as origin gives the lattice vectors \(w_j\) in (1).
Their span is the whole \(q\)-space because the Newton polytope of their sum has the \(q\)-dimensional Newton polytope of \(f\) as a summand.
This last assertion follows by multiplying initial forms in every direction.
A \(q\)-dimensional centrally symmetric polytope has at least \(2q\) vertices, since its opposite vertex differences span dimension \(q\).
A Minkowski sum has at least as many vertices as each summand by exposing each vertex with a generic linear functional.
Hence \(p\ge2q\).
All basis choices can be fixed at the finitely many terminal nodes, giving the finite list in (1).
If \(q=1\), a nonzero power of \(\alpha\) is a root of one of finitely many one-variable factors of bounded degree with coefficients from a finite list.
A zero power would specialize the vanishing factor identically to zero, which is impossible.
This contradicts \(e_*\to\infty\), so eventually \(q\ge2\).

<a id="four"></a>
## The quadrilateral and its root-field parameters

At \(p=4\), necessarily \(q=2\).
The central polygon of the factor has at least two independent pairs of opposite edges.
The polygon of the four points has the same two pairs of edge directions by the exposed faces of a Minkowski sum.
It has at most four vertices, hence is a parallelogram and all four support points are its vertices.
The sum of opposite vertices is equal before and after the projection (1).
Since the projected exponents are distinct, their increasing order is \(0,a,b,a+b\) for the normalized four-term test.

The exact relation now reads \(Y=R(X)\) at \(X=\alpha^a,\ Y=\alpha^b\), with \(R(Z)=\gamma(Z-\mu)/(Z-\lambda)\).
Indeed all four coefficients are nonzero, and a linear denominator cannot vanish at \(X\) for large indices since its coefficient ratios come from a finite list.
A proportional numerator and denominator would give a fixed-list value of \(\alpha^b\), again impossible.
Thus \(\lambda,\mu,\gamma\) are nonzero members of finite lists in the root field, and \(\mu\ne\lambda\).
Applying the involution at the distinguished point yields \(R(X)R^\iota(1/X)=1\).
Clearing denominators gives a bounded-degree polynomial at \(X\) over a fixed finite coefficient list.
The degree of \(X\) tends to infinity, so this is a rational function identity eventually.
Its zeros and poles give \(\mu=1/\iota(\lambda)\).
Taking the value at infinity gives \(\gamma\iota(\gamma)=\lambda\iota(\lambda)\), hence \(\nu=\gamma/\lambda\) gives (2).
Applying each embedding fixing the parameters to the same equation proves the converse after clearing the denominator.
