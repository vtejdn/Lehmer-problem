# Exactly two quadratic trace perturbations descend on the plastic Salem family

<a id="statement"></a>
## The minimum perturbation degree and both descents

Take \(f(z)=z^3-z-1,\ f^*(z)=z^3 f(1/z)\), and let \(\theta>1\) be the real root of \(f\).
Use the [full Salem inputs](Yin_C_G0345_v129.md#progression) \(q_N=z^N f+f^*=z^m S_N(z+z^{-1})\), where \(N\) is sufficiently large and odd, \(N\not\equiv7\bmod8,\ N\not\equiv21\bmod30\), and \(2m=N+3\).
Their Salem numbers satisfy \(\theta<\sigma_N<4/3\) and \(\sigma_N\to\theta\).
For \(R\in\mathbb Z[X]\) of degree at most two, form the entire polynomial
\[
             P_{N,R}(z)=z^m(S_N+R)(z+z^{-1}).
\]
For every sufficiently large admissible \(N\), the complete list of \(R\) for which \(P_{N,R}\) has a Salem factor with exterior root strictly smaller than \(\sigma_N\) is
\[
                    X^2-2,\qquad -X^2+X+4 .                         \tag{1}
\]
Thus the minimum degree is two, allowing the coefficients to depend arbitrarily on \(N\).
Each of the two full polynomials is a single smaller Salem minimal polynomial times cyclotomic factors, so its full Mahler measure decreases.
The Salem degrees are at most \(m\) and \(m+1\), respectively, compared with \(2m\) for the input.
Both smaller Salem numbers tend to \(\theta\) from below.

More precisely, among nonzero polynomials of degree at most two, (1) and their negatives are exactly those producing any Salem factor of measure below \(4/3\) for all sufficiently large admissible \(N\), with one threshold uniform over the polynomials.
Each negative gives a unique Salem factor strictly larger than the input and tending to the same limit from above.
The coordinator proposed the polynomial envelope, the low-degree test and the shorter signed Salem constructions.
The contributor checked uniformity for changing coefficients, the exact envelope, the sign choices and the degree reduction.

<a id="low-degree"></a>
## Every target below \(4/3\) has three distinct neutral traces

For a Salem number \(\tau<4/3\) the exterior trace \(T=\tau+\tau^{-1}\) lies in \((2,25/12)\).
Let \(D\) be its monic trace minimal polynomial.
A genuine Salem number has at least one neutral conjugate pair, so \(\deg D\ge2\).
If \(\deg D=2\), its second root lies in \((-2,2)\) and \(0<|D(2)|<4/12<1\), impossible.
If \(\deg D=3\), write \(x_1,x_2\in(-2,2)\) for its neutral traces and \(u_i=2+x_i\).
Then \(0<-D(2)<16/12\), hence \(D(2)=-1\) and \((4-u_1)(4-u_2)=1/(T-2)>12\).
The arithmetic-geometric mean inequality gives \(u_1+u_2<8-4\sqrt3<11/10\).
It follows that \(0<-D(-2)=(T+2)u_1u_2<(49/12)(11/20)^2<2\), so \(D(-2)=-1\).
Thus \(u_1+u_2\ge2/\sqrt{T+2}>9/10\).
Writing \(D=X^3+aX^2+bX+c\), the two endpoint values give \(b=-4,\ c=-1-4a\).
The sum of the roots gives \(a=4-T-u_1-u_2\in(0,2)\), hence \(a=1\).
The resulting cubic \(X^3+X^2-4X-5\) has discriminant \(-23\), contradicting its three distinct real roots.
Therefore \(\deg D\ge4\), giving at least three distinct neutral traces.

<a id="envelope"></a>
## A necessary polynomial bound on the whole trace interval

At every neutral trace \(x=z+z^{-1}\) of a Salem factor of \(P_{N,R}\), the circle identity gives
\[
                  |R(x)|=|q_N(z)|\le 2|f(z)|\le6 .
\]
Uniformly over all Salem factors below \(4/3\), three such trace points can be chosen with their pairwise distances bounded below by an absolute constant.
For sufficiently large factor degrees the [angular count for irreducible units](Yin_C_G0345_v108.md#phases) at log measure bounded by \(\log(4/3)\) supplies conjugates in each of three fixed small disjoint arcs of the upper semicircle, for example around \(\pi/4,\pi/2,3\pi/4\), with positively separated trace images.
All conjugates in those arcs are neutral for a Salem polynomial.
At bounded degrees there are only finitely many targets by the coefficient bounds for monic integer polynomials at bounded measure.
Each has three distinct neutral traces by the preceding argument, so the same separation conclusion follows by taking a finite minimum.
Interpolation of a degree-at-most-two polynomial at the three points now bounds all its coefficients by an absolute constant.
Indeed the Lagrange numerators have bounded coefficients on \([-2,2]\) and both factors in every denominator are bounded away from zero.
Thus the successful integer polynomials belong to one finite set independent of \(N\).

At \(x=z+z^{-1}\) on the circle put \(H(x)=|f(z)|^2=5+4x-x^2-x^3\).
If a fixed \(R\) violates
\[
                      R(x)^2\le4H(x)\qquad(-2\le x\le2),             \tag{2}
\]
then it violates the inequality on a fixed open subinterval inside \((-2,2)\).
Its inverse angular arc has no unit root of \(P_{N,R}\) for any \(N\), so the same angular count bounds the degrees of its possible Salem factors below \(4/3\).
There are again only finitely many targets.
For any fixed such \(\tau>1\), put \(u=\tau^{(N-3)/2}\) and \(T=\tau+\tau^{-1}\).
The equation \(P_{N,R}(\tau)=0\) is equivalent to
\[
                      f(\tau)u^2+R(T)u+f(1/\tau)=0 .
\]
Here \(f(1/\tau)<0\), since \(0<1/\tau<1\), so the polynomial is nonzero even when \(R(T)=0\).
Its at most two roots give at most two admissible exponents for this pair.
Finiteness of the coefficient set makes (2) necessary at every sufficiently large admissible exponent, uniformly in \(R\).

<a id="quadratics"></a>
## All integer polynomials within the bound

Write \(R=aX^2+bX+c\).
Inequality (2) gives \(|4a+c|+2|b|\le2\) at \(x=\pm2\) and \(|c|\le\sqrt{20}<5\) at zero.
Thus \(|a|,|b|\le1\) and \(|c|\le4\).
At \(x=-3/2\), \(H=1/8\), so \(|R(-3/2)|\le1/\sqrt2\).
If \(a=0,b=0\) this forces \(c=0\), and if \(a=0,|b|=1\) the endpoint test gives \(c=0\), failing the last bound.
Otherwise negate \(R\) if necessary to obtain \(a=1\).
For \(b=0\) one has \(c=-4,-3,-2\), of which only \(-2\) meets the last bound.
For \(b=\pm1\) one has \(c=-4\), and only \(b=-1\) meets the bound.
Thus the only candidates other than zero are the two signs of \(A=X^2-2\) and \(B=X^2-X-4\).
They all satisfy (2), by the identities
\[
           4H-A^2=(2-X)(X+2)^3,\qquad
           4H-B^2=(4-X^2)(X+1)^2 .
\]

<a id="factorization"></a>
## Which sign produces the smaller Salem number

Set \(D_1=-A,\ D_2=B\).
Direct expansion gives \(D_l(z+z^{-1})=z^{-l}f^*(z)+z^{l-3}f(z)\) for \(l=1,2\).
Consequently for \(\varepsilon=\pm1\),
\[
     P_{N,\varepsilon D_l}(z)
       =(z^{m-l}+\varepsilon)\bigl(z^{m+l-3}f(z)+\varepsilon f^*(z)\bigr).   \tag{3}
\]
The first factor is a product of cyclotomic polynomials.
For \(k=m+l-3\) sufficiently large, the [signed Salem construction](../audits/literature/Smy15/Yin_U002.md#smy15-u002) for this nonreciprocal Pisot seed gives exactly one Salem polynomial and only cyclotomic cofactors in the second factor.
For \(\varepsilon=-1\), evaluation at \(\theta\) gives \(-f^*(\theta)=\theta^2+\theta>0\).
At each fixed \(1<s<\theta\) the value \(s^k f(s)-f^*(s)\) is eventually negative, so the unique Salem root lies below \(\theta\) and tends to it.
The polynomial \(z^k f-f^*\) has degree \(m+l\) and vanishes at \(1\).
Its Salem factor therefore has degree at most \(m+l-1\).
For \(\varepsilon=+1\), one has \(k<N\) and \(f(\sigma_N)>0\), so
\[
           \sigma_N^k f(\sigma_N)+f^*(\sigma_N)
                =(\sigma_N^k-\sigma_N^N)f(\sigma_N)<0 .
\]
The unique Salem root is strictly larger than \(\sigma_N\).
At any fixed \(s>\theta\) the value \(s^k f(s)+f^*(s)\) is eventually positive, so this root also tends to \(\theta\) and is eventually below \(4/3\).
Finally \(R=0\) produces only the original irreducible polynomial.
The sign \(\varepsilon=-1\) in (3) gives \(-D_1=A\) and \(-D_2=-B\), precisely (1).
