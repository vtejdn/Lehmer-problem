# Field radicals and common exponents on a fixed rational graph

<a id="statement"></a>
## An absolute bound as the slope increases

Let \(R\) be a nonzero algebraic rational function regular at \(1\), such that every conjugate of \(R(1)\) has modulus one.
This includes every \(R\) satisfying the rational identity \(R(X)R(1/X)=1\).
For a positive real unit \(\beta>1\) of a number field, write \(I(\beta)\) for the largest positive integer \(j\) such that \(\beta^{1/j}\in\mathbb Q(\beta)\), taking the positive root.
There are constants \(s_R\) and \(H_R\in\mathbb Z_{\ge1}\), depending only on the fixed rational function, such that
\[
       \beta^v=R(\beta^u),\qquad u,v\in\mathbb Z_{>0},\quad v/u>s_R
                 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I(\beta)\mid H_R .          \tag{1}
\]
The degree of \(\beta\), the two exponents and the multiplicative relations between poles are unrestricted.

For a concrete choice of \(H_R\), write \(R=A/B\) with relatively prime polynomials in \(\mathcal O_E[X]\) over a number field \(E\) of degree \(e\).
Put \(D=\max(\deg A,\deg B)\), and for a complex polynomial \(P\) write \(\|P\|_1\) for the sum of the absolute values of its coefficients.
The products and sums below run over all embeddings \(\sigma:E\hookrightarrow\mathbb C\), including conjugate complex embeddings separately.
Set
\[
 L=\prod_\sigma(\|A^\sigma\|_1+\|B^\sigma\|_1),\qquad
 c=\frac1e\sum_\sigma\log\max(1,\|A^\sigma\|_1,\|B^\sigma\|_1),\qquad
 N=\max(1,\lfloor L\exp(ec/2)\rfloor).
\]
One may take \(H_R=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,N)\) after a sufficiently large fixed threshold \(s_R\).
In particular this bound imposes no separate condition on \((u+v)h(\beta)\).

If \(\alpha>1\) is a real algebraic unit satisfying \(\alpha^b=R(\alpha^a)\), put \(g=\gcd(a,b)\).
Whenever \(\mathbb Q(\alpha^g)=\mathbb Q(\alpha)\) and \(b/a>s_R\), one has \(g I(\alpha)\mid H_R\).
For Salem numbers the field condition is automatic.
For a fixed finite family of maps one can use the maximum of their thresholds and the least common multiple of their respective bounds.

<a id="real"></a>
## An interval for positive solutions under all coefficient embeddings

Suppose \(s=v/u\) is large and \(t^s=R^\sigma(t)\) for \(t>1\).
There exist \(1<\lambda<\Lambda\), independent of \(s,\sigma\), with \(\lambda\le t\le\Lambda\).
In fact all the maps are regular and nonzero at \(1\), and \(\log|R^\sigma(x)|\) is continuously differentiable in a common neighborhood of \(1\) on the real line, with value zero at \(1\).
Thus \(|\log|R^\sigma(x)||\le C\log x\) on some \(1<x<\lambda\), ruling out solutions there for \(s>C\).
Beyond the moduli of all poles, \(|R^\sigma(x)|\le C' x^D\) for all sufficiently large \(x>1\) and all \(\sigma\).
Taking \(s>D+1\) bounds solutions from above as well.
For a reciprocal map the order at \(1\) of \(R(1/X)\) is the order of \(R(X)\) there, so the reciprocal identity gives order zero and \(R(1)^2=1\).
This verifies the hypotheses at \(1\) directly for that class.

<a id="intersections"></a>
## Excluding a degree-\(p\) power on the same graph

There is a fixed threshold such that \(t^s=R(t)\) and \(w^s=R^\sigma(w)\) with \(t>1,\ w=t^{1/p}\) are impossible simultaneously for \(s\) beyond it, for every prime \(p\) and every \(\sigma\).
Indeed \(w\ge\lambda\) and \(t\le\Lambda\) by the preceding bounds, so \(p\le\log\Lambda/\log\lambda\).
It suffices to treat each of finitely many primes.
If \(R\) is a monomial \(c_0X^j\), allowing \(j\in\mathbb Z\), then \(|c_0|=1\) and \(t^s=R(t)\) forces \(s=j\).
This case gives (1) vacuously at larger slopes.

Otherwise \(R\) has a zero or a pole in \(\mathbb C^\times\).
Fix one such type and let \(M>0\) be the number of its distinct locations of that type in \(\mathbb C^\times\).
All coefficient embeddings preserve this count, including when a location is a power of another.
For a simultaneous solution,
\[
                          R^\sigma(w)^p=R(w^p).                         \tag{2}
\]
The left side as a rational function of \(w\) has \(M\) distinct such locations, while the right side has \(pM\), since every nonzero complex number has \(p\) distinct \(p\)-th roots.
Thus (2) is a nonidentity and there are only finitely many solutions \(w\) away from its poles.
For each real \(w>1\) among them with \(R^\sigma(w)>0\) real, the second equation prescribes the single slope \(s=\log|R^\sigma(w)|/\log w\).
Removing this finite set of slopes for all the primes and embeddings proves the exclusion.

<a id="norm"></a>
## The integral equation and its height

For \(s>D+1\), consider the polynomial
\[
       P_{u,v}(X)=
          \prod_\sigma\big(X^vB^\sigma(X^u)-A^\sigma(X^u)\big).
\]
Its coefficients are rational algebraic integers by Galois invariance and integrality of all the input coefficients.
Each factor is nonzero because \(v>Du\).
Its degree is at most \(e(v+Du)\), and its coefficient length is at most \(L\), independently of \(u,v\).
For \(t=\beta^u\), the equation gives \(P_{u,v}(\beta)=0\).
If \(P_{u,v}(\beta^{1/p})=0\) then for \(w=t^{1/p}\) one factor gives \(w^s=R^\sigma(w)\), since the coprime numerator and denominator have no common root.
The [finite intersection argument](#intersections) gives \(P_{u,v}(\beta^{1/p})\ne0\) for every prime after one fixed threshold.

By the [prime-power norm test](Yin_C_G0345_v206.md#norm-test), each prime power \(p^j\mid I(\beta)\), with \(j\ge1\), now satisfies
\[
                      p^j\le L\exp\!\big(e(v+Du)h(\beta)/p\big).        \tag{3}
\]
For the absolute logarithmic height and any \(x\) outside the poles of \(R\), one has \(h(R(x))\le D h(x)+c\).
Indeed the product formula writes \(h(R(x))\) as the normalized sum of \(\log\max(|A(x)|,|B(x)|)\) over all places of \(E(x)\).
At a finite place integrality bounds the maximum by \(\max(1,|x|)^D\).
At an archimedean place above \(\sigma\), one can multiply this same bound by \(\max(1,\|A^\sigma\|_1,\|B^\sigma\|_1)\).
Summing gives the claimed height inequality.
At \(x=\beta^u\) it reads \((v-Du)h(\beta)\le c\).
Consequently (3) gives
\[
                      p^j\le L\exp\!\left(\frac{ec}{p}
                                                \frac{s+D}{s-D}\right).
\]
Uniformly in \(p\ge2\) the right side is bounded by a quantity tending to \(L\exp(ec/2)\) as \(s\to\infty\), so it is less than \(N+1\) for all sufficiently large \(s\).
All prime powers in \(I(\beta)\) therefore divide \(\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,N)\), proving (1).

<a id="common"></a>
## Common dilations and a graph with power-related poles

For a fixed real number field \(K\) and a positive nontorsion \(z\in K\), the positive radical exponents in \(K\) are finite by bounded degree and height, are closed under divisors and are closed under least common multiples by Bézout's identity.
Writing \(I_K(z)\) for their maximum, the [radical index under powering](Yin_C_G0345_v747.md#dilation) gives \(I_K(z^g)=gI_K(z)\).
Indeed for any allowed \(j\) at \(z^g\), Bézout gives \(z^{1/(j/\gcd(j,g))}\in K\), so \(j\mid gI_K(z)\).
Conversely the \(gI_K(z)\)-th root of \(z^g\) is already in \(K\).
Apply (1) to \(\beta=\alpha^g\) and \(u=a/g,\ v=b/g\).
The field hypothesis then identifies \(I(\beta)=gI(\alpha)\).
Every positive power of a Salem number generates its original field, since a collision of two conjugate powers would give two distinct conjugates of exterior modulus upon moving the first to the Salem number.

For example,
\[
               R(X)=\frac{(2X-1)(4X-1)}{(X-2)(X-4)}
\]
is reciprocal, with distinct positive poles \(2\) and \(4=2^2\).
Here \(E=\mathbb Q,\ D=2,\ L=30,\ c=\log15\), giving \(N=116\).
For every \(u,v\ge1\), the equation \(\beta^v=R(\beta^u)\) has a positive real algebraic-unit solution above \(4^{1/u}\).
On that interval \(R(\beta^u)\) decreases from an unbounded positive value near the left endpoint to a finite limit as \(\beta\to\infty\), while \(\beta^v\to\infty\).
The equation after clearing denominators is monic in \(\beta\) with constant term \(-1\).
Every such solution at sufficiently large slope has \(I(\beta)\mid\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,116)\).
