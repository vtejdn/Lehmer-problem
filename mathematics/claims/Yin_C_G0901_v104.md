# A coefficient character excluding the real root beyond the pole at every exponent

<a id="statement"></a>
## The all-exponent sign restriction

Let \(q\ge5,\ p=q+1,\ L=2q+1,\ M=pq\), and let \(0<u<v\) be coprime.
For a sign \(\epsilon\) and nonzero integers of magnitudes \(|a|=q,\ |b|=p\), consider
\[
 P(X)=a(1+\epsilon X^{u+v})+b(X^u+\epsilon X^v),\qquad
 s=-\operatorname{sgn}(b/a).
\]
Let \(H_L\) be the subgroup of \((\mathbb Z/M\mathbb Z)^\times\) generated by the residues of all rational primes dividing \(L\).
If \(-1\notin H_L\), no nontorsion algebraic-unit factor can contain a real root \(\beta\) for which \(s\beta^u>p/q\).
For odd \(u\) and \(-\epsilon s^{v+1}=-1\), the parent has exactly one real exterior root for every \(q\) in the stated range.
If in addition \(-1\notin H_L\), no nontorsion unit factor then has **any** real conjugate, excluding every Salem factor, including a signed Salem minimum, at these exponent pairs.
All conclusions refer to the original primitive root after taking the \(g\)th power of a root at a common dilation; a real exterior root at a dilation likewise maps to a real exterior root.

The subgroup condition holds for every coefficient in the five residue classes
\[
                      q\equiv 0,3,4,6,7\pmod8 .
\]
It also holds for every \(q\ge3\) for which \(L\) is a prime power.
For this last coefficient family the signed pole norm of every reciprocal primitive unit root is exactly \(L^{2j}\) for some \(0\le2j\le u\), no matter how large \(u\) is.
At \(q=3,4\) the prime-power condition holds, so every reciprocal nontorsion unit factor excludes the root beyond the pole; in the odd-\(u\) negative-sign case such a reciprocal factor has no real conjugate.
Finally for arbitrary \(L\), and all sufficiently large \(q\), this power classification holds for \(u=5,6\), extending the [classification through short exponent four](Yin_C_G0345_v877.md#norm).

<a id="root"></a>
## One real root determines the sign of the norm

Use the normalization \(T=s\beta^u,\ Y=-\epsilon s\beta^v\) from the [signed pole equation](Yin_C_G0345_v877.md#norm), so that
\[
 Y=\frac{pT-q}{p-qT},\qquad
 K=N_{\mathbb Q(T)/\mathbb Q}(p-qT),\qquad
 K\mid L^u,\quad K\equiv1\pmod M .                                       \tag{1}
\]
The two integer restrictions apply when the minimum of \(T\) is reciprocal.
For every nontorsion unit root and \(q\ge5\), the [measure window](Yin_C_G0345_v886.md#statement) and [Smyth's bound](../audits/literature/Smy71/Yin_U001.md#smy71-statement-and-conventions) give a reciprocal minimum for \(\beta\).
An automorphism sending \(\beta\) to its inverse also sends \(s\beta^u\) to its inverse.
The minimum of \(T\) is consequently reciprocal.
The graph relation and Bézout for \(u,v\) give \(\mathbb Q(\beta)=\mathbb Q(T)\), with every corresponding \(\beta\) uniquely determined by \(T\).

For a real exterior root, \(|T|>1\) and \(|Y|=|T|^{v/u}\).
If \(T=-r<-1\), one has \(|Y|=(pr+q)/(p+qr)<r\), a contradiction.
Thus \(T>1\).
For \(T>p/q\), the positive function \((pT-q)/(qT-p)\) decreases strictly from infinity to \(p/q\), whereas \(T^{v/u}\) increases strictly to infinity.
Their equality has exactly one solution.
Since the graph then fixes \(Y\) and the two signed powers determine \(\beta\), at most one actual root of the entire primitive parent can give \(T>p/q\).
Also any real conjugate of \(T\) gives a real conjugate of \(\beta\) by the same field equality.
Hence if a unit factor contains that root its norm \(K\) is negative: every other real factor \(p-qT_i\) is positive and nonreal pairs give positive products.

When \(u\) is odd the only possible sign of a real exterior \(\beta\) is \(s\), so \(Y\) has sign \(-\epsilon s^{v+1}\).
A negative sign forces \(T>p/q\), since the graph is positive for \(1<T<p/q\) and the pole itself is impossible.
Conversely the unique solution just constructed yields the actual real root \(\beta=sT^{1/u}\) in this sign.
If it cannot be a unit, any reciprocal nontorsion unit factor has no real conjugate: a real conjugate of modulus less than one would give an exterior real inverse, and a real conjugate of modulus one would be torsion.
For \(u=1\) the stronger known result excludes all unit factors in that sign for \(q\ge3\), using the additional one-exterior-root count. [Short exponent one](Yin_C_G0345_v877.md#sign)

## Testing the coefficient and explicit families

Each prime factor of \(L\) is coprime to \(M\), since \(L^2=4M+1\).
If \(K<0\) satisfies (1), then \(|K|\) is a positive product of these primes and has residue \(-1\bmod M\).
This proves the subgroup criterion.
Conversely if \(-1\in H_L\), one can replace all negative exponents in a residue representation by nonnegative exponents using the finite orders modulo \(M\).
The resulting integer \(J>0\) divides \(L^u\) for every sufficiently large \(u\), and \(K=-J\) meets both arithmetic conditions in (1).
For example \(q=37,\ L=75,\ M=1406\) permits \(K=-3^9\) at every \(u\ge9\), since \(3^9=14M-1\).
This verifies the precise limit of the sign test on the two integer conditions without asserting that such a norm is realized.

For \(4\mid M\), consider the negative fundamental discriminant \(\Delta\) of \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-M})\).
Its odd prime divisors divide \(M\), each to exponent one.
If \(v_2(M)=2\), the squarefree part of \(-M\) is odd and the 2-part of \(\Delta\) divides four.
If \(v_2(M)\ge3\), the 2-part of every fundamental discriminant divides eight.
Thus \(|\Delta|\mid M\).
The Kronecker character for a negative fundamental discriminant is a Dirichlet character modulo its absolute value and takes value \(-1\) at \(-1\), by quadratic reciprocity.
For every odd \(\ell\mid L\), the integer \(-4M=1-L^2\) is a nonzero square modulo \(\ell\), so \((\Delta/\ell)=1\) since \(\Delta\) has squareclass \(-M\).
Inflating this character to modulus \(M\) proves \(-1\notin H_L\).
This covers \(q\equiv0,3,4,7\bmod8\).
For \(q\equiv6\bmod8\) write \(q=2a_0\), \(a_0\equiv3\bmod4\).
The squarefree part \(a_1\) of \(a_0\) is still \(3\bmod4\), so the fundamental discriminant of \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-2q})\) is \(-a_1\) and divides \(q\) in absolute value.
Every \(\ell\mid L\) has \(-2q\equiv1\bmod\ell\).
This second negative character gives the same conclusion.

When \(L=\ell^a\) and \(q\ge3\), the order of \(\ell\bmod M\) is exactly \(2a\).
It divides \(2a\), and every proper divisor of \(2a\) is at most \(a\), with \(1<\ell^j\le L<M-1\) for \(1\le j\le a\).
The only element of order two in the subgroup generated by \(\ell\) is \(\ell^a=L\bmod M\), different from \(-1\).
Thus \(K\) is positive and its exponent at \(\ell\) must be divisible by \(2a\).
The norm bound in (1) gives \(K=L^{2j}\) with \(2j\le u\).

## The two next short exponents with arbitrary coefficient factorization

For \(u=5\) or \(6\), take \(J\) to be a signed integer of smaller absolute value among \(K\) and \(K'=L^u/K\).
Then \(J\mid L^6\) and \(|J|\le L^3\).
First assume \(J\equiv1\bmod M\), and write \(J=1+hM\).
The cases \(h=0,4\) give \(J=1,L^2\).
Otherwise the identity \(hL^2\equiv h-4\bmod J\) implies \(J\mid(h-4)^3\).
Along an unbounded sequence of \(q\), a bounded nonzero \(h\ne4\) cannot satisfy this because \(|J|\to\infty\).
For \(|h|\to\infty\), one has \(|h|=O(L)\) and \(k=(h-4)^3/J=O(1)\), since \(|J|\) is comparable to \(|h|L^2\).
This integer \(k\) is positive for sufficiently large \(|h|\), even if \(h<0\).
But \(J\equiv1\bmod h\) gives \(k\equiv-64\bmod h\), which forces \(k=-64\) for sufficiently large \(|h|\).
This rules out every remaining case on this residue.
When \(u=6\) both divisors have residue one, since \(L^2\equiv1\bmod M\); this gives exactly the necessary alternatives \(K\in\{1,L^2,L^4,L^6\}\).

For \(u=5\) it remains possible that the smaller divisor is \(J=K'\equiv L\bmod M\), with \(|J|\le L^{5/2}\).
Put \(d=\gcd(|J|,L)>0,\ h_0=L/d,\ J_0=J/d\).
Then \(\gcd(J_0,h_0)=1\) and \(J_0\mid d^4\) by \(J\mid L^5\).
Since \(d\) is coprime to \(M\), write \(J_0=h_0+Mz\) with \(z\in\mathbb Z\).
If \(z=0\) we get \(J=L\).
Otherwise \(|J_0|\) is bounded above and below by absolute positive multiples of \(L^2|z|\) for large \(L\), as \(h_0\le L\).
It follows from \(|J|\le L^{5/2}\) that \(d|z|\ll\sqrt L\), while \(d^4\ge |J_0|\gg L^2\).
Thus \(d\) and \(h_0=L/d\) are both comparable to \(\sqrt L\), and \(z\) and the nonzero integer \(e=d^4/J_0\) are bounded.
Now
\[
                 4d^4=e\bigl(z d^2h_0^2+4h_0-z\bigr).
\]
It follows that \(d^2\mid e(4h_0-z)\).
The right side is \(O(d)\) and nonzero for large \(L\), giving a contradiction.
This leaves \(K\in\{1,L^2,L^4\}\) at \(u=5\).
Both short-exponent conclusions are necessary alternatives; the positive norm branches and factors with exclusively nonreal exterior roots remain possible under these tests.
