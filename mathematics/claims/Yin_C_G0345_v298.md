# The attained spectrum of normalized residue shapes

<a id="statement"></a>
## Norm levels and all finite third-order limits

Fix the Salem polynomial, trace order and coefficient congruence data of the [chamber-norm theorem](Yin_C_G0345_v297.md#statement).
Thus \(P=z^mQ(z+z^{-1})\), \(T>2\) is the exterior trace, \(x_1<\cdots<x_h\) are the neutral traces, \(h=m-1\), and
\[
 F=\mathbb Q(T),\quad \mathcal O=\mathbb Z[T],\quad
 H=Q/(X-T),\quad Z=H(T),\quad D_H=\operatorname{disc}H,\quad
 \mathfrak a=|H(-2)|.
\]
Let \(M\ge1\) and let \(W\) be a coefficient residue polynomial modulo \(M\) of formal degree at most \(h\).
The chamber \(\mathcal C_{M,W}\) consists of the elements with unique power-basis representative \(R\equiv W\pmod M\) satisfying
\[
                         R(T)<0,\qquad R(x_i)H'(x_i)<0 .
\]
Write \(\mathcal N_{M,W}\) for its set of positive absolute norms and \(\mu_{M,W}\) for the least such norm.
For an actual congruent Pisot inverse, use
\[
 q=f(0),\quad \delta=\theta-q,\quad E=T-2,\quad
 \eta=\delta-\frac{E\theta}{\theta-1},\quad
 \mathcal R(f)=|\operatorname{Res}(f,f^*)|.
\]
Consider sequences of these inverses with \(q\to\infty\) and \(\delta\to E\).

For every \(n\in\mathcal N_{M,W}\) there is a finite positive constant \(c_n\), defined explicitly in (2) below.
If \(h\ge2\), the full set of finite joint limits of
\[
                  \left(\frac{\mathcal R(f)}q,\ q^{1+1/h}\eta\right)
\]
is exactly
\[
 \boxed{\displaystyle
       \bigcup_{n\in\mathcal N_{M,W}}
            \{\,2\mathfrak a|P(1)|n^2\,\}\times[c_n,\infty).}        \tag{1}
\]
Every point in this set is attained as a limit along a sequence.
For \(h=1\), replace the ray in each norm level by the singleton \(\{n/Z\}\).

The numbers \(c_n\) satisfy \(c_n\ge h(n/(ZD_H))^{1/h}\).
Their minimum over all attained norms is attained and is the sharp lower limit of the scaled error over the entire congruence class.
It equals the arithmetic-geometric mean lower bound \(h(\mu_{M,W}/(ZD_H))^{1/h}\) exactly under the closure condition below.
For \(h=2\) this condition always holds at every norm, so
\[
                       c_n=2\sqrt{\frac n{ZD_H}}.
\]
In particular every fixed Salem polynomial of degree six, with every prescribed coefficient congruence, attains the lower constant from [C297](Yin_C_G0345_v297.md#third-order).
At degree four the corresponding value is \(\mu_{M,W}/Z\).

<a id="shape"></a>
## The closed set governing each unit orbit

**Definition.**
Let \(U_M^+\) be the group of totally positive units of \(\mathcal O\) congruent to one modulo \(M\mathcal O\).
Its logarithmic image \(\Lambda_M\) is a full lattice in
\[
 V=\{(\lambda_0,\ldots,\lambda_h)\in\mathbb R^{h+1}:
                                               \textstyle\sum_i\lambda_i=0\}.
\]
Indeed the [order-level unit theorem](../audits/literature/Con26/Yin_U001.md#con26-u001) supplies the full lattice, and signs and reduction have finite image.
Put
\[
 Y=\{y\in\mathbb R^h:\textstyle\sum_i y_i=0\},\qquad
 (\pi\lambda)_i=\lambda_i+\lambda_0/h,\qquad
 G_M=\overline{\pi\Lambda_M}\subseteq Y.
\]
For each \(n\in\mathcal N_{M,W}\), choose one representative \(\nu\) from each \(U_M^+\)-orbit in the norm-\(n\) part of the chamber, and denote this finite set by \(\mathcal V_n\).
The finiteness is proved below.
For its real embeddings \(\nu_0,\nu_1,\ldots,\nu_h\), define
\[
 a_{\nu,i}=\log\frac{|\nu_i|}{|H'(x_i)|},\qquad
 s_{\nu,i}=a_{\nu,i}-\frac1h\sum_j a_{\nu,j},\qquad
 C_n=\left(\frac n{ZD_H}\right)^{1/h}.
\]
Then
\[
 c_n=C_n\min_{\nu\in\mathcal V_n}
                  \min_{y\in s_\nu+G_M}\ \sum_{i=1}^h e^{y_i}.      \tag{2}
\]
The inner minima are attained.
Changing the representative only translates \(s_\nu\) by an element of \(\pi\Lambda_M\), so the definition is intrinsic.

More precisely, on the single orbit through \(\nu\), the finite limiting vectors of normalized residues \((q^{1/h}b_i)_i\), with all coordinates positive, are exactly
\[
                 \{(C_ne^{y_i})_i:y\in s_\nu+G_M\}.                \tag{3}
\]
Every vector in (3) is attainable while the distinguished logarithm tends to \(+\infty\).
Equality \(c_n=hC_n\) holds exactly when \(0\in s_\nu+G_M\) for some \(\nu\in\mathcal V_n\).
Consequently the sharp constant at the least chamber norm equals the lower bound in the statement exactly when this closure condition holds for an orbit of norm \(\mu_{M,W}\).

<a id="orbits"></a>
## Finitely many unit orbits in an arbitrary order

For \(\nu\ne0\) in the full order \(\mathcal O\), multiplication by \(\nu\) is an injective integral endomorphism of its power-basis lattice.
Its determinant is the field norm, so
\[
                           [\mathcal O:\nu\mathcal O]=|N(\nu)|.
\]
There are only finitely many subgroups of \(\mathbb Z^m\) of a given finite index.
For example any subgroup of index \(n\) contains \(n\mathbb Z^m\), and the quotient \((\mathbb Z/n)^m\) is finite.
Thus only finitely many principal ideals \(\nu\mathcal O\) have index \(n\), including when \(\mathcal O\) is not the maximal order.

If \(\nu\mathcal O=\nu'\mathcal O\), there are \(a,b\in\mathcal O\) with \(\nu'=a\nu\) and \(\nu=b\nu'\).
Cancellation gives \(ab=1\), so \(\nu'/\nu\) is an order unit.
When both generators belong to the fixed strict chamber, that unit is totally positive.
The subgroup \(U_M^+\) has finite index in the totally positive unit group.
The chamber generators of each ideal are therefore a finite union of its orbits.
Selecting only those generators satisfying \(R\equiv W\pmod M\) preserves this finiteness, since multiplication by \(U_M^+\) preserves that congruence.
This proves the finiteness of \(\mathcal V_n\) and, by summing over \(n\), finiteness of these orbits in every bounded norm range.

<a id="recurrence"></a>
## Approximating a projected shape with positive escape

The kernel of \(\pi:V\to Y\) is the line generated by \(v=(h,-1,\ldots,-1)\).
There are lattice vectors \(\lambda\in\Lambda_M\) arbitrarily close to \(tv\) with arbitrarily large \(t>0\).
For a direct recurrence argument, put a translation-invariant metric on the compact torus \(V/\Lambda_M\).
Given a neighborhood radius \(\epsilon>0\) and an integer \(K>0\), cover this torus by finitely many sets of diameter less than \(\epsilon\).
One of these sets contains infinitely many of the points \(kv+\Lambda_M\), \(k\ge0\).
Two such indices differ by more than \(K\).
Their difference produces \(t>K\) and \(\lambda\in\Lambda_M\) with \(\|\lambda-tv\|<\epsilon\), by the quotient metric.
In particular \(\lambda_0\) can be arbitrarily large while \(\pi\lambda\) is arbitrarily small.

If \(y\in s_\nu+G_M\), choose \(\mu_j\in\Lambda_M\) with \(s_\nu+\pi\mu_j\to y\).
Add a recurrence vector whose projected norm is less than \(1/j\) and whose distinguished coordinate exceeds \(j-(\mu_j)_0\).
The resulting \(\lambda_j\) still satisfies \(s_\nu+\pi\lambda_j\to y\), and \((\lambda_j)_0\to+\infty\).

Let \(R_j\) represent the corresponding chamber element \(\nu\varepsilon_j\), where \(\lambda_j\) is the logarithm of \(\varepsilon_j\in U_M^+\).
The [interpolation formula](Yin_C_G0345_v297.md#unit-orbit) gives an integer constant
\[
 q_j=1-[X^h]R_j
     =1+\frac{|\nu_0|e^{(\lambda_j)_0}}Z
        -\sum_i\frac{b_{j,i}}{T-x_i}.
\]
Here \(b_{j,i}=e^{a_{\nu,i}+(\lambda_j)_i}\).
Since the projected vectors stay bounded, each \(b_{j,i}\) is comparable to \(e^{-(\lambda_j)_0/h}\).
It follows that \(q_j\sim|\nu_0|e^{(\lambda_j)_0}/Z\to\infty\), while every \(b_{j,i}\to0\).
The strict inverse-simplex conditions hold eventually, giving actual Pisot minimal polynomials of the required degree and coefficient congruence.
They also satisfy \(q_j\equiv1-[X^h]W\pmod M\).

For these sequences,
\[
 \log(q_j^{1/h}b_{j,i})
   =\log C_n+s_{\nu,i}+(\pi\lambda_j)_i+o(1).                       \tag{4}
\]
The constant follows from
\(\prod_i|\nu_i/H'(x_i)|=n/(|\nu_0|D_H)\).
This proves attainability in (3).
Conversely any sequence in the orbit with positive finite normalized residue limits satisfies (4) by the [fixed-fiber limits](Yin_C_G0345_v297.md#simplex), and hence its shape lies in the same closed translate.

<a id="closure"></a>
## A line in every projected closure and full density for two coordinates

For \(h\ge2\), no nonzero vector of \(\Lambda_M\) belongs to \(\mathbb Rv\).
Indeed a corresponding positive unit would have distinguished value \(e^{ht}\) and the same neutral value \(e^{-t}\) at all \(h\) other embeddings, with \(t\ne0\).
There would be exactly two distinct conjugates of that unit.
Each embedding of the field it generates extends to exactly the same number of embeddings of \(F\).
Their multiplicities \(1,h\) must therefore agree, contradicting \(h\ge2\).
For \(t=0\) all embeddings equal one and the unit is one.

Thus \(\pi\) injects the rank-\(h\) lattice into an \((h-1)\)-dimensional real space.
Its image cannot be discrete, since a discrete subgroup of a finite-dimensional real vector space has rank at most its dimension.
For completeness, choose subgroup vectors forming a basis of its real span.
A compact parallelepiped for their integer span contains only finitely many subgroup points by discreteness, so that integer span has finite index and the rank is the dimension of the real span.

Choose nonzero \(g_j\in\pi\Lambda_M\) tending to zero, and pass to a subsequence for which \(g_j/\|g_j\|\to w\), with \(\|w\|=1\).
For any real \(t\), integer multiples of \(g_j\) with multipliers nearest to \(t/\|g_j\|\) tend to \(tw\).
Hence the whole line \(\mathbb Rw\) lies in \(G_M\).
When \(h=2\), this line is all of the one-dimensional space \(Y\), proving \(G_M=Y\).
This argument works for every congruence kernel because its logarithmic lattice remains full.

The function \(\Phi(y)=\sum_i e^{y_i}\) is proper on \(Y\).
Indeed a bound on \(\Phi\) bounds every coordinate above, and the zero coordinate sum then bounds every coordinate below.
It follows that \(\Phi\) attains a minimum on every nonempty closed translate \(s_\nu+G_M\).
For \(h\ge2\), a line through a minimizer lies entirely in that translate, and \(\Phi\) tends to infinity along that line in either direction.
Continuity then makes the image of this translate under \(\Phi\) exactly the ray from its minimum to infinity.
For \(h=1\), \(Y=\{0\}\) and this image is the singleton \(\{1\}\).
This proves the claimed ray or singleton already on each orbit.

<a id="limits"></a>
## The full joint limit set and its sharp endpoint

The [exact norm and excess identities](Yin_C_G0345_v297.md#simplex) give
\[
 n=|R(T)|D_H\prod_i b_i,\qquad
 \frac{\mathcal R(f)}q=\frac{f(-1)}q|P(1)|n^2,\qquad
 \eta=\sum_i\frac{b_i}{\theta+\theta^{-1}-x_i}.
\]
Along the sequences in question, \(|R(T)|/q\to Z\), \(f(-1)/q\to2\mathfrak a\), and the denominators in the last sum are \(q+O_P(1)\).
Therefore bounded \(q^{1+1/h}\eta\) bounds every \(q^{1/h}b_i\) above and bounds the positive integer \(n\).
After passing to a subsequence, its norm and one of its finitely many unit orbits are fixed.
The product identity then bounds each normalized residue away from zero, so the shape vectors have a convergent subsequence in \(Y\).
Its limit is described by (3).
Conversely every shape in (3) has an actual sequence supplied above, and on that sequence
\[
 \frac{\mathcal R(f)}q\longrightarrow2\mathfrak a|P(1)|n^2,\qquad
 q^{1+1/h}\eta\longrightarrow C_n\Phi(y).
\]
The orbit rays and finite orbit count prove (1).

Arithmetic-geometric mean gives \(\Phi(y)\ge h\), with equality exactly at \(y=0\).
Properness gives strict inequality for the minimum of a closed translate not containing zero.
This proves the criterion for equality in (2).
It also proves that \(c_n\to\infty\) as \(n\to\infty\) through the attained norms.
To minimize over all norms, compare with the finite value of any one orbit.
Only finitely many integers \(n\) have \(hC_n\) below that comparison value, and each has finitely many orbits.
Thus the global minimum is attained.
It equals \(h(\mu_{M,W}/(ZD_H))^{1/h}\) precisely when one norm-\(\mu_{M,W}\) orbit has zero in its closed shape translate.
This proves the sharp-constant assertions, including those for \(h=1,2\).
When \(h=2\), (3) also permits every positive pair of normalized residues with product \(n/(ZD_H)\), within each individual congruent unit orbit.

## What a biquadratic field requires in three shape coordinates

When \(h=3\) and \(F\) is biquadratic, the density question has an explicit additional condition.
Choose positive even powers \(u_1,u_2,u_3>1\) of units from its three quadratic subfields.
Their logarithmic vectors, in a suitable order of the four embeddings, are
\[
 a(1,1,-1,-1),\qquad b(1,-1,1,-1),\qquad c(1,-1,-1,1),
 \qquad a=\log u_1,\ b=\log u_2,\ c=\log u_3>0.
\]
Their sign patterns are the three nontrivial quadratic characters of the biquadratic Galois group, so the three logarithmic vectors are linearly independent over the reals.
The same vectors and the finite-index unit description appear in [Kim–Song, §3](../audits/literature/KS24/Yin_U001.md#biquadratic-units).

After taking a further common positive power, all three units belong to \(U_M^+\).
For an elementary verification choose an integer \(a_0\ge1\) with \(a_0\mathcal O_F\subseteq\mathcal O\).
A sufficiently high common power is one modulo \(Ma_0\mathcal O_F\), by finiteness of the unit group of this quotient.
That power and its inverse belong to \(\mathcal O\), and it is one modulo \(M\mathcal O\).
The three displayed vectors are linearly independent, so their subgroup has finite index in the full rank-three lattice \(\Lambda_M\).
This supplies the finite-index assertion used here without any classification of fundamental units.

In the coordinates \((y_1-y_3,y_2-y_3)\) on \(Y\), these projections generate
\[
                     (2a,0),\qquad(0,2b),\qquad(-2c,-2c).
\]
Their closure is all of \(\mathbb R^2\) exactly when there are no integers \(k,l,r\), not all zero, with
\[
                \frac{k}{a}+\frac{l}{b}-\frac{r}{c}=0,
       \quad\text{equivalently}\quad kbc+lac-rab=0.                 \tag{5}
\]
To see this, a nonzero real linear functional integral on the first two generators has coefficients \(k/(2a),l/(2b)\).
Integrality on the third is exactly (5).
Conversely any proper closed subgroup containing the two independent axis generators has such a functional.
Indeed its maximal vector subspace has a discrete quotient, as follows from the small-vector argument above, and a nonzero linear functional on the resulting lattice can be chosen integral.
Thus a proper closure supplies the functional.
The same criterion follows by quotienting by the two axis periods and applying the classical linear equidistribution criterion on the two-dimensional torus.
See [Tao, §1, Exercise 5](../literature/pdf/Tao10/equidistribution-20100328.html#equiva).
Passing to a subgroup of finite index preserves density, since its closure has only finitely many translates covering the larger closure, and a real vector space has no proper closed subgroup of finite index.

The quotient of any two of \(a,b,c\) is irrational.
A rational relation between two of these logarithms would give a common nontrivial power of units in distinct quadratic subfields.
That power would lie in their intersection \(\mathbb Q\), whose positive units are one, a contradiction.
Consequently every nontrivial relation in (5), if present, has all three coefficients nonzero.
The present argument leaves those remaining relations unevaluated.
It proves the exact density criterion and the unconditional ray spectrum, while making no general density assertion for \(h=3\).
