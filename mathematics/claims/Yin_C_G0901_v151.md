# Denominators of all monic derivatives of a Salem trace polynomial

<a id="statement"></a>
## Exponential denominators in the middle and strict descent for every integral derivative

Let \(1<\alpha\le2\) be a Salem number of degree \(2n\), let \(H\in\mathbb Z[X]\) be the monic minimum of \(T=\alpha+\alpha^{-1}\), and let \(V_n\) be the variance of its \(n\) trace roots with equal weights.
For \(2\le r<n\) set
\[
       G_r=\frac{r!}{n!}H^{(n-r)},\qquad
       q_r=\min\{q\ge1:q\in\mathbb Z,\ qG_r\in\mathbb Z[X]\}.
\]
There are positive absolute constants \(c,C\) such that, at every such order,
\[
 \log q_r\ge cr(1-r/n)^2-C\log(2r),\qquad
 q_r\ge \left(\frac{n-1}{2r V_n}\right)^{r/4}.                         \tag{1}
\]
One has \(V_n=2+O(\sqrt{\log(2n)/n})\).
For \(r\to\infty\) and \(r/n\to s\in[0,1]\), the first bound has the asymptotic form
\[
                    \liminf\frac{\log q_r}{r}\ge\tfrac12(1-s)^2.         \tag{2}
\]
Thus denominators grow exponentially in the residual degree at any fixed interior proportion.

For every sufficiently large \(n\), if \(G_r\) is integral at any positive order of residual degree at least two, its largest root is the trace of a **strictly smaller Salem number of strictly smaller degree**.
In consequence an \(\alpha\) smaller than every Salem number of lower degree has **no** integral monic derivative of positive order and residual degree at least two.
The [earlier derivative result](Yin_C_G0345_v112.md#records) supplies this obstruction for the initial consecutive orders whose largest root exceeds two.
Here (1) rules out integrality also throughout all later orders.
The residual linear polynomial can be integral, for instance at zero trace.

For a shrinking sequence \(\alpha\to1\) and any fixed \(0<s<1\), all the zeros \(y\) of all \(G_r\) with \(2\le r\le sn\) satisfy
\[
                     |y|\le 2\sqrt{s(2-s)}+o(1).                         \tag{3}
\]
In that range the number of algebraic-integer roots of any one \(G_r\) is bounded solely in terms of \(s\); they belong to a fixed finite set of cyclotomic traces.
For \(s<1-\sqrt3/2\) the only possible integral root is zero, even at bounded \(1<\alpha\le2\) and growing degree.
This exclusion concerns the individual roots, without requiring the whole derivative to be integral.

<a id="moments"></a>
## Trace moments, derivative variance and the integral discriminant

Strict Rolle interlacing makes the \(r\) roots of \(G_r\) real and simple, all greater than \(-2\) and below \(T\), with at most one above two.
This includes the one-sided root location in the [original derivative calculation](Yin_C_G0345_v112.md#coefficients).
If \(\mu\) is the mean of the roots of \(H\), the roots of \(G_r\) have the same mean and variance \(V_r=(r-1)V_n/(n-1)\).
Indeed the first two elementary symmetric functions are multiplied by \(r/n\) and \(r(r-1)/(n(n-1))\), respectively.

For clarity, the [reflected discriminant estimate](Yin_C_G0345_v108.md#phases) for a squarefree integer polynomial \(P\) of degree \(D\), leading coefficient \(q>0\) and nonzero roots \(z_i=e^{t_i+i\theta_i}\), reads
\[
 \sum_{h\ge1}\frac{\rho^{2h}}h
    \left|\sum_i e^{-h|t_i|+ih\theta_i}\right|^2
 \le 2(D-1)\log M(P)+D(D-1)\log(1/\rho)
                           +D\log\frac1{1-\rho^2},\quad 0<\rho<1.        \tag{4}
\]
The same pair-distance proof covers reducible and nonmonic polynomials here: the log of the ordered product of root distances is at least \(-(2D-2)\log q\) by the nonzero integral discriminant.
The radial contribution \(2(D-1)\sum_i t_i^+\) together with that leading coefficient gives the displayed Mahler term.
At \(\rho=e^{-1/D}\) the last two terms are \(O(D\log(2D))\).

First apply (4) to the Salem minimum.
At the frequencies one and two the actual power sums differ from the reflected sums by at most four, since only the positive exterior root is modified and \(\alpha\le2\).
The actual sums are \(n\mu\) and \(\sum_{H(t)=0}(t^2-2)\).
Thus \(\mu=O(\sqrt{\log(2n)/n})\) and the stated variance estimate follows.

Next take the integer polynomial \(P=q_r X^rG_r(X+X^{-1})\).
If a trace root equals two, remove both copies of \(X-1\), leaving a squarefree integer polynomial of degree \(D=2r+O(1)\) and leading coefficient \(q_r\).
There is no trace root at \(-2\).
The sole possible exterior contribution is a positive root smaller than \(\alpha\), so \(M(P)\le2q_r\) also after this removal.
Restoring the deleted roots and undoing the reflection changes the second power sum by an absolute bounded amount.
With \(y\) running over the roots of \(G_r\), (4) gives
\[
 \left|\sum_y(y^2-2)\right|
                      \ll\sqrt{r(\log q_r+\log(2r))}.                      \tag{5}
\]
On the other hand \(r^{-1}\sum_y y^2=\mu^2+(r-1)V_n/(n-1)\).
Consequently \(2(n-r)/(n-1)\) is bounded by a constant times \(\sqrt{(\log q_r+\log(2r))/r}+\sqrt{\log(2n)/n}\).
The second error is at most a constant times \(\sqrt{\log(2r)/r}\) since \(2\le r\le n\).
Squaring proves the first inequality of (1).
In the limit of (2) the actual second power sum divided by \(r\) tends to \(-2(1-s)\).
Keeping its single frequency-two term with coefficient \(\rho^4/2\) in (4), dividing by \(r^2\) and using \(2(D-1)/r\to4\) gives (2).

For the second bound in (1) use the discriminant of \(q_rG_r\) directly.
The arithmetic mean of the squared distances between unordered pairs of its roots is \(2r V_r/(r-1)=2r V_n/(n-1)\).
Their product is bounded above by this mean to the power \(\binom r2\).
Multiplication by \(q_r^{2r-2}\) gives a nonzero integer of absolute value at least one, proving the bound.

<a id="descent"></a>
## Which integral derivatives can occur

When \(q_r=1\), the second inequality in (1) implies \(r\ge(n-1)/(2V_n)\), hence \(r\gg n\).
The first inequality now forces \(k=n-r\ll\sqrt{n\log(2n)}\).
Put \(\delta=T-2\) and let \(t_i\) range over the \(n-1\) neutral traces.
Write
\[
       H(2+X)=(X-\delta)B(X),\qquad B(X)=\prod_i (2-t_i+X).
\]
If \(B_j=[X^j]B\), then \(B_k/B_{k-1}\ge (n-k)/(4k)\).
Indeed in the elementary symmetric functions of the \((2-t_i)^{-1}>1/4\), each term of degree \(k-1\) extends in \(n-k\) ways and each term of degree \(k\) is counted \(k\) times.
The [Voutier bound](../audits/literature/Vou96/Yin_U001.md#height) at degree \(2n\) gives \(\delta=2\cosh(\log\alpha)-2\ge(\log\alpha)^2\gg(\log\log(2n)/\log(2n))^6\).
Thus \(\delta(n-k)>4k\) uniformly in this possible integral range at large \(n\).
It follows that \(B_{k-1}-\delta B_k<0\), so \(G_r(2)<0\) and its largest root \(y\) is in \((2,T)\).
If \(G_r\) is integral, \(y\) is an algebraic integer and is irrational since \(T\le5/2\).
All its other conjugates belong to \((-2,2)\) by interlacing and the strict sign at two.
The quadratic \(X^2-yX+1\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q(y)\) because its discriminant is negative at any one of those other real embeddings.
Its exterior root is Salem, strictly less than \(\alpha\), and has degree \(2\deg y\le2r<2n\).
In terms of \(H(X)=\sum_{j=0}^n h_j X^{n-j}\), the test is entirely integral: \(q_r\) is the least common multiple of the reduced denominators of \(h_j\binom rj/\binom nj\), \(0\le j\le r\).

<a id="confinement"></a>
## The roots at a positive proportion of differentiations

For a real-rooted polynomial \(P\) of degree at least two and \(z\) larger than its largest root put \(\Phi_P(z)=P'(z)/P(z)\), \(h=1/\Phi_P(z)\).
Then \(z-h\) is still strictly above all roots and \(\Phi_{P'}(z-h)\le1/h\).
To verify the inequality put \(a_i=h/(z-h-x_i)\), \(p_i=a_i/(1+a_i)=h/(z-x_i)\), where \(x_i\) are the roots.
The \(p_i\) sum to one.
The logarithmic derivative of \(P'=P\Phi_P\) gives the desired inequality as \(\sum a_i^2\ge(\sum a_i)(\sum a_i-1)\).
The difference is the variance of the \(a_i\) with weights \(p_i\), since \(a_i=p_i(1+a_i)\).
Iterate with this fixed step \(h\): if the next available inverse logarithmic derivative is larger than \(h\), the shorter displacement keeps the argument farther right and can only lower the logarithmic derivative there.
Consequently after \(k\) differentiations the largest root is at most \(z-k/\Phi_H(z)\).
The same argument applies to the reflected trace roots for a lower bound on the least one.

For shrinking Salem numbers the empirical trace law tends to the distribution of \(2\cos\theta\) for uniform \(\theta\) on \([0,\pi]\), by the [angular trace law](Yin_C_G0901_v112.md#trace) translated back by two.
The outer trace tends to two.
Therefore for each fixed \(z>2\) the Cauchy transform \(\Phi_H(z)/n\) tends to \((z^2-4)^{-1/2}\), as does that of the reflected trace polynomial.
The integral follows by the substitution \(v=\tan(\theta/2)\) in \(\pi^{-1}\int_0^\pi (z-2\cos\theta)^{-1}d\theta\).
For \(k=n-r\ge(1-s)n\), choose \(z=2/\sqrt{s(2-s)}\).
Then \(z-(1-s)\sqrt{z^2-4}=2\sqrt{s(2-s)}\), proving (3) uniformly over these orders.
If \(s<1-\sqrt3/2\), this edge is strictly below one and the chosen \(z\) exceeds four.
Even on a bounded sequence \(1<\alpha\le2\) it is separated from the entire trace support \([-5/2,5/2]\); the same arcsine law and transform limit hold there without requiring \(\alpha\to1\).

For every fixed \(s<1\) in (3), all conjugates of any integral root eventually belong to a fixed interval \([-B_s,B_s]\) with \(B_s<2\).
It is then a cyclotomic trace by Kronecker's theorem applied to its monic reciprocal quadratic lift.
The orders are bounded in terms of \(s\), since the largest conjugate of a primitive trace of order \(N\ge3\) is \(2\cos(2\pi/N)\).
There are only finitely many such traces, and each occurs at most once by simplicity.
In the strict interval \((-1,1)\) a nonzero algebraic integer cannot have all conjugates there, by its nonzero integer norm.

<a id="shift"></a>
## A trace minimum of magnitude below one

There is also an integer perturbation without differentiation.
At all sufficiently large degrees under \(1<\alpha\le2\), \(H(2)\le-1\) and \(H'(2)<0\) by the same coefficient test at \(k=1\).
Thus \(H+1\) has a real root strictly between \(2\) and \(T\): at two it is nonpositive and initially decreases, and its value at \(T\) is one.
If all local minima in the negative gaps between successive neutral roots have value at most \(-1\), then \(H+1\) is entirely real-rooted.
Indeed these minima and the final minimum beyond two reach the level \(-1\), while every intervening local maximum is positive; equality at a minimum gives a double root.
The least root cannot fall below \(-2\), since for even degree \(H\) is positive below its least root, and for odd degree it increases there to \(H(-2)\le-1\).
There is only one root above two by the unique final critical point and the sign at two.
Its irreducible trace factor yields a strictly smaller Salem number by the same argument as above, now of degree **at most** \(2n\).
Any Salem number minimizing among all Salem numbers of degree at most its own must therefore have an interior critical value strictly between \(-1\) and zero at these large degrees.
The criteria do not force an integral derivative or a successful shift for an arbitrary Salem trace, so they leave the unrestricted Salem gap open.
