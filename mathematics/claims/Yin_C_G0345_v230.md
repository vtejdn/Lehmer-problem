# The exceptional common factor for five terms in three blocks

<a id="statement"></a>
## Classification and the individual-factor gap near the midpoint

With five nonzero integral coefficients, write
\[
 P=A(X)+c X^n+X^D B(X^{-1}),\qquad
 A=A_0+A_1X^a,\quad B=B_0+B_1X^b,\qquad 0<a<n<D-b<D .
\]
Set \(j=D-2n,\ w=\max(a,b)\), and \(K=\|P\|_1/\min(|A_0|,|B_0|)\).
For \(H\in\mathbb Q(X)\) write \(H^*=H(X^{-1})\), and consider
\[
 F=A+cY+X^jB^*Y^2,\qquad F^\dagger=B+cX^jY+X^j A^*Y^2
                                                   \quad\text{in }\mathbb Q(X)[Y].
\]
Apart from \(j=0,\ A=B\), the two quadratics are nonproportional.
If they share a nonzero root over an algebraic closure of \(\mathbb Q(X)\), then
\[
                         j=2w-a\quad\hbox{or}\quad j=b-2w .              \tag{1}
\]
Their unique common root is a signed Laurent monomial, except for the following family up to reversal of \(P\):
\[
 \begin{split}
   &a=b=g,\quad D=2n+g,\quad Z=X^g,\\
   &P=C\big((1-zZ)X^n-s(Z-z)\big)(zX^n-s),\\
   &\hspace{15mm}C,z\in\mathbb Q^\times,\quad z\ne\pm1,\quad s=\pm1 . \tag{2}
 \end{split}
\]
Conversely (2) has a nonmonomial common root in the two formal quadratics.
Every nontorsion monic unit factor of (2) divides its displayed four-term factor over \(\mathbb Q\).

Let \(S\) be a primitive irreducible divisor of sufficiently small positive logarithmic measure \(m\) and degree \(d\), and put \(t=\max_{S(x)=0}\log|x|\).
Outside the centered reciprocal case \(j=0,\ A=B\) and the products (2) in either orientation, there is an absolute \(c_1>0\) such that
\[
                \frac m d\left(\frac{\log K}{t}+|j|\right)\ge c_1,
                   \qquad 20m^2\log K+|j|\frac m d\ge c_1 .             \tag{3}
\]
In particular, for every fixed \(D_0\ge0\), if \(|j|\le D_0w\), the same exclusions give a lower bound \(c_{D_0}/\sqrt{\log K}\) on the positive logarithmic measure of every integral divisor.
For \(|j|<w\) the product exception is impossible.
Every asymmetric centered input enjoys this gap without a condition on common roots of its binomials or on its discriminant.

<a id="width"></a>
## Two possible displacements

Proportionality forces \(j=0,\ A=B\) by [comparison of the middle coefficients](Yin_C_G0345_v229.md#elimination).
Assume otherwise, with common root \(R\).
The [unique-root and reversal argument](Yin_C_G0345_v229.md#elimination) gives \(R\in\mathbb Q(X)^\times,\ RR^*=1\).
Put \(T=AA^*-BB^*,\ E=A-X^{-j}B\).
Subtracting \(B^*F^\dagger\) from \(A^*F\) and substituting \(R\) gives
\[
                         T=-c E^*R,\qquad T^2=c^2EE^*.                 \tag{4}
\]
If \(T=0\) then \(E=0\), forcing \(A=B,\ j=0\) by their nonzero constant coefficients.
If \(T\) is a nonzero constant then \(a=b,\ A_0A_1=B_0B_1\) by the outer coefficients of \(T\).
Equation (4) makes \(E\) a Laurent monomial, since the highest exponent of \(EE^*\) is the width of \(E\).
The two binomial supports in \(E\) must agree: if they differ, their symmetric difference contains at least two indices with nonzero coefficients.
Thus \(j=0\), and one coefficient agrees between \(A\) and \(B\).
Their equal coefficient products then give \(A=B\), again impossible.

In all other cases the highest exponent of \(T\) is \(w\), and (4) gives \(\operatorname{width}(E)=2w\).
For \(|j|<w\) the supports of \(A,X^{-j}B\) together have width at most \(w+|j|<2w\).
For \(j\ge w\) the extreme exponents of \(E\) are \(-j,a\), both with nonzero coefficients.
For \(j\le-w\) they are \(0,b-j\).
This proves (1).

<a id="linear"></a>
## The rational root in the common exponent

Set \(g=\gcd(a,b),\ p=a/g,\ q=b/g\).
By (1), \(k=j/g\) is an integer and both quadratics are defined over \(\mathbb Q(Z)\), where \(Z=X^g\).
Their degree-one gcd over \(\mathbb Q(X)\) is already defined up to scalar over \(\mathbb Q(Z)\), since the Euclidean algorithm can be carried out there.
In particular \(R\in\mathbb Q(Z)\).
Writing \(R=U/V\) in coprime Laurent polynomials of \(Z\), the [root equation and its reversal](Yin_C_G0345_v229.md#elimination) make \(V\) associated to \(U^*\) and \(U\) a divisor of both \(A_0+A_1Z^p,\ B_0+B_1Z^q\).
Any common root \(z\) of these binomials is rational and unique.
Indeed \(z^p=-A_0/A_1,\ z^q=-B_0/B_1\), so Bézout's identity expresses \(z\) as a product of integer powers of these two rational values.
The binomials have only simple roots.

Unless \(R\) is a Laurent monomial, there must therefore be a common root \(z\in\mathbb Q^\times\), with \(z\ne\pm1\) by coprimality of \(U,V\).
Up to a Laurent unit \(U=Z-z\), and \(RR^*=1\) gives
\[
                         R=s Z^e (Z-z)/(Z^{-1}-z),\qquad
                         s=\pm1,\quad e\in\mathbb Z.                    \tag{5}
\]
Any Laurent monomial satisfying that same norm identity is a signed monomial.

<a id="intervals"></a>
## The two binomials after removing their common linear factor

Write \(L_i(Z)=(Z^i-z^i)/(Z-z)\).
For (5), clearing \(F(Z,R)=0\) and canceling the common factor gives
\[
 A_1 (Z^{-1}-z)L_p(Z)
      +cs Z^e
      +B_1 Z^{k+2e}(Z-z)L_q(Z^{-1})=0.                                \tag{6}
\]
The first and third summands have as their respective supports the full integer intervals
\[
                         I=[-1,p-1],\qquad J=[k+2e-q+1,k+2e+1] .
\]
In fact the coefficients of \((Z^{-1}-z)L_p(Z)\), from exponent \(-1\) to \(p-1\), are \(z^{p-1}\), then \(z^{p-2-i}(1-z^2)\) at \(0\le i\le p-2\), then \(-z\).
The third interval has the reversed coefficient pattern for \(q\), before multiplication by \(B_1\).
All these coefficients are nonzero.
Consequently \(I\mathbin{\triangle}J\subseteq\{e\}\) and \(e\in I\cup J\), and \(|p-q|\le1\).

We may assume \(p\ge q\) by reversing \(P\): this exchanges \(A,B\), sends \(j\) to \(-j\), and uses the variable \(Y'=X^jY\) for the middle power \(X^{D-n}\).
The two formal quadratics interchange under this substitution, so the property of having a nonmonomial common root is unchanged.

Suppose \(p=q+1\).
The interval \(J\) is obtained by deleting one endpoint \(e\) of \(I\).
If \(e=-1\), then \(J=[0,p-1]\) and \(k=p\).
If \(e=p-1\), then \(J=[-1,p-2]\) and \(k=-p-1\).
In either case their interiors have \(p-2\) consecutive indices in common, all different from \(e\).
On consecutive interior indices the first summand in (6) has coefficient ratio \(z^{-1}\) and the third has ratio \(z\).
Cancellation at two such indices would require \(z^2=1\).
Thus \(p=2\) or \(3\).
For completeness, the following pairs are two required values of \(B_1/A_1\), obtained from the coefficients at the indicated indices of (6).
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
p&e&\text{indices}&\text{required ratios}\\ \hline
2&-1&0,1& (1-z^2)/z,\ z\\
2&1&-1,0&1,\ z^2-1\\
3&-1&1,2&-1,\ 1\\
3&2&-1,0&z,\ -z
\end{array}
\]
The first two lines would give \(z^2=1/2\) or \(2\), impossible for rational \(z\).
The last two contradict \(z\ne0\).

It follows that \(p=q=1\), since they are coprime.
The intervals have equal size and symmetric difference of size at most one, so \(I=J=[-1,0]\) and \(e=-1\) or \(0\).
This gives respectively \(k=1\) or \(k=-1\).
Reverse the polynomial if necessary to have \(k=1\).
The constant coefficient of (6) gives \(B_1=zA_1\); the coefficient at \(-1\) gives \(c=s A_1(z^2-1)\).
With \(A=A_1(Z-z)\) and \(B=B_1(Z-z)\), direct multiplication gives (2) with \(C=A_1\).
Conversely in this factorization (5) with \(e=-1\) is a root of \(F\), and \(RR^*=1\) makes it a root of \(F^\dagger\).
A nontorsion algebraic unit \(x\) cannot satisfy \(z x^n=s\), since the rational \(s/z\ne\pm1\) is not an algebraic unit.

<a id="gap"></a>
## The measure bound and the excluded product

Outside the stated exceptions, the two quadratics are coprime or have only a signed monomial common root.
In the first case their Sylvester determinant is a nonzero Laurent polynomial vanishing at every root of \(S\).
In the second case the monomial factor can be removed before taking the linear resultant.
The [two resultant estimates](Yin_C_G0345_v229.md#elimination) apply in either case, with width \(O(a+b+|j|)\) and an absolute bound on the number of terms.
Their proofs in these two cases require only coprimality or the asserted form of the common root.
The [width versus height bound](Yin_C_G0345_v186.md#statement) now gives \((a+b+|j|)m/d\gg1\).
The [endpoint and radial estimates](Yin_C_G0345_v229.md#outer) give \(wt\le\log K,\ dtm\ge1/20\), which prove (3) and the near-midpoint bound.
The normalization for reducible integral divisors follows by additivity and the measure of their content as in that proof.
For larger \(m\) decrease the constant in the bound using \(K\ge2\).

The four-term factor in (2) realizes every normalized exceptional binomial-block input of the [four-term comparison](Yin_C_G0345_v182.md#statement).
Indeed for \(B_0'(X)=uX^g+\epsilon v\) there, set \(z=-u/(\epsilon v)\) and multiply the first factor of (2) by \(\epsilon v\).
It becomes \(X^nB_0'(X)-sX^gB_0'(1/X)\), with \(n>g\).
Clearing rational coefficient denominators in (2) gives an actual integral five-term multiple.
Thus the product case retains the individual-factor question of the preceding four-term family.
