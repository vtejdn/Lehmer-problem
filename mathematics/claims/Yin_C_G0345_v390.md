# A uniform exponent determining all irreducible factors under power substitution

<a id="statement"></a>
## One exponent for every root with a relation of bounded length

Fix an integer \(L\ge2\), take the [radical denominator \(R_L\)](Yin_C_G0345_v210.md#statement) and put \(A_L=2R_L\).
Let \(\alpha\) be any nonzero nontorsion algebraic number satisfying a nonzero integer polynomial of coefficient one-norm at most \(L\).
Write \(P\) for its primitive integer minimal polynomial with positive leading coefficient.
Every rational irreducible factor \(B\) of \(P(X^{A_L})\) is hereditarily irreducible: \(B(X^n)\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\) for every \(n\ge1\).
For every \(N\ge1\), put \(g=\gcd(N,A_L)\).
The complete list of irreducible factors of \(P(X^N)\), normalized as above, is
\[
       H(X^{N/g}),\qquad H\ \text{an irreducible factor of }P(X^g).       \tag{1}
\]
In particular the number and the multiset of individual logarithmic Mahler measures depend only on \(g\).
The smallest log measure of any radical of \(\alpha\), over all positive orders and all complex choices, is attained by a factor of \(P(X^{A_L})\).
Here \(\log A_L\ll L^3\log(2L)\), independently of the degree or signature of \(\alpha\) and of the degree of its relation.
The [number-field good heredity theorem](../ledger/Yin_L_G0450_v1.md#good-heredity-source-comparison) supplies an exponent for a fixed seed.
We derive the uniform choice and exact gcd formula from the bounded denominator.

<a id="prime"></a>
## How late the last splitting step at one prime can occur

A root \(\eta\) of any dilation of \(P\) still has a relation of length at most \(L\), by applying the same substitution to the original relation.
For any root \(\eta_j\) with \(\eta_j^{p^j}=\eta\), the denominator theorem gives
\[
   j-v_p([\mathbb Q(\eta_j):\mathbb Q(\eta)])\le v_p(R_L).             \tag{2}
\]
Choose a coherent chain of successive \(p\)-th roots leading to \(\eta_j\).
At each step the degree over the preceding field is \(p\) if the prime binomial is irreducible; if it splits, each chosen factor has degree between one and \(p-1\).
Thus the left hand side of (2) counts the splitting steps on that chain exactly.

For odd \(p\), once \(X^p-z\) is irreducible over the current field, all successive \(p\)-power binomials over that field are irreducible.
Indeed \(z\) is not a \(p\)-th power, and the relative norm of a root of an irreducible \(X^{p^i}-z\) is \(z\).
If that root were a \(p\)-th power, so would be its norm.
The [prime binomial criterion](Yin_C_G0345_v387.md#substitutions) gives the induction.
Therefore any chain ending in a splitting step at depth \(j\) has split at every preceding step and \(j\le v_p(R_L)\).

For \(p=2\), consider a chain ending in a split and any preceding irreducible step over a field \(K\), from \(z\) to \(y\) with \(y^2=z\).
The two elements \(y,-y\) are conjugate over \(K\).
If \(y\) is not a square in \(K(y)\), neither sign is a square there; the [two-sign norm induction](Yin_C_G0345_v387.md#substitutions) then excludes every later split on the chain.
Thus the very next step splits, and \(N_{K(y)/K}(y)=-z\) is a square in \(K\).
Consequently \(K(y)\) contains \(i\), the ratio of \(y\) to a square root of \(-z\).
In a field containing \(i\), both signs of a nonsquare are nonsquares, so no further irreducible step can precede another split.
There was therefore at most one irreducible step before the final split, giving \(j\le v_2(R_L)+1\).
At either prime, after the indicated number of steps every resulting factor remains irreducible under all further powers of that prime.

<a id="order"></a>
## Saturating primes in decreasing order

Use the primes dividing \(A_L\) in decreasing order, taking \(v_p(A_L)\) root steps at \(p\) and keeping all branches.
The preceding calculation applies afresh from every branch at the start of each prime.
For an odd prime \(p\) already processed, adjunction of a root of smaller prime order \(\ell\) preserves the exclusion of \(p\)-th powers.
Indeed if \(z\) is not a \(p\)-th power in \(K=\mathbb Q(z)\) and \(w^\ell=z\), the extension \(K(w)/K\) has degree at most \(\ell<p\).
A \(p\)-th power \(w\) there would make \(z\) a \(p\)-th power in the same extension.
Taking norms and using Bézout with the extension degree would make \(z\) a \(p\)-th power in \(K\).
This argument applies at each smaller-prime step because \(\mathbb Q(w)\) contains \(\mathbb Q(z)\).
The prime two is processed last.
At any prime outside \(A_L\) the denominator theorem already gives prime-order irreducibility for every terminal root, since it has a relation of length at most \(L\).

For a terminal root \(z\), first adjoin its full required \(2\)-power root, an irreducible extension of that exact degree by the previous section.
Then adjoin the odd prime roots in any order.
The root of an irreducible \(X^m-z\) has norm \((-1)^{m+1}z\); for an odd prime the sign does not change the power class, and each step thus has its full degree.
This proves the hereditary assertion over \(\mathbb Q(z)\), hence for the rational minimal polynomial over \(\mathbb Q\).

<a id="gcd"></a>
## Recovering every factorization from the divisors of one exponent

Suppose more generally that \(P(X^A)\) has only hereditarily irreducible factors.
For \(g=\gcd(N,A)\) put \(n=N/g,\ m=A/g\), so \(\gcd(n,m)=1\).
Choose any root \(\eta\) of an irreducible factor \(H\) of \(P(X^g)\) and any \(m\)-th root \(\delta\) of \(\eta\).
The minimal polynomial of \(\delta\) is a factor of \(P(X^A)\), so \(X^n-\delta\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q(\delta)\).
Raising its \(n\) roots to the \(m\)-th power is an equivariant bijection onto the \(n\) roots of \(X^n-\eta\), since the \(m\)-th power map permutes the \(n\)-th roots of unity.
Transitivity over \(\mathbb Q(\delta)\) implies transitivity over \(\mathbb Q(\eta)\), giving irreducibility of \(H(X^n)\).
Distinct old factors have disjoint roots after the substitution, and primitive integer representatives stay primitive, giving (1) with no multiplicity changes.
Substitution preserves the log measure of each entire polynomial, including its leading coefficient.
Lifting a factor at level \(g\mid A\) to level \(A\) expresses its log measure as a sum of log measures of factors at \(A\), all positive since every root is nontorsion.
This proves attainment of the minimum.

<a id="sharp"></a>
## Why an extra dyadic step can be necessary

For \(P=X+4\), \(P(X^2)=X^2+4\) is irreducible, while
\[
              P(X^4)=(X^2+2X+2)(X^2-2X+2).
\]
Each quadratic generates \(\mathbb Q(i)\), and each of its roots has valuation one at the Gaussian prime \(1+i\).
Every binomial \(X^n-z\) above such a root is Eisenstein there.
Thus \(A=4\) works and the gcd formula shows that every reduced relative-degree denominator for descendants of this seed divides two.
Any exponent \(A\) making the factors hereditary must be divisible by four: otherwise (1) applied to \(N=4\) would give irreducibility from \(P\) or \(P(X^2)\).
The single-seed denominator two therefore cannot itself serve as the hereditary exponent.

<a id="plane"></a>
## A smaller exponent for primitive plane sections

In the [fixed plane setting](Yin_C_G0345_v389.md#statement) suppose that \(\alpha\) is a unit factor root satisfying the degree threshold there, and take the integer \(L\) to be the coefficient length of the plane polynomial.
Start with its finite set of nongood primes and close this set under prime divisors of \(\ell-1\) whenever \(\ell\) belongs to it.
Call the result \(S\); it is finite since all added primes are strictly smaller.
In (1) one can replace \(A_L\) by
\[
                  A_{F,L}=\prod_{p\in S}p^{v_p(A_L)}.                 \tag{3}
\]
To prove this, process just the primes dividing (3) as above.
Every step of prime order \(\ell\) occurs in an extension whose degree divides \(\ell(\ell-1)\), since the splitting field is obtained by first adjoining the \(\ell\)-th roots of unity and then one \(\ell\)-th root.
Thus the degree of the entire root field extension over \(\mathbb Q(\alpha)\) has all prime divisors in \(S\).
For any prime \(p\notin S\), if a terminal root were a \(p\)-th power in its field, so would be its \(A_{F,L}\)-th power \(\alpha\).
Descent by the norm and the coprime degree contradicts the good-prime exclusion.
Primes in \(S\) not dividing (3) are excluded by the bounded denominator at the terminal root.
When \(S\ne\varnothing\), it contains two, which divides the stated \(A_L\); for \(S=\varnothing\) the all-good-prime theorem already supplies heredity including the dyadic signs.
The same induction and gcd argument prove (3).

In particular for the reciprocal seeds \(U^s-U-1+V(1-U^{s-1}-U^s)\), \(s\ge3\), every factorization above a sufficiently large primitive direction depends on \(\gcd(N,1)=1\) if \(s\not\equiv2\bmod3\).
For \(s\equiv2\bmod3\) it depends only on \(\gcd(N,2^{1+v_2(R_6)})\), uniformly in the seed degree once its own primitive threshold is met.
In this case the finitely many dyadic tests determine every mixed odd and even dilation, while deciding which of those dyadic tests actually split remains a further question.
