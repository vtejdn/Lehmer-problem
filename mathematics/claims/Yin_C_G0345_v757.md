# Reciprocal roots and individual factor measures on powers of arithmetic progressions

<a id="statement"></a>
## Finitely many slopes can support a nontorsion inverse pair

Fix \(D\ge2,\ T\ge1,\ H\ge1\) integral, and let \(\vartheta>1\) be the real root of \(Z^3-Z-1\).
There is a finite set \(\mathcal E\subset\mathbb Q_{\ge0}\) with the following property.
For \(n\ge0,\ b\ge1,\ h\ge1,\ s\in\mathbb Z\), with \(n,b,h\) integers, take a nonzero Laurent polynomial
\[
         P(X)=X^s\sum_{j=0}^T c_jX^{h(n+bj)^D},
                 \qquad c_j\in\mathbb Z,\quad |c_j|\le H .
\]
If \(n/b\notin\mathcal E\), every pair of nonzero roots \(z,z^{-1}\) of \(P\) consists of roots of unity.
In particular every root on the unit circle is a root of unity, every reciprocal irreducible factor is cyclotomic up to a Laurent unit and rational scalar, and every integer Laurent factor of positive logarithmic Mahler measure has measure at least \(\log\vartheta\).
Over **all** \(n,b,h,s\), including the exceptional slopes, every such positive factor measure is bounded below by a constant \(c_{D,T,H}>0\).

For any fixed \(f\in\mathbb Z[Z]\) of degree \(D\ge2\), the same inverse-pair exclusion and the bound \(\log\vartheta\) hold for exponents \(s+hf(n+bj)\) whenever \(n>A_{f,T,H}(1+b)\).
In particular with a fixed step \(b\), only finitely many translations \(n\) can have a nontorsion inverse pair for this family of polynomials.
For arbitrary \(D\) these conclusions use bounded input coefficients.
The [quadratic progression bound](Yin_C_G0345_v752.md#statement) also covers unbounded integer coefficients in degree two at the cost of a different factor gap.

<a id="projection"></a>
## A projection of the support and the common hypersurfaces

Let \(S\subset\mathbb Z^r\) be finite, \(r\ge2\), and suppose some linear projection to \(\mathbb R^2\) arranges its points in strictly increasing first coordinates with strictly increasing slopes of consecutive chords.
For one or two points only the applicable conditions are imposed.
Let \(G\) be a Laurent polynomial with algebraic coefficients and nonempty support in \(S\), and put \(G^*(Y)=G(Y_1^{-1},\ldots,Y_r^{-1})\).
Every common irreducible factor of \(G,G^*\) over \(\overline{\mathbb Q}\), up to Laurent units, is \(Y^w-\zeta\) for a primitive nonzero \(w\in\mathbb Z^r\) and a root of unity \(\zeta\).

To see this, let \(g\) be a common irreducible factor.
Then \(g\) and \(g^*\) both divide \(G\).
If they are associates, the Newton polytope of \(g\) is centrally symmetric up to translation.
If they are distinct up to units, \(gg^*\mid G\), and its Newton polytope is the sum of the polytope of \(g\) with its negative.
In both cases the Newton polytope of \(G\) has a centrally symmetric Minkowski summand of dimension \(\dim\operatorname{Newt}(g)\).
Here the Newton polytope of a product is the Minkowski sum, since the exposed face polynomial in every direction is the product of the two nonzero face polynomials.

Suppose this summand has dimension at least two.
First perturb the given projection by an arbitrarily small amount so that it has rank two on the direction space of the summand.
The projections of rank less than two there satisfy a proper system of minor equations, whereas the strict ordering and slope inequalities persist on an open neighborhood.
The true support of \(G\), even after points of \(S\) are omitted, still projects to a strictly convex lower chain with a single upper chord.
Indeed slopes of skipped chords are weighted averages of the intervening consecutive slopes.
Such a hull has at most one direction of parallel distinct edges, since the slopes on the lower chain strictly increase.
On the other hand its projected centrally symmetric summand is a two-dimensional polygon with opposite edge pairs in at least two directions.
Both edges of each pair persist in the Minkowski sum by taking the exposed faces with the two opposite normals.
This is impossible, by the same planar argument as in the [Newton polygon estimate](Yin_C_G0345_v752.md#polygon).

Thus the polytope of the nonunit \(g\) is a segment.
After removing a monomial its exponents are multiples of a primitive direction \(w\), so it is univariate in \(Y^w\).
Absolute irreducibility gives \(g=Y^w-\lambda\) up to a unit, with \(\lambda\ne0\).
The inverse factor gives the value \(\lambda^{-1}\) for the same character.
Complete \(w\) to an integral lattice basis, and split the monomials of \(G\) into cosets of \(\mathbb Zw\).
On setting \(Y^w=\lambda\) or \(\lambda^{-1}\) each resulting coefficient, indexed by the other basis coordinates, vanishes.
The projection hypothesis rules out three collinear points of \(S\), so every nonempty coset has at most two terms.
It must have two, since a monomial cannot vanish at a nonzero point.
Its coefficient, after a monomial is removed, is \(c_0+c_1 Z^l\) with \(l\ge1\) and \(c_0c_1\ne0\).
Its two zeros at \(\lambda,\lambda^{-1}\), possibly identical, imply \(\lambda^{2l}=1\).
This proves the asserted description of all common hypersurfaces.

<a id="directions"></a>
## Exceptional directions lie in finitely many rational hyperplanes

For any fixed finite list of such \(G\), there is a finite set \(\mathcal B\subset\mathbb Z^r\setminus\{0\}\) such that the following implication holds.
If \(G(X^{u_1},\ldots,X^{u_r})\) is nonzero and vanishes at both \(z,z^{-1}\) for some nontorsion \(z\in\mathbb C^\times\), then \(B\cdot u=0\) for some \(B\in\mathcal B\).
Indeed \(z\) is algebraic since it is a root of a nonzero Laurent polynomial with algebraic coefficients.
The point \((z^{u_1},\ldots,z^{u_r})\) belongs to the common zero set of \(G,G^*\) in the torus.
On any common hypersurface \(Y^w=\zeta\) it satisfies \(w\cdot u=0\), since a nonzero power of a nontorsion element cannot be torsion.
The other irreducible components of the common zero set have codimension at least two.
For each such fixed component, [Bombieri, Masser and Zannier, Theorem 4.1](Yin_C_G0345_v236.md#source) gives a nonzero integer vector of bounded sup norm whose character takes the value one at the point.
Here the torsion twists of that theorem are all one, so this is again a relation on \(u\).
There are only finitely many components, finitely many \(G\) and finitely many relation vectors of bounded norm.
This proves the implication without a lower bound on the degree of \(z\) or an upper bound on the specialized width.

<a id="slopes"></a>
## Powers and polynomial translates

Take the integer support points \(w_j=(j,j^2,\ldots,j^D)\) for \(0\le j\le T\).
Projection onto the first two coordinates gives the required lower chain, since the adjacent slopes of the parabola are strictly increasing.
The bounds on \(c_j\) give a finite list of all nonzero \(G(Y)=\sum_j c_j Y^{w_j}\).
After subtracting the common exponent \(h n^D+s\), the power progression specializes with direction
\[
      u_k=h\binom Dk n^{D-k}b^k,\qquad 1\le k\le D .
\]
Consequently a relation with \(B\in\mathcal B\) implies
\[
                    \sum_{k=1}^D B_k\binom Dk(n/b)^{D-k}=0 .
\]
This univariate polynomial is nonzero since the powers of its argument are distinct and \(B\ne0\).
Taking its nonnegative rational roots over the fixed finite list of \(B\)'s gives \(\mathcal E\), independent even of common dilations.
For real coefficients a unit-circle root and its inverse are complex conjugates, giving the asserted unit-circle conclusion.

For the shifted polynomial \(f\), translation by the constant term of its Taylor expansion gives
\[
                u_k=h\,b^k f^{(k)}(n)/k! .
\]
For every \(k\) the derivative divided by \(k!\) is a polynomial of exact degree \(D-k\) with nonzero leading coefficient.
For \(n\) sufficiently large all these values are nonzero, and \(|u_{k+1}/u_k|\le C_f b/n\) for \(k<D\).
If \(n>A(1+b)\) with \(A\) sufficiently large in terms of \(f,\mathcal B\), the first nonzero term \(B_k u_k\) of any fixed relation strictly dominates the sum of the absolute values of its subsequent terms by a geometric series.
Thus no such relation holds.
Only nonzero specializations are under consideration, as required for a factor measure.

<a id="gap"></a>
## The exceptional slopes and the coefficient condition

At a slope with no nontorsion inverse pair, any nonconstant irreducible integral factor of positive logarithmic measure, after removal of a monomial, is either nonmonic up to sign or is the minimal polynomial of a nonreciprocal algebraic integer.
In the first case its measure is at least \(\log2\), and in the second it is at least \(\log\vartheta\) by [Smyth's theorem](../audits/literature/Smy71/Yin_U001.md#smy71-statement-and-conventions).
Integer content of absolute value greater than one also costs at least \(\log2\).
Multiplicativity now gives the bound for all factors at these slopes.

At a fixed exceptional ratio \(n/b=x/y\) in lowest terms, including \(0/1\), write \(n=mx,\ b=my\).
After a Laurent monomial is removed, the input is a dilation by \(h m^D\) of one of the finitely many polynomials \(\sum_j c_j X^{(x+yj)^D}\).
Only finitely many base polynomials arise over all exceptional ratios.
Every reciprocal nontorsion algebraic-unit root \(z\) from a dilation has a positive integral power \(\beta\) among the roots of this fixed list.
Write \(z^l=\beta\), \(K=\mathbb Q(\beta)\), \(s_0=[\mathbb Q(z):K]\), and \(\gamma=\operatorname{N}_{\mathbb Q(z)/K}(z)\).
Then \(\gamma^l=\beta^{s_0}\), hence \(\gamma\) is a nontorsion unit of \(K\) and its absolute logarithmic height satisfies \(h(\gamma)=s_0 h(z)\).
Thus \(m(\gamma)=[\mathbb Q(\gamma):\mathbb Q]h(\gamma)\le[\mathbb Q(z):\mathbb Q]h(z)=m(z)\).
The degrees of all such \(\gamma\)'s are bounded by the maximum degree of a base polynomial.
At bounded degree the positive total logarithmic measures of algebraic units are bounded away from zero, by finiteness of the possible monic polynomials of measure at most one in logarithmic units.
Together with the integer, nonunit and nonreciprocal cases this proves a uniform factor gap over all slopes and dilations.

Exceptional slopes can occur.
For \(D=3,\ T=2,\ n=0,\ b=h=1\) the polynomial \(X^8-987X+377\) has a reciprocal quadratic factor \(X^2-3X+1\), since reduction modulo that quadratic gives \(X^8=987X-377\).
There is also a reason to bound coefficients in the assertion about excluding inverse pairs.
Take the larger root \(\alpha>1\) of that quadratic.
For any three distinct exponents, the three powers of \(\alpha\) are rationally dependent since \(\mathbb Q(\alpha)\) has degree two.
No two are rationally dependent, since a nonzero power of \(\alpha\) is positive, different from one, and conjugate to its inverse.
Clearing denominators then gives an integer trinomial on the three exponents vanishing at both \(\alpha\) and its inverse, including on arbitrarily many slopes of the cubic progression.
