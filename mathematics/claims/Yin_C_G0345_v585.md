# Primitive exponent patterns for terminal rational relations at Salem numbers

<a id="statement"></a>
## Many primitive patterns with the same rational coefficients

Let \(\alpha\) be a nontorsion algebraic unit with a conjugate of modulus one, write \(m=\log M(\alpha)>0\), and let \(k\) be the larger of its numbers of expanding and contracting conjugates, counted over all complex embeddings.
Fix distinct nonzero integers \(t_1,\ldots,t_s\), \(s\ge1\), and nonzero rational coefficients \(a_i\).
There are at most
\[
 s\left\lceil\frac{k\log(64(s+1)^2)}m\right\rceil
\]
positive integers \(q\) for which \(\sum_i a_i\alpha^{qt_i}\) is rational, with the rational value allowed to vary freely.
For Salem numbers \(k=1\).
The count is independent of the sizes of all coefficients and exponents.

Consequently on any sequence of such units with bounded \(k\) and \(m\to0\), there are weights \(r\to\infty\) and, for each input, a single fixed ordered tuple of \(r-1\) nonzero rational coefficients with at least
\[
 \exp\big((1-o(1))(r-2)\log(1/m)\big)
\]
distinct primitive marked exponent lists realized by [terminal rational relations](Yin_C_G0345_v584.md#statement) having those outgoing coefficients.
Here one term is distinguished at exponent zero, the remaining exponents are sorted, and primitive means that all exponents are divided by their common positive gcd.
Terminality refers to each original realization, with all nonzero rational multiples of powers at arbitrary integer exponents allowed as intermediate terms.
Forgetting the mark and the coefficients, while allowing translation and positive dilation, loses at most a factor \(r\) in the count.

<a id="matrix"></a>
## A complex evaluation sum at separated dilations

Fix \(\beta\in\mathbb C\) with \(|\beta|=e^h>1\), positive integers \(u_1<\cdots<u_s\), and nonzero complex coefficients \(b_i\) of total absolute value \(S\).
Put \(L=\lceil\log(64(s+1)^2)/h\rceil\) and \(z=e^{-hL}\).
There are fewer than \(sL\) positive integers \(q\) satisfying
\[
                  \left|\sum_i b_i\beta^{qu_i}\right|\le4S.
\]
Indeed suppose \(L\le q_1<\cdots<q_s\) are such integers with consecutive differences at least \(L\), and write \(V_{ji}=\beta^{q_j u_i}\).
In the determinant expansion the largest product in absolute value is \(D=\exp(h\sum_j q_j u_j)\).
Sorting any permutation by adjacent swaps removes its inversions one at a time, increasing the logarithm of its product modulus by at least \(hL\) per swap.
Thus the sum of all other product moduli divided by \(D\) is at most
\[
 \sum_{\pi\ne1}z^{\operatorname{inv}(\pi)}
 =\prod_{a=1}^s(1+z+\cdots+z^{a-1})-1\le(1-z)^{-s}-1<1/2.
\]
The product formula follows by inserting the largest element at each possible place, and the last bound follows from \((1-z)^s\ge1-sz\) and \(sz<1/4\).
Hence \(|\det V|\ge D/2\) irrespective of phases.
The same argument bounds each deleted-row-and-column determinant by twice its own largest product, also for the empty minor of size zero.
After deleting row \(j\) and column \(i\), extend a maximizing matching of the remaining indices by joining \(j\) to \(i\).
Its full product has modulus at most \(D\), so the minor's largest product is at most \(D e^{-h q_j u_i}\).
Every inverse entry has modulus at most \(4z\).
Since each coordinate of \(Vb\) has absolute value at most \(4S\), inversion gives \(S\le16s^2 z S<S\), a contradiction.
Among admissible \(q\ge L\), repeatedly select the least remaining integer and discard it and the next \(L-1\) positions.
At most \(s-1\) integers are selected, and the initial \(L-1\) positions complete the bound.

<a id="rational"></a>
## Choosing an expanding place for the larger side

A neutral embedding bounds the absolute value of each rational output by \(A=\sum_i|a_i|\).
Choose the positive or negative exponents according to which side carries at least \(A/2\) of coefficient mass, and denote its mass by \(S\).
On the positive side take \(\beta\) an expanding conjugate of greatest modulus.
On the negative side take \(\beta\) the reciprocal of a contracting conjugate of least modulus, and reverse all exponents.
In either case \(h=\log|\beta|\ge m/k\), since the sum of the positive logarithms is \(m\) and the absolute sum of the negative logarithms is also \(m\) for a unit.
The unchosen side at this embedding has absolute value at most \(A-S\), so the sum on the chosen side has absolute value at most \(2A-S\le 4S\).
Applying the preceding estimate to its positive exponents gives the claimed bound, by monotonicity in the number of terms and in \(1/h\).
For general lists one first groups repeated exponents, deletes zero coefficients, and subtracts the rational constant at exponent zero.
If no terms remain, every dilation gives a rational output and the bound does not apply.

<a id="rays"></a>
## Removing dilations in the terminal count

Apply the [fixed-coefficient terminal count](Yin_C_G0345_v584.md#statement) with \(B=1\), permitted eventually since the minimum degree of an integral power tends to infinity along this sequence by the [power-field norm bound](../ledger/Yin_L_G0462_v270.md#descent).
For one ordered coefficient tuple at a weight \(r\to\infty\), it supplies at least \((c_0/m)^{r-2}\) sorted integer lists before division by the gcd.
For \(r\ge4\) the exponents of all \(r\) terms, including the distinguished exponent, are distinct.
Indeed two equal exponents give a two-term sum which is either zero, contradicting nondegeneracy, or an eligible rational multiple of a power, contradicting terminality since \(2\le r-2\).
Each primitive marked list therefore has distinct nonzero outgoing exponents, and its possible positive dilations number at most \((r-1)\lceil k\log(64r^2)/m\rceil\).
The logarithm of this factor for bounded \(k\) is \(O(\log r+\log(1/m))=o((r-2)\log(1/m))\), which proves the stated primitive count.
For an unmarked translated exponent set the primitive spacing is fixed by the gcd of all pairwise differences, and choosing the distinguished position fixes the sorted marked list, giving the factor \(r\).
