# A rank-two residue factor at the first nonunit pole

<a id="statement"></a>
## Restrictions on the trace and the primitive exponents

Fix integers \(q,n\ge2\), and write \(p=q+1,\ M=pq,\ L=2q+1\).
Let the monic integral polynomial \(H\) realize the [sign simplex](Yin_C_G0345_v891.md#statement) at pole value \(M^nH(1/M)=L^2\).
For its positive root \(w\), write \(T+T^{-1}=2+w,\ T>1\), and \(Y=(pT-q)/(p-qT)\).
The [common-power criterion](Yin_C_G0345_v883.md#criterion) gives \(T=\beta^u,\ Y=\beta^v\) with \(\beta>1\) and coprime integers \(1\le u<v\).
For odd \(v\), define the monic polynomial \(\Psi_v\in\mathbb Z[W]\) by
\[
 X^v-1=(X-1)X^{(v-1)/2}\Psi_v(X+X^{-1}-2).
\]
Then over \(R=\mathbb Z/L\mathbb Z\) there is a monic polynomial \(J\) of degree \(n-1\) satisfying
\[
 H(W)=(W+4)J(W),\qquad J\mid\Psi_v,\qquad J(-4)\in R^\times .
\]
In particular \(2n-1\le v\) and \(\gcd(H'(-4),L)=1\).
The minimal polynomial of \(\beta\) also has a reciprocal quadratic factor over \(R\) dividing \(X^u+1\).

If \(u,v\) have opposite parity, then \(|H(-4)|=L^2\).
If \(u\) is even and \(2^a\parallel u\), every prime \(\ell\mid L\) satisfies \(4^n\not\equiv1\pmod\ell\) and \(\ell\equiv\pm1\pmod{2^{a+2}}\).
In particular a prime divisor of \(L\) congruent to three or five modulo eight excludes every even short exponent.
This applies at \(q=2\) and rules out the [proposed family](../ledger/Yin_L_G0462_v324.md#order) with \(u=4\) and \(L=3^{2k}\) on this pole branch.
Write \(b=|H(-4)|/L^2\).
If \(3\mid L\), either \(b=1\) or \(3\mid b\), and odd \(v\) implies \(3\mid u\).
If \(5\mid L\) and \(v\) is odd, either \(5\mid u\) and \(5\mid b\), or \(3\mid u\) and \(b\equiv3\cdot4^{n-1}\pmod5\).
These tests apply to integral realization on every grid and in all degrees.

<a id="split"></a>
## Splitting the integral order at the coefficient sum

The field \(F=\mathbb Q(T)\) has degree \(2n\), with an involution \(\iota\) sending \(T\) to \(T^{-1}\) and \(Y\) to \(Y^{-1}\).
Bézout's identity expresses \(\beta\) as a product of integral powers of \(T,Y\), so \(F=\mathbb Q(\beta)\) and \(\iota(\beta)=\beta^{-1}\).
The order \(A=\mathbb Z[\beta]\) is free of rank \(2n\) and contains \(\beta^{-1}\), since \(\beta\) is a unit.
In this order put \(E=p-q\beta^u,\ E'=p+q\beta^v\).
The graph equation gives
\[
 EE'=L,\qquad E(\beta^v+1)=\beta^u+1,\qquad
 \iota(E)=E\beta^{v-u}.
\]
Since \(E\iota(E)=1-Mw\), the pole identity gives \(N_{F/\mathbb Q}(E)=L^2\).

Assume \(v\) is odd.
A maximal ideal containing \(E,E'\) would lie over a prime \(\ell\mid L\), with \(\ell\) odd and \(p,q\) both invertible in its residue field.
There \(\beta^u=-1\) and \(\beta^v=1\), giving conflicting values for \(\beta^{uv}\).
The Chinese remainder theorem therefore splits \(A/LA=A/EA\oplus A/E'A\) as an \(R\)-algebra.
At each \(\ell^s\parallel L\), its components are free over \(\mathbb Z/\ell^s\mathbb Z\).
Indeed, lifting bases of both summands modulo \(\ell\) gives a combined square matrix with invertible determinant and hence a basis of their direct sum.
The index \([A:EA]=|N(E)|=L^2\) now gives local ranks \(2\) and \(2n-2\), respectively.
Combining local bases also gives these ranks over \(R\).

On the first summand \(\beta^u=-1\), and on the second \(\beta^v=1\).
Both algebras are generated by the image of \(\beta\).
For a free algebra of rank \(d\) generated by one element over a local factor of \(R\), the first \(d\) powers starting at one give a basis.
Indeed they span and are independent modulo the residue prime by the one-generator presentation over a field, so their determinant is a unit.
Thus the characteristic polynomial of multiplication by the generator generates its ideal of polynomial relations, by monic division or the companion matrix in this basis.
The same conclusion over \(R\) follows by reduction to each prime power.
In particular the characteristic polynomial \(C\) on the first summand divides \(X^u+1\).

Inversion preserves \(EA\) by the formula for \(\iota(E)\).
It conjugates multiplication by \(\beta\) on the first summand to its inverse, so the determinant \(d\) of that multiplication satisfies \(d^2=1\).
Modulo every \(\ell\mid L\), its two eigenvalues are stable under inversion and neither equals one, since their \(u\)-th powers equal \(-1\).
They therefore form an inverse pair or are both \(-1\), giving \(d\equiv1\pmod\ell\).
As \(\ell\) is odd, \(d+1\) is invertible modulo \(\ell^s\), whence \(d=1\) over \(R\).
Thus \(C\) is reciprocal.
It divides the minimal polynomial of \(\beta\) modulo \(L\), since this polynomial is the characteristic polynomial on \(A\) and the reduction splits as above.

<a id="trace"></a>
## The complementary factor in the trace variable

The characteristic polynomial of multiplication by \(T\) on \(A\) is \(S_T(X)=X^nH(X+X^{-1}-2)\), since \(T\) generates \(F\).
On the first summand this operator is minus the identity.
On the second it still generates the algebra, since \(\gcd(u,v)=1\) and \(\beta^v=1\) there.
Its monic characteristic polynomial \(D\) therefore divides \(X^v-1\) in \(R[X]\).
The splitting gives \(S_T=(X+1)^2D\) over \(R\).
Evaluation at \(-1\) gives \(H(-4)=0\) in \(R\).
Writing \(H(W)=(W+4)J(W)\) and putting \(W=X+X^{-1}-2\) gives \(D=X^{n-1}J(W)\), because \(W+4=(X+1)^2/X\).
Since \(X^v-1\) takes the unit value \(-2\) at \(-1\), both \(D(-1)\) and \(J(-4)\) are units.

The polynomial \(D\) is reciprocal, with even degree and constant coefficient one.
Its monic cofactor in \(X^v-1\) is anti-reciprocal of odd degree, by cancellation of the monic factor \(D\).
The cofactor vanishes at one since two is a unit in \(R\).
Dividing by \(X-1\) leaves a monic reciprocal polynomial of even degree \(2a\).
After dividing by \(X^a\), pairing the powers \(X^j,X^{-j}\) and using the recurrence for \(X^j+X^{-j}\) in \(X+X^{-1}\) expresses the cofactor after removal of \(X-1\) as \(X^a P(W)\).
The same construction on \((X^v-1)/(X-1)\) over the integers defines \(\Psi_v\).
Comparison gives \(\Psi_v=JP\) since substitution of \(W\) is injective, as seen from its highest Laurent coefficient.

<a id="parity"></a>
## The endpoint norm when the exponents have opposite parity

In \(A/(\beta^v+1)A\), the graph identity gives \(\beta^u=\beta^v=-1\).
If the parities differ, evaluation of \(\beta^{uv}\) gives \(2=0\) there.
Then \(\beta^u=\beta^v=1\), and Bézout gives \(\beta=1\), so the quotient is a quotient of \(\mathbb F_2\).
Hence \(|N(\beta^v+1)|\) is either one or two.
For any even \(m\), \(\beta^m+1=\beta^{m/2}(\beta^{m/2}+\beta^{-m/2})\) has square absolute norm, since the expression in parentheses is in the fixed field of index two.
If \(v\) is even this applies immediately.
If \(u\) is even, \(|N(\beta^u+1)|=L^2|N(\beta^v+1)|\) gives the same conclusion.
Thus \(|N(\beta^v+1)|=1\) in either case.
Evaluating \(S_T(-1)\) gives \(|H(-4)|=|N(T+1)|=L^2\).

If \(u\) is even, coprimality gives odd \(v\) and so \(H'(-4)\) is invertible modulo \(L\).
The root locations give \(H(-4)=(-1)^n L^2\).
Expand \(M^nH(1/M)\) at \(-4\) using \(1/M+4=L^2/M\).
Dividing by \(L^2\) and reducing modulo \(\ell\mid L\) gives
\[
 1\equiv (-M)^n+M^{n-1}H'(-4)\pmod\ell.
\]
Since \(-M\equiv1/4\pmod\ell\), it follows that \(4^n\not\equiv1\pmod\ell\).

<a id="orders"></a>
## The even part of the short exponent

Assume \(v\) is odd and fix a prime \(\ell\mid L\).
Let \(z\) be a root of \(C\) in \(\overline{\mathbb F}_\ell\), and put \(y=z^v\).
If \(C\) splits, \(z\) lies in \(\mathbb F_\ell^\times\).
Otherwise Frobenius sends \(z\) to its inverse, so \(z\) belongs to the norm-one subgroup of \(\mathbb F_{\ell^2}^\times\).
These groups have orders \(\ell-1\) and \(\ell+1\), respectively.
The norm of \(Y+1\) is positive, since both real conjugates of \(Y\) are positive and the remaining places contribute absolute squares.
From \(E(Y+1)=T+1\) and the quadratic lift it follows that \(b=N(Y+1)\) is a positive integer.
Computing this determinant on the two summands modulo \(\ell\) gives
\[
 b\equiv 4^{n-1}(1+y)(1+y^{-1})\pmod\ell,
\]
since \(Y=1\) on the summand of rank \(2n-2\).

If \(2^a\parallel u\) with \(a\ge1\), then \(b=1\) by the endpoint result.
Since \(z^u=-1\) and \(v\) is odd, the order of \(y\) has even part exactly \(2^{a+1}\).
The determinant congruence gives \((1+y)^2/y=4^{1-n}\).
Thus \(x=(1+y)/2^{1-n}\) satisfies \(x^2=y\).
In the split case \(x\) lies in \(\mathbb F_\ell^\times\).
In the nonsplit case \(x^\ell=(1+y^{-1})/2^{1-n}=x/y=x^{-1}\), so \(x\) belongs to the same norm-one group.
Its order has even part \(2^{a+2}\), which proves the claimed congruence for \(\ell\).

For \(\ell=3,5\), the even short exponent is therefore impossible.
Suppose \(v\) is odd also.
At \(\ell=3\) the orders of the two groups are two and four, so \(z^u=-1\) with odd \(u\) forces \(z=-1\).
Thus \((X+1)^2\mid X^u+1\) in \(\mathbb F_3[X]\), which forces \(3\mid u\) by differentiation.
Also \(y=-1\) gives \(3\mid b\).
At \(\ell=5\) the group orders are four and six.
Here \(z\) has order two or six.
In the first case the same double-root argument gives \(5\mid u\) and \(5\mid b\).
In the second case \(3\mid u\), and \(y\) still has order six since \(v\) is odd and prime to \(u\).
Hence \(y+y^{-1}=1\), giving the asserted residue of \(b\).
