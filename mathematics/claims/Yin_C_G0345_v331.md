# Averaging the resultant of two independent slices of a band polynomial

<a id="statement"></a>
## A bound for the variation of the fiber Mahler measure

Let \(F(X,Y)=\sum_{j=0}^{M} A_j(X)Y^j\ne0\) have integral Laurent coefficients \(A_j\in\mathbb Z[X^{\pm1}]\).
Assume that the nonzero \(A_j\) have no common nonunit factor in \(\mathbb Q[X^{\pm1}]\).
Multiply \(F\) by a power of \(X\) so that its smallest and largest occupied \(X\)-exponents are \(0,s\), respectively.
Set \(d\nu(t)=dt/(2\pi)\) on the circle and define
\[
 B(t)=\max_j|A_j(e^{it})|,\qquad
 V(t)=m_Y(F(e^{it},Y)),\qquad I=m_2(F)=\int V\,d\nu .
\]
The common-factor assumption and the choice of exponent zero guarantee that the \(A_j\) have no common zero anywhere in \(\mathbb C\).
In particular \(V(t)\) is defined at every \(t\) and \(B(t)>0\).
If \(s=0\), then \(V=I\) and \(B\) is a positive integer.
For \(s\ge1\) the polynomial
\[
                      R(Y,Z)=\operatorname{Res}_X(F(X,Y),F(X,Z))
\]
is nonzero with integral coefficients, and
\[
 \int |V-I|^2\,d\nu
      \le sI-\tfrac12m_2(R)\le sI.                               \tag{1}
\]
In particular, writing \(L_\pm=\int\log^\pm B\,d\nu\), where \(\log^-x=\max(0,-\log x)\), one has
\[
 L_-\le \log(M+1)+\tfrac12\sqrt{sI},\qquad
 L_+\le L_-+I+M\log2.                                            \tag{2}
\]
These estimates remove the term \(\log(2d)\) inside the square root of the [squarefree-section bound](Yin_C_G0345_v328.md#statement) when \(s\le2d\).
The [resultant-one example](Yin_C_G0345_v328.md#example) still precludes a width-independent bound of order \(1+I\) for \(L_-\).
For a common base factor \(H(X)\), both the diagonal and the full Mahler measures contain \(m(H)\) additively when the diagonal specialization is nonzero, so the variation estimates apply to the quotient after content removal.

<a id="pair"></a>
## The resultant on the two parameter circles

Write \(P_y(X)=F(X,y)\), with leading \(X\)-coefficient \(c(y)=[X^s]F(X,y)\), a nonzero integral polynomial.
The \(X\)-degree is \(s\) for all but finitely many \(y\).
Fix one such \(y\), and consider all roots \(\alpha\) of \(P_y\).
Each \(F(\alpha,Z)\) is a nonzero polynomial because the \(A_j\) have no common complex zero.
We can pick \(z\) with \(c(z)\ne0\) outside the finitely many zeros of all these polynomials.
This gives two coprime fibers of full degree, proving that \(R\) is not identically zero.
Consequently two independent Haar parameters \(y,z\) give full-degree coprime polynomials almost surely, even if an individual fiber always has some repeated roots.

For any nonzero integral polynomial in finitely many variables its logarithm of absolute value is integrable on the product torus and its mean is nonnegative.
Indeed the positive part is bounded, and the inner Jensen integral is at least the logarithm of the absolute value of the leading coefficient in the variable being integrated.
That coefficient vanishes on a Haar null set, by iteration of the univariate zero count, so induction gives a finite lower bound and hence absolute integrability.
The induction starts with the logarithm of the absolute value of a nonzero integer.
Applying this to \(R\), \(F\) and \(c\) gives \(m_2(R)\ge0\) and \(I\ge0\), with \(\mathbb E_y m_X(P_y)=I\) by Fubini.
The possible vanishing of \(R\) on the parameter diagonal causes an integrable singularity.

<a id="energy"></a>
## The energy of the mean reflected zero measure

For a full-degree \(P_y\), list the \(s\) roots \(\alpha_i(y)\) with multiplicities and put
\[
 h_i(y)=\max(1,|\alpha_i(y)|),\qquad w_i(y)=\alpha_i(y)/h_i(y)^2.
\]
Let \(\sigma=\mathbb E_y\sum_{i=1}^s\delta_{w_i(y)}\), a positive measure of mass \(s\) on the closed unit disk.
The multiset of roots varies continuously wherever \(c(y)\ne0\), so the averaged measure is defined independently of root labels.
The estimates in this section use the [reflected pair inequality](../ledger/Yin_L_G0462_v70.md#energy).
For arbitrary roots \(\alpha,\beta\), write \(h=\max(1,|\alpha|)\), \(k=\max(1,|\beta|)\), \(w=\alpha/h^2\) and \(v=\beta/k^2\).
Then \(|1-w\overline v|\ge |\alpha-\beta|/(hk)\).
Equality holds for roots on opposite sides of the unit circle.
On the same side the normalized distance equals \(|w-v|\), so the bound follows from \(|1-w\bar v|^2-|w-v|^2=(1-|w|^2)(1-|v|^2)\).
For \(0<q<1\) we further have \(|1-q w\bar v|^2\ge q|1-w\bar v|^2\), by expanding the difference as \((1-q)(1-q|w\bar v|^2)\).

For a full-degree coprime pair use these inequalities for all \(s^2\) cross pairs, along with the product formula for the resultant and univariate Jensen.
They give
\[
 \begin{split}
 -\sum_{i,j}\log|1-qw_i(y)\overline{w_j(z)}|
 &\le s\bigl(m_X(P_y)+m_X(P_z)\bigr)\\
 &\quad-\log|R(y,z)|-\tfrac{s^2}{2}\log q.                       \tag{3}
 \end{split}
\]
Indeed \(|R(y,z)|=|c(y)c(z)|^s\prod_{i,j}|\alpha_i(y)-\alpha_j(z)|\).
Each \(h_i(y)\) or \(h_j(z)\) occurs \(s\) times, so its logarithm combines with the corresponding leading-coefficient term into \(s\,m_X(P_y)\) or \(s\,m_X(P_z)\).
All three unsmoothed functions on the right of (3) are integrable over the two parameter circles by the preceding section.

For fixed \(q\), the kernel on the left has a uniformly absolutely convergent logarithm series on the two closed disks and is bounded.
The independence of \(y,z\) changes the integral of each power product into a squared moment of \(\sigma\), so (3) yields
\[
 \sum_{\ell\ge1}\frac{q^\ell}{\ell}
                \left|\int w^\ell\,d\sigma(w)\right|^2
       \le 2sI-m_2(R)-\tfrac{s^2}{2}\log q .
\]
The series now has nonnegative terms.
Letting \(q\uparrow1\) by monotone convergence gives
\[
 \mathcal E(\sigma):=
 \sum_{\ell\ge1}\frac1\ell\left|\int w^\ell\,d\sigma(w)\right|^2
                 \le 2sI-m_2(R).                                 \tag{4}
\]
Independent slices allow all the root pairs to be included with their multiplicities, eliminating the regularized diagonal loss in the single-polynomial discriminant calculation.

<a id="logarithms"></a>
## From reflected moments to the real-circle coefficient sizes

On \(|X|=1\), reflection of a root gives \(|e^{it}-\alpha_i(y)|=h_i(y)|1-w_i(y)e^{-it}|\).
The function \(\log|1-we^{-it}|\) has mean zero and positive part bounded by \(\log2\) for every \(|w|\le1\), so its \(L^1(d\nu)\)-norm is at most \(2\log2\) uniformly, including boundary points.
Average the logarithm of the factorization of each \(P_y\) and use Fubini to obtain
\[
                 V(t)-I=\int \log|1-w e^{-it}|\,d\sigma(w)
\]
for almost every \(t\).
The Fourier coefficients of this centered potential at the opposite frequencies \(\ell,-\ell\), \(\ell\ge1\), are conjugates with magnitude \(|\int w^\ell\,d\sigma|/(2\ell)\).
Radial approximation in \(L^1\) gives the kernel formula also at \(|w|=1\).
Indeed the logarithms at \(w\) and \(\rho w\) have equal means, and the positive part of their difference is at most \(\log(2/(1+\rho))\) by the [disk modulus calculation](Yin_C_G0345_v328.md#energy).

For every \(t\), expansion of \(F(e^{it},Y)\) into linear factors bounds all its coefficients by \(2^M e^{V(t)}\), also when the degree drops or the fiber is a nonzero constant.
The triangle inequality on \(|Y|=1\) gives the opposite bound \(V(t)\le\log B(t)+\log(M+1)\).
Thus \(V\) is bounded between bounded functions since \(B\) is continuous and positive.
Parseval now gives
\[
       \int|V-I|^2\,d\nu
        =\tfrac12\sum_{\ell\ge1}\frac1{\ell^2}
                         \left|\int w^\ell\,d\sigma(w)\right|^2
        \le \tfrac12\mathcal E(\sigma),
\]
which proves (1).
As \(I\ge0\), the negative part of \(V\) is at most the negative part of \(V-I\), whose integral is half the \(L^1\)-norm of \(V-I\).
The pointwise upper bound for \(V\) and Cauchy–Schwarz then yield the first inequality of (2).
The second follows from \(\int\log B\,d\nu\le I+M\log2\).
These bounds permit \(L_-\) to grow with \(s\) at fixed positive \(I\).
The diagonal Mahler comparison for all moving coefficients remains a separate question.
