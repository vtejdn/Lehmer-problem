# Moving coefficients of the reflected denominator and their equality cases

<a id="statement"></a>
## The coefficient defect and the roots

Let \(P\) be a monic integer polynomial of degree \(d\ge1\), with \(|P(0)|=1\) and no root on the unit circle.
Write \(m=\log M(P)\) and \(L=2m\).
Reflect each exterior root \(\alpha\) to \(1/\bar\alpha\) and keep each interior root, with multiplicities.
Write the resulting points as \(\zeta_j=e^{-\ell_j+i\theta_j}\), so that \(\sum_j\ell_j=L>0\), and let \(\mu=L^{-1}\sum_j\ell_j\delta_{e^{i\theta_j}}\).
The [reflected denominator](Yin_C_G0345_v621.md#evaluation) gives the real finite Blaschke product \(J=O^*/O\) with precisely these zeros.
Set \(b=J(0)\), \(s=\operatorname{sign}(b)\), and \(J_n=[z^n]J\), so \(|b|=e^{-L}\).
For any positive integer \(g\) and \(\epsilon\in\{-1,1\}\), define
\[
     \delta=1-\frac{\epsilon J_g}{1-b^2},\qquad \sigma=-s\epsilon.
\]
The [endpoint bound](Yin_C_G0345_v640.md#endpoints) gives \(\delta\ge0\).

1. Exact equality \(\delta=0\) forces \(g=d\) and
   \[
       P(0)P(z)P^\#(z)=z^{2d}-Tz^d+1,\qquad
       T=\sigma\,2\cosh(2m)\in\mathbb Z,\qquad |2-T|=P(1)^2,        \tag{1}
   \]
   where \(P^\#(z)=z^dP(1/z)\).
   In particular \(P\) and \(P^\#\) are coprime and \(M(P)\ge(1+\sqrt5)/2\).
   The polynomial \(z^2-z-1\) attains both equalities for a suitable sign and \(g=2\).

2. At every reflected root one has
   \[
       (1-e^{-2\ell_j})\,|b+\epsilon e^{-g\ell_j+ig\theta_j}|^2
                \le 2(1-b^2)\delta.                                  \tag{2}
   \]
   As \(L\to0\), uniformly over all \(g\ge1\) with \(gL\le1\),
   \[
       \frac{J_g}{1-b^2}=-s\widehat\mu(g)+O(gL),
       \qquad \widehat\mu(g)=\int z^{-g}\,d\mu(z).                   \tag{3}
   \]
   Therefore along any sequence with \(gL\to0\), one has \(\delta\to0\) if and only if \(\int|z^g-\sigma|^2\,d\mu(z)\to0\).
   If \(L,\delta\to0\) and some root has \(\ell_j\ge cL\) for a fixed \(c>0\) at each index, then \(gL=O_c(L+\sqrt\delta)\) and the same angular integral is \(O_c(L+\sqrt\delta)\).
   In particular, on any subsequence with \(L,\delta\to0\) and \(gL\) bounded away from zero, the largest individual radial weight \(\max_j\ell_j/L\) tends to zero.

3. For arbitrary \(g\), all multiples satisfy
   \[
   0\le 1+\frac{s\sigma^k J_{kg}}{1-b^2}
      \le k^2\delta+(k^2-1)\frac{1-|b|}{1+|b|}
                   \qquad(k\ge1).                                    \tag{4}
   \]
   Thus \(\delta,L\to0\) forces signed saturation simultaneously at the multiples \(kg\) for \(1\le k\le K\) whenever \(K^2(\delta+L)\to0\), without a restriction on \(gL\).

<a id="identity"></a>
## An analytic residual and exact arithmetic equality

Use Haar probability measure on the unit circle and define
\[
     D(z)=J(z)-b-\epsilon z^g(1-bJ(z)).
\]
The two summands \(J-b\) and \(z^g(1-bJ)\) have squared norm \(1-b^2\).
Their inner product is \(J_g\), since \((J-b)(1-b\bar J)=J-2b+b^2\bar J\) on the circle.
Consequently
\[
                         \|D\|_2^2=2(1-b^2)\delta.                    \tag{5}
\]
At a zero \(\zeta_j\) of \(J\), evaluation of the Taylor series of \(D\) by Cauchy--Schwarz gives \((1-|\zeta_j|^2)|D(\zeta_j)|^2\le\|D\|_2^2\), proving (2).

When \(\delta=0\), the analytic identity \(D=0\) gives
\[
                 J(z)=\frac{b+\epsilon z^g}{1+\epsilon b z^g}.
\]
This rational function has \(g\) simple zeros at \(z^g=q\), where \(q=-\epsilon b=\sigma e^{-L}\).
The reflected product has degree \(d\), hence \(g=d\).
There is exactly one reflected zero on each of the \(d\) rays with \(e^{id\theta}=\sigma\).
The pair obtained from the original root on such a ray and its inverse conjugate consists of the two points \(z^d=q\) and \(z^d=q^{-1}\) on that ray.
Since \(P\) is real, its inverse conjugates are exactly the roots of \(P^\#\) as a multiset.
Comparing the monic products gives (1) with \(T=q+q^{-1}\).
Their roots are all simple, giving coprimality.
Integrality gives \(T\in\mathbb Z\), and evaluation at one gives \(|2-T|=P(1)^2\).
Now \(|T|=M(P)^2+M(P)^{-2}>2\), so \(|T|\ge3\) gives the asserted lower bound.
For \(P=z^2-z-1\) the reflected roots are \(\pm(\sqrt5-1)/2\).
The rational Blaschke product with these zeros and real constant \(b\) equals \((b+\epsilon z^2)/(1+\epsilon b z^2)\) for the sign making \(-\epsilon b=(3-\sqrt5)/2\), since both products have the same zeros and constant value.
This proves sharpness.

<a id="moving"></a>
## A uniform Taylor estimate at a moving order

Put \(F=J/b\).
The normalized factors of \(F\) are \((1-z/\zeta_j)/(1-\bar\zeta_j z)\).
As in the [fixed-order logarithmic expansion](Yin_C_G0345_v639.md#angles), write
\[
     d_n=[z^n](-\log F)
        =\frac{2}{n}\sum_j \sinh(n\ell_j)e^{-in\theta_j}.
\]
For \(1\le n\le g\) and \(gL\le1\), this gives \(|d_n|\le3L\) and
\[
      d_g=2L\widehat\mu(g)+O\bigl(L(gL)^2\bigr),
\]
using \(|\sinh t-t|\le C t^3\) on \([0,1]\) and \(\sum_j\ell_j^3\le L^3\).
In the exponential \(F=\exp(-\sum_{n\ge1}d_nz^n)\), the terms with \(r\ge2\) factors from the logarithm contribute at most \((3L)^r\binom{g-1}{r-1}/r!\) to the absolute value of the coefficient of \(z^g\).
Summing this over \(2\le r\le g\) gives at most \(3L(e^{3gL}-1)=O(gL^2)\).
Thus \(J_g=b(-d_g+O(gL^2))\).
Since \(1-b^2=2L+O(L^2)\), this proves (3) with an absolute constant.
Conjugation symmetry makes \(\widehat\mu(g)\) real, so the angular integral is \(2(1-\sigma\widehat\mu(g))=2\delta+O(gL)\).

If \(\ell_j\ge cL\), the quotient \((1-b^2)/(1-e^{-2\ell_j})\) is bounded in terms of \(c\) as \(L\to0\).
Taking moduli in (2) and using the reverse triangle inequality gives \(e^{-g\ell_j}\ge e^{-L}-O_c(\sqrt\delta)\).
Taking logarithms near one yields \(g\ell_j=O_c(L+\sqrt\delta)\).
Then \(gL\le g\ell_j/c\), so (3) applies eventually and gives the stated angular estimate.
The conclusion for the largest weight follows by passing to any subsequence on which it is bounded below.

<a id="multiples"></a>
## Fourier coefficients at multiples of the index

The circle density \((1-s\operatorname{Re}J)/(1-|b|)\) with respect to Haar probability measure defines a probability \(\nu\).
For \(n\ge1\), its \(n\)th Fourier coefficient is \(-sJ_n/(2(1-|b|))\).
For \(|w|=1\), telescoping the powers gives \(|1-w^k|\le k|1-w|\).
Apply the squared inequality with \(w=\sigma z^g\) and integrate against \(\nu\).
Since \(J\) is real on the real axis, all these Fourier coefficients are real and we get
\[
   1+\frac{s\sigma^k J_{kg}}{2(1-|b|)}
      \le k^2\left(1-\frac{(1+|b|)(1-\delta)}2\right).
\]
Rearranging proves the upper bound in (4), and the endpoint coefficient bound gives its lower bound.

The single factor \(J=(b-z)/(1-bz)\) with \(b=e^{-L}\) has \(J_g/(1-b^2)=-b^{g-1}\) and \(\widehat\mu(g)=1\), so the approximation in (3) can fail when \(gL\) stays positive.
Conversely, saturation can occur at large radial scale for real finite Blaschke products of degree \(g\).
Taking \(b=e^{-L}\) and \(J=(b+z^g)/(1+bz^g)\) gives \(\delta=0\) for \(\epsilon=1\) and \(\ell_j=L/g\) for every zero.
One may choose \(g=\lceil L^{-2}\rceil\) as \(L\to0\), which gives \(gL\to\infty\).
This example concerns the analytic product alone.
Equation (1) excludes its realization by the stated integral denominators for all sufficiently small \(L\).
