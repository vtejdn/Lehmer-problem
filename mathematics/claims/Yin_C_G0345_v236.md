# Finite reciprocal Laurent families for factors of sparse polynomials

<a id="statement"></a>
## A finite reduction with a bound on the number of variables

Fix integers \(s\ge2\) and \(H\ge1\).
There is a finite collection of primitive integral Laurent polynomials \(Q\), irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\), with the following properties.
Each has \(q\) variables, its support differences span \(\mathbb Q^q\), and
\[
  1\le q\le\lfloor s/2\rfloor,\qquad
  Q(Y^{-1})=\epsilon Y^w Q(Y),\qquad \epsilon\in\{1,-1\},\quad w\in\mathbb Z^q .
\]
Here inversion is applied to every variable, and \(Y^w=\prod_iY_i^{w_i}\).
Let \(P\) be a nonzero integral polynomial with at most \(s\) terms and primitive coefficient height at most \(H\) after removing a monomial and content.
For every primitive irreducible reciprocal noncyclotomic factor \(S\) of \(P\) with nonzero constant coefficient there are such a \(Q\) and a vector \(v\in\mathbb Z^q\) for which
\[
              S(X)\mid Q(X^{v_1},\ldots,X^{v_q})\mid P(X)
                   \quad\text{in }\mathbb Q[X^{\pm1}].
\]
The middle polynomial is nonzero.
For each input with \(p\) terms the stronger bound \(q\le\lfloor p/2\rfloor\) holds.
The height and number of terms of the middle polynomial are bounded in terms of \(s,H\), allowing monomial and primitive normalization.

Consequently, any sequence of positive-measure factors with measures tending to zero gives, on passing to positive-measure irreducible factors and a subsequence, a single \(Q\) as above with \(2\le q\le\lfloor s/2\rfloor\).
For \(s=4,5\) one must have \(q=2\), and along this subsequence
\[
            \min\{\|b\|_\infty:0\ne b\in\mathbb Z^2,\ b\cdot v=0\}
                    \longrightarrow\infty .
\]
This controls the multiple of each factor.
Factorization after specialization and the measures of the separate factors of such a multiple remain relevant even when its coefficients at distinct support points are preserved.

<a id="source"></a>
## Bounded relations on a codimension-two intersection

We use Bombieri, Masser and Zannier, Theorem 4.1, in its untwisted case.
If \(W\subset\mathbb G_m^r\) is an irreducible variety defined over \(\overline{\mathbb Q}\) of dimension at most \(r-2\) and \((\alpha^{u_1},\ldots,\alpha^{u_r})\in W\) for integral \(u_i\) and nontorsion algebraic \(\alpha\), there is a nonzero \(b\in\mathbb Z^r\), bounded in sup norm in terms of \(W\), with \(b\cdot u=0\).
Indeed the source gives a bounded nontrivial character taking value one at the point, and \(\alpha\) has infinite multiplicative order.
We checked the published theorem and its hypotheses in Section 4, pp. 20–21, and accept it at statement and applicability depth.
The source permits torsion twists, all taken to be one here. [BMZ07, Theorem 4.1](https://doi.org/10.1093/imrn/rnm057)

Amoroso, Leroux and Sombra use this bounded relation to restrict a sparse system to subtori and recover its common nontorsion zeros from a multivariate gcd.
In the author's edition dated March 17, 2014, Theorem 3.1 gives the bounded-relation statement on p. 11, and Algorithm 4.2 and Theorem 4.3 give the gcd construction on pp. 15–16.
We inspected these passages for comparison.
The argument below follows the chosen irreducible factor and its inversion at every restriction, since a multivariate gcd can also contain a pair of distinct factors exchanged by inversion. [ALS15, author edition](../literature/pdf/ALS15--author.pdf#page=11)

<a id="descent"></a>
## Following a reciprocal factor through restrictions

Fix the nonzero coefficients \(c_0,\ldots,c_{p-1}\) of the normalized multiple in order of increasing exponent.
There are finitely many choices.
A one-term input has no factor of the indicated kind.
For \(p\ge2\) start with \(L=c_0+\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}c_iY_i\) in \(r=p-1\) variables and a vector \(u\) of its positive exponents, so \(L(X^u)=P(X)\) after normalization.
Choose any root \(\alpha\) of \(S\).
At every stage the current Laurent polynomial \(L\), with at most \(p\) terms, specializes to that same normalized nonzero \(P\).
Factor \(L\) over the rational Laurent ring.
Some irreducible factor \(G\) vanishes at \(\alpha^u=(\alpha^{u_1},\ldots,\alpha^{u_r})\), and every factor has nonzero specialization because their product does.
All coefficients of \(G\) are rational, and \(\alpha^{-1}\) is a conjugate of \(\alpha\), so \(G((\alpha^{-1})^u)=0\) as well.
Write \(G^\iota(Y)=G(Y^{-1})\).

If \(G^\iota\) and \(G\) are associates, retain \(G\).
Otherwise they are coprime and their common zero set over \(\overline{\mathbb Q}\) has codimension at least two.
Indeed any common geometric hypersurface factor and all its Galois conjugates would divide both polynomials, giving a rational common factor after normalizing and multiplying its distinct conjugates.
There are finitely many irreducible components, each defined over \(\overline{\mathbb Q}\).
The [bounded-relation theorem](#source) on a component through \(\alpha^u\) gives a bounded nonzero relation \(b\cdot u=0\).
Dividing by the gcd of its entries preserves this equality and makes \(b\) primitive.
The quotient \(\mathbb Z^r/\mathbb Z b\) is free of rank \(r-1\).
Choose a basis of this quotient once for each \(b\) and send every monomial exponent to its quotient class.
The map \(m\mapsto m\cdot u\) factors through the quotient, giving integer exponents for the next specialization.
The resulting image of the whole polynomial \(L\) still specializes to \(P\), with at most \(p\) terms since restricting monomials can only identify their exponents.
Refactor this image and repeat.

At each fixed node there are finitely many rational irreducible factors up to associates, finitely many components for each intersection, and finitely many \(b\)'s within their bounds.
The dimension decreases at every restriction, and in dimension one two coprime Laurent polynomials cannot share a torus point.
Thus the tree has finitely many terminal factors for all exponent choices.
Choose primitive integral representatives.
The rational Laurent unit relating \(G^\iota\) to \(G\) is a monomial times a scalar of square one by applying inversion twice.

<a id="rank"></a>
## Newton polytopes, support and coefficient bounds

Let \(N(G)\) denote the convex hull of the support of a terminal factor, of affine dimension \(q\ge1\).
The reciprocity identity makes this polytope centrally symmetric.
Its opposite vertex pairs, measured from its center, span its \(q\)-dimensional direction space, so it has at least \(2q\) vertices.
Writing \(L=G B\), we have \(N(L)=N(G)+N(B)\) since the leading forms in each direction multiply without vanishing.
For each vertex of \(N(G)\) choose a linear functional exposing it uniquely, perturbed to expose a unique vertex of \(N(B)\) as well.
The sum is then a vertex of \(N(L)\), and its decomposition into points of the two summands is unique by this exposing functional.
Distinct vertices of \(N(G)\) therefore give distinct vertices of \(N(L)\).
Since \(L\) still has at most \(p\) terms, \(2q\le p\).

Shift one exponent of \(G\) to zero and take the saturated lattice \(\mathbb Z^r\cap\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb Q}(\operatorname{supp}(G)-\operatorname{supp}(G))\).
Its basis extends to a basis of \(\mathbb Z^r\), so the shifted polynomial is a \(q\)-variable Laurent polynomial \(Q\) in these coordinates.
Irreducibility is preserved, since any nontrivial factorization over the smaller Laurent ring remains nontrivial after adjoining independent Laurent variables.
Reciprocity is preserved as well, since the translating exponent relating the two supports belongs to the saturated lattice.
The exponent vector \(v\) pairs the old specialization with each member of the chosen basis.
Up to the removed monomial and scalar, \(Q(X^v)\) specializes the chosen factor of \(L\).
It is a nonzero divisor of \(P\) vanishing at \(\alpha\), hence \(S\mid Q(X^v)\).
Fixed basis choices at the finitely many leaves give the asserted finite collection.

In every specialization of \(Q\), colliding terms add their coefficients, so the coefficient height is at most \(\sum_m|[Y^m]Q|\) and the number of terms is at most \(\#\operatorname{supp}Q\).
If \(v\) pairs nontrivially with every nonzero difference of support points of \(Q\), no terms collide and the entire coefficient multiset is preserved.
In particular sufficiently large shortest orthogonal relation guarantees this for a fixed \(Q\).
In general even a flat input, meaning that all its nonzero coefficients have modulus one, only yields the stated bounds on its selected multiple.
Neither factorization of the restricted \(L\) nor addition at coincident exponents imposes flat coefficients on the resulting factors.

<a id="rank-one"></a>
## Positive measure in a fixed one-variable family

For a shrinking sequence we may take irreducible factors and discard integer content by nonnegativity and additivity of logarithmic measure.
Once \(m<\log 2\) an irreducible positive-measure factor is a monic unit polynomial up to sign by the two endpoint bounds.
The [nonreciprocal gap of Smyth](../audits/literature/Smy71/Yin_U001.md#smy71-u001) then gives reciprocity throughout a tail of the sequence.
Finiteness permits choosing a single \(Q\).

If \(q=1\), a nonzero specialization containing \(\alpha\) gives a nonzero power \(\beta=\alpha^v\) among the finitely many roots of \(Q\).
Indeed \(v=0\) would give a constant specialization.
Set \(K=\mathbb Q(\alpha)\), \(E=\mathbb Q(\beta)\), \(e=[K:E]\) and \(\gamma=N_{K/E}(\alpha)\).
Then \(\gamma^v=\beta^e\), so \(\gamma\) is a nontorsion unit of degree at most \([E:\mathbb Q]\).
Its absolute logarithmic height satisfies \(h(\gamma)=e h(\alpha)\), hence \(m(\gamma)\le m(\alpha)\).
The finitely many possible \(\beta\) bound the degree of \(\gamma\).
There are finitely many monic integer polynomials of bounded degree and logarithmic measure at most one, since the elementary symmetric functions of their roots are bounded by the corresponding binomial coefficients times \(\exp(1)\).
Thus the positive \(m(\gamma)\) have a uniform lower bound, excluding rank one.

Finally suppose \(q=2\) and some subsequence has bounded shortest orthogonal relation.
After another subsequence the same nonzero primitive \(b\) annihilates every \(v\), so \(v=jv_0\) for a fixed primitive generator \(v_0\) of its integral kernel.
The single polynomial \(Q(X^{v_0})\) is nonzero since its subsequent specialization at \(X^j\) is nonzero.
The same one-variable argument applied to its finitely many nonzero roots rules out the subsequence.
This proves the two-variable assertion while leaving open the possibility that a positive-measure irreducible factor carries only a small fraction of the measure of a specialized multiple.
