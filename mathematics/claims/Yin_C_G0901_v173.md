# The degree forced by vanishing initial moments at a rational pole with one denominator prime

<a id="statement"></a>
## A congruence that survives dropping integrality at any one prime

Let \(q\ge2\) be an integer, \(p=q+1\), \(K=(2q+1)^2\), and let \(\ell\) be any prime.
Take a monic reciprocal polynomial \(S(Z)\in\mathbb Z[1/\ell][Z]\) of degree \(2D\) subject to the exact pole condition
\[
                          q^{2D} S(p/q)=K .
\]
Suppose its first \(J\) coefficients after the leading one vanish, where \(1\le J<2D\).
Equivalently its first \(J\) power sums of roots vanish, by Newton's identities in characteristic zero.
For each fixed \(J\ge2\), for all sufficiently large \(q\) in terms of \(J\),
\[
                 D\gg_J q^{J-1}.
\]
If \(J\) is even and \(\ell\nmid q\), the stronger bound \(D\gg_J q^J\) holds.
In particular \(J\ge4\) and \(q\ge240\) imply \(D>2q^3\).
For any sequence with \(q,J\to\infty\), the ratio \(D/q^A\) tends to infinity for every fixed \(A>0\), even if the omitted prime moves.

This concerns exact zeros, a stronger restriction than merely requiring the [initial trace coefficients to be integral](Yin_C_G0345_v889.md#statement).
For an actual integral polynomial with the [strict root signature](Yin_C_G0345_v891.md#return), the sharper classical bound on the degree in terms of the small positive log Mahler measure already applies without a vanishing prefix. [Voutier's bound](../audits/literature/Vou96/Yin_U001.md#height)
Here no conditions on the complex roots, irreducibility or size of the denominator are used.
The [single-prime construction with vanishing power sums](Yin_C_G0901_v169.md#statement) and the [single-prime realization at the specified pole](Yin_C_G0901_v171.md#statement) can thus have all these particular constraints simultaneously only at the indicated degree cost.
At very large degrees the bound leaves existence open.

<a id="congruence"></a>
## Two evaluations supply at least one denominator-free modulus

Write \(S(Z)=\sum_{i=0}^{2D} b_i Z^{2D-i}\).
The two end coefficients are one and \(b_i=b_{2D-i}=0\) for \(1\le i\le J\).
Homogenization gives \(K=\sum_i b_i p^{2D-i}q^i\).
If \(\ell\nmid q\), reduction modulo \(q^{J+1}\) therefore gives \(p^{2D}\equiv K\); if \(\ell\mid q\), reduction at the other end modulo \(p^{J+1}\) gives \(q^{2D}\equiv K\).
The respective moduli are invertible with respect to the possible denominator, so these are ordinary integer congruences.
With \(x=q\) in the first case and \(x=-p\) in the second, both read
\[
                   (1+x)^{2D}\equiv(1+2x)^2\bmod |x|^{J+1}.              \tag{1}
\]
In particular \(|x|\ge q\); choosing the other end when necessary is what permits \(\ell\mid q(q+1)\).

Set \(L(X)=\log(1+2X)/\log(1+X)=\sum_{j\ge0} c_jX^j\) in \(\mathbb Q[[X]]\), with \(c_0=2\).
For each \(J\ge1\) there is a positive integer \(B_J\) depending only on \(J\), clearing the denominators of \(c_0,\ldots,c_{J-1}\), such that (1) gives
\[
             B_J D\equiv B_J\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}c_j x^j \bmod |x|^J.        \tag{2}
\]
To see this use an indeterminate \(Y\) and \(E=2\log(1+X)(Y-L(X))\).
Over \(\mathbb Q[Y][[X]]\) one has
\[
 \frac{(1+X)^{2Y}-(1+2X)^2}{X}=(Y-L(X))U(X,Y),\qquad
 U=(1+2X)^2\frac{2\log(1+X)}{X}\sum_{j\ge0}\frac{E^j}{(j+1)!}.
\]
The constant coefficient of \(U\) is two.
Thus \(V=U^{-1}\) belongs to the same formal ring.
Choose \(B_J\) also to clear all polynomial coefficient denominators in \([X^i]V\) for \(i<J\).
Multiply the left hand side by the degree-\((J-1)\) truncation of \(B_JV\) and substitute \(Y=D\).
At this integer value the divided difference on the left is an integral polynomial in \(X\), even for small \(D\), and the truncated multiplier has integral coefficients.
The product differs from \(B_J(D-\sum_{i<J}c_iX^i)\) by an integral polynomial divisible by \(X^J\), by the formal identity.
Evaluating at the signed integer \(x\) and using (1) proves (2); it involves no evaluation of an infinite series at \(x\).

<a id="coefficients"></a>
## Every order contributes a nonzero term

The formal identity \(X/\log(1+X)=\int_0^1(1+X)^t\,dt\) follows by integrating the binomial series, or first integrating for real \(|X|<1\).
Write it as \(1+\sum_{i\ge1}(-1)^{i-1}a_iX^i\).
Each \(a_i>0\), since \(\binom{t}{i}\) has the indicated sign for \(0<t<1\).
Putting \(X=-s\) and taking \(s\uparrow1\) gives \(\sum_{i\ge1}a_i=1\) by monotone convergence and \(-s/\log(1-s)\to0\).
Multiplying this series by that of \(\log(1+2X)/X\) yields, for \(r\ge1\),
\[
 (-1)^r c_r=\frac{2^{r+1}}{r+1}
 \left(1-\sum_{i=1}^r a_i\frac{r+1}{2^i(r+1-i)}\right)>0 .
\]
Indeed \(2^i(r+1-i)\ge r+1\) because \(2^i\ge1+i\) and \(r+1-i\ge1\), while the finite sum of the \(a_i\) is strictly below one.

For fixed \(J\ge2\) the right hand representative in (2) therefore has absolute value comparable to \(|x|^{J-1}\) for large \(|x|\).
If the two sides agree as integers, this gives the asserted bound directly.
Otherwise \(|B_J D|\ge |x|^J-O_J(|x|^{J-1})\).
For even \(J\) and \(x=q>0\) the representative is negative for all sufficiently large \(q\), so equality as integers is impossible.
This proves both growth bounds.
For \(J\) varying, truncate at any fixed order \(j>A+1\) to deduce the claimed divergence whenever \(J\ge j\) eventually.

<a id="four"></a>
## An explicit inequality for four vanishing moments

Here \(c_1=-1,\ c_2=3/2,\ c_3=-29/12\) and one can use \(B_4=48\).
For example the coefficients of \(V\) through order three are
\[
 \tfrac12,\quad -\tfrac34-\tfrac12 Y,\quad
 \tfrac98+\tfrac43Y+\tfrac16Y^2,\quad
 -\tfrac{83}{48}-\tfrac83Y-\tfrac34Y^2 .
\]
These follow by multiplying the truncation by \(((1+X)^{2Y}-(1+2X)^2)/X\) and matching \(Y-L(X)\) through order three.
Hence (2) takes the form
\[
                48D\equiv 96-48x+72x^2-116x^3\bmod |x|^4 .
\]
For \(y=|x|\ge240\), the absolute value of the displayed cubic representative is strictly between \(115y^3\) and \(117y^3\), by bounding its lower powers.
If \(48D\) equals this representative then \(D>2y^3\); otherwise \(48D>(y-117)y^3>96y^3\).
This gives \(D>2q^3\) in either case.
