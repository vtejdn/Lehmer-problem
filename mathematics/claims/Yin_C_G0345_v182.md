# Four unrestricted coefficients and the degree cost of a small factor

<a id="statement"></a>
## A reciprocal multiple or a shorter trace relation

Let \(S\) be an irreducible integral polynomial of degree \(d\) with positive logarithmic Mahler measure \(m\).
Suppose \(S\) divides an integer polynomial \(P\) with exactly four nonzero terms.
Remove the common monomial and content of \(P\) and choose its leading coefficient positive.
Write \(D=\deg P\) and \(t=\max_{S(z)=0}\log|z|\).
For all sufficiently small \(m>0\), with absolute constants, the following alternative holds.

* The normalized multiple is
  \[
       P=X^N B+\sigma B^*,\qquad
       B=uX^a+\epsilon v,\qquad
       \gcd(u,v)=1,\quad v>u\ge1,\quad N>a>0,
       \qquad \sigma,\epsilon\in\{-1,1\}.                           \tag{1}
  \]
  The reciprocal uses the actual degree \(a\).
  If \(S\) is the minimal polynomial of a Salem number, then \(\epsilon=-1\).
* Otherwise \(S\) divides a nonzero reciprocal integer polynomial with at most five terms and degree \(O(Dm/(dt))\).
  In particular
  \[
                     D\gg\frac{d^2t}{m^2}\gg\frac{d}{m^3}.          \tag{2}
  \]
  For a Salem factor \(t=m\), so \(D\gg d^2/m\).

In both cases the first and last gaps between successive exponents are \(O(Dm/(dt))\), and the logarithm of the largest absolute primitive coefficient is at most \(\log 6+3Dm/d\).
The conclusions allow unbounded coefficients and arbitrary cofactors.
They constrain the degree and form of possible multiples without supplying a uniform positive lower bound for their factors.

<a id="coefficients"></a>
## The coefficients of a minimal relation of powers

Let \(\alpha\ne0\) be algebraic of absolute logarithmic height \(h\).
Suppose powers with exponents \(0=e_0<\cdots<e_{s-1}=D\) have a one-dimensional space of rational linear relations, generated by a primitive integer vector \((c_i)\).
Then
\[
                   \log\max_i|c_i|\le\log((s-1)!)+(s-1)D h.        \tag{3}
\]
To prove this, form the matrix \((\alpha_j^{e_i})_{j,i}\) of the distinct conjugates of \(\alpha\).
Writing the powers in a rational basis of \(\mathbb Q(\alpha)\) gives a rational coefficient matrix of rank \(s-1\).
The embedding matrix of the basis is invertible in a normal closure, so the conjugate matrix also has rank \(s-1\).
Choose \(s-1\) independent rows.
Their signed maximal minors form a nonzero vector proportional to \((c_i)\).
At each place of the normal closure the maximum absolute minor is at most the product of the row maxima, with an extra factor \((s-1)!\) at archimedean places.
The row for \(\alpha_j\) has maximum \(\max(1,|\alpha_j|_v)^D\).
Taking normalized sums of logarithms over the places, the product formula cancels the common proportionality scalar, and each conjugate contributes \(D h\).
Primitivity makes the projective height of the rational vector equal to \(\log\max_i|c_i|\), proving (3).

For small \(m\), \(S\) is monic up to sign with unit constant term by the two endpoint bounds, and reciprocal by [Smyth's theorem](../audits/literature/Smy71/Yin_U001.md#smy71-u001).
Its roots have height \(m/d\).
The [three-term factor gap](Yin_C_G0345_v181.md#statement) excludes any rational relation involving just three of the four powers.
Two independent relations on all four could eliminate a coefficient and produce such a shorter relation.
Thus (3) applies to \(P\) with \(s=4\).

<a id="widths"></a>
## Gaps next to the extreme terms

Put \(H=\max_i|c_i|\) and take a root \(\alpha\) with \(\log|\alpha|=t>0\).
If the gap between the two largest exponents is \(a\), the leading term evaluated at \(\alpha\) gives \(e^{at}\le3H\).
The reversed polynomial also vanishes at \(\alpha\), and yields the same inequality with the lowest gap \(c\) in place of \(a\).
The [sparse divisor estimate](../audits/literature/Dob06/Yin_U001.md#irreducible) gives \(Dm/d\gg1\), since \(e^m-1\le2m\) for small \(m\).
Consequently
\[
                      w=\max(a,c)\ll Dm/(dt).                        \tag{4}
\]
For a reciprocal unit the logarithmic moduli occur in opposite pairs.
Their positive squares sum to at most \(t m\), and the [quadratic bound for a nontorsion unit](Yin_C_G0855_v2.md#statement) gives \(2d t m\ge 1/10\).
Thus \(d t/m\gg1/m^2\).
In particular \(w/D\) tends to zero uniformly as \(m\) tends to zero.
Also \(D t=(Dm/d)(dt/m)\) tends to infinity, and \(\log(3H)/(Dt)\) tends to zero by (3).

Write \(P=X^N B+E^*\) with \(B\) and \(E\) binomials of degrees \(a,c\), respectively, and with \(N=D-a\).
The separating gap \(N-c=D-a-c\) is at least \(D/2\) for small \(m\).
Hence
\[
                        |B(\alpha)|\le2H e^{-(N-c)t}<1/2.           \tag{5}
\]

<a id="trace"></a>
## Comparing the two binomials in both orientations

For \(J\in\mathbb Z[X]\) write \(T_J=J(X)J(X^{-1})\).
The equations for \(P\) at \(\alpha,\alpha^{-1}\) give \(T_B(\alpha)=T_E(\alpha)\).
The reciprocal Laurent polynomial \(T_B-T_E\) uses only the exponents \(0,\pm a,\pm c\).
If it is nonzero, multiplying by a monomial to make its extreme exponents zero and twice the maximum positive exponent gives a nonzero reciprocal polynomial of degree at most \(2w\).
It vanishes at \(\alpha\), so \(S\) divides it.
Applying the same sparse divisor estimate with at most five terms now gives \(w\gg d/m\).
Combination with (4) proves the first inequality in (2), and \(d t m\ge1/20\) proves the second.

If \(T_B=T_E\), comparison of the outermost coefficients forces \(a=c\).
The two binomials then have the same product of endpoint coefficients and the same sum of their squares.
Their ordered coefficient pairs agree up to common sign and possible interchange, as follows by squaring their sum and their difference.
Thus \(E=\pm B\) or \(E=\pm B^*\).
The second choice gives \(P=B(X)(X^N\pm1)\), which cannot carry \(S\) by the three-term gap and nontorsion.
The first gives (1), with positive leading coefficient \(u\) and constant coefficient \(\epsilon v\).
The integers \(u,v\) are coprime because \(P\) is primitive with disjoint blocks.
If \(u>v\), then \(|B(\alpha)|\ge u|\alpha|^a-v>1\), contrary to (5).
At \(u=v\), \(B^*=\pm B\) gives the excluded product case.
Thus \(u<v\), and (5) at a positive real Salem root also rules out \(\epsilon=+1\).
