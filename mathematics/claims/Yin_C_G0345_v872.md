# An odd-prime resultant excludes adjacent coefficients at a long dyadic or triadic exponent

<a id="statement"></a>
## Exclusion at the long exponent itself

Let \(0<u<v\) be coprime, \(g\ge1\), \(\epsilon=\pm1\), and \(a,b\) integers with \(|a|=q,\ |b|=q+1\).
Put \(P(T)=a(1+\epsilon T^{u+v})+b(T^u+\epsilon T^v)\).
If \(q\ge1024\) and \(v\) is any power of two or three, every algebraic-unit root of \(P(T^g)\) is a root of unity.
More generally, for every fixed positive integer \(k\) the same exclusion holds for \(v=k2^j\) or \(v=k3^j\), \(j\ge0\), as soon as \(q\ge q_0(k)\).
There is no restriction on the prime factors of either \(v-u\) or \(v+u\), or on how large \(v/u\) can be.
In the excluded families each positive-measure integral factor has logarithmic Mahler measure at least \(\log2\), by the [integral-factor conclusion](Yin_C_G0345_v869.md#degree).

In fact, for \(q\ge7\) and \(v=k\ell^j\), \(\ell\in\{2,3\}\), the existence of a nontorsion unit root forces, for **each** prime \(p\mid2q+1\),
\[
 \left(1-\frac{12}{q\log2}\right)\log p
 \le \log2+\frac{3k(p+1)}q.                                      \tag{1}
\]
These bounds use a prime where the two coefficients agree up to sign; this leaves a product of a short and a long binomial in the residue field.
The [fixed-pole local-period restriction](Yin_C_G0345_v755.md#period) uses a fixed local pole and critical-width hypotheses.
Here both the pole and the residue characteristic can vary, and the total primitive measure estimate from [near-equal coefficients](Yin_C_G0345_v869.md#statement) suffices for every nontorsion unit.

<a id="residue"></a>
## Covering the long binomial and allowing all short factors

Suppose \(z\) is such a unit and set \(\beta=z^g\).
Its monic minimal polynomial \(S\) lies in \(\mathbb Z[X]\), has unit constant term and divides \(P\) by monic division.
Write \(D=\deg S\) and \(m=Dh(\beta)\).
The [primitive degree and exterior sum](Yin_C_G0345_v869.md#degree) give \(u/D\le12/(q\log2)\) and \(m\le3/q\), since \(H=q+1\ge8\).
Fix \(p\mid2q+1\), necessarily odd, and choose \(c\in\{1,-1\}\) congruent to \(b/a\bmod p\).
This is possible because the two coefficient magnitudes sum to \(2q+1\), and neither is divisible by \(p\).
Over \(\mathbb F_p\), \(P/a=(1+c X^u)(1+\epsilon c X^v)\).
Denote the first factor by \(U\) and the monic associate of the second by \(V=X^{k\ell^j}-s\), where \(s=-\epsilon c\).
Set \(J=\overline S/\gcd(\overline S,U)\), using monic gcd.
Comparing the multiplicity of each irreducible factor shows \(J\mid V\), even when the two binomials have common factors, and \(\deg J\ge D-u\).

There is an integer \(1\le L\le(p+1)D\) and a sign \(e\) with
\[
                     J\mid X^{kL}-e.                              \tag{2}
\]
For \(J=1\) take \(L=e=1\).
Otherwise for any root \(x\) of \(J\) its degree \(f\) over \(\mathbb F_p\) is at most \(D\), so every nonzero element of \(\mathbb F_p(x)\) has order dividing \(p^f-1\).

For \(\ell=2\), \(x^k\) has order a power of two, since its \(2^j\)-th power is \(s\).
For every odd \(p\) the two-part of \(p^f-1\) is at most \((p+1)f\).
For odd \(f\) it is the two-part of \(p-1\); for even \(f\) its valuation is \(v_2(p-1)+v_2(p+1)+v_2(f)-1\).
Indeed odd powers preserve the two-part of the difference from one, and in repeated squaring after the first square each new sum with one has two-part two.
One of \(p-1,p+1\) has two-part two, giving the stated bound.
Take \(L\) to be the largest of the orders of \(x^k\) and \(e=1\).
These orders form a divisibility chain, so all roots of \(J\) are covered, and \(L\le(p+1)D\).
Every root of \(V\) has multiplicity \(p^{v_p(k)}\), also the multiplicity of every root of \(X^{kL}-1\), since \(p\) is odd and \(L\) is a power of two.

For \(\ell=3\), \(p\ne3\), take the orders of \(s x^k\), which are powers of three since \(3^j\) is odd.
The three-part of \(p^f-1\) is at most \((p+1)f\).
Indeed if \(p\equiv1\pmod3\) its valuation is \(v_3(p-1)+v_3(f)\); if \(p\equiv-1\pmod3\) it is zero for odd \(f\) and \(v_3(p+1)+v_3(f)\) for even \(f\).
To see the formulas, raising a unit congruent to one modulo three to an exponent prime to three preserves the valuation of its difference from one, while cubing raises that valuation by one by the binomial expansion.
In the even case apply this to \(p^2\) and \(f/2\).
With \(L\) again the largest order, take \(e=s\) since \(L\) is odd.
The same multiplicity check gives (2).
Finally for \(p=\ell=3\), all roots of \(J\) satisfy \(x^k=s\) in characteristic three.
Take \(L\) to be the least power of three at least \(D\), so \(L<3D\) for \(D>1\).
Then \(X^{kL}-s=(X^k-s)^L\) covers each multiplicity in \(J\), and (2) follows also here.

<a id="norm"></a>
## An integer determinant and the uniform threshold

On the free lattice \(\mathbb Z[X]/S\) of rank \(D\) take the determinant of multiplication by \(X^{kL}-e\).
It is \(N_{\mathbb Q(\beta)/\mathbb Q}(\beta^{kL}-e)\), nonzero since \(\beta\) is nontorsion.
Modulo \(p\) the kernel dimension is \(\deg\gcd(\overline S,X^{kL}-e)\): dividing both polynomials by their gcd gives that the kernel consists of the multiples of \(\overline S/\gcd(\overline S,X^{kL}-e)\).
This dimension is at least \(\deg J\ge D-u\).
Each zero diagonal entry modulo \(p\) in an integer Smith normal form contributes at least one \(p\) to the determinant.
Consequently the logarithm of its absolute value is at least \((D-u)\log p\), also valid when \(D-u\le0\).
Over the complex embeddings \(|\beta_i^{kL}-e|\le2\max(1,|\beta_i|)^{kL}\), so its logarithm is at most \(D\log2+kL m\).
Division by \(D\) and substitution of the bounds for \(L,m,u/D\) give (1).

For \(q\ge1024\) put \(A=1-12/(q\log2)>0.98\).
The function \(A\log x-\log2-3k(x+1)/q\) is concave on \([3,2q+1]\), so it is positive throughout if it is positive at both endpoints.
For \(k=1\) the first endpoint is bounded below by \(0.98\log3-\log2-12/1024>0\).
At the other endpoint it is at least \(0.98\log2049-\log2-6-6/1024>0\).
Since \(2q+1\) has an odd prime divisor, this contradicts (1).
For fixed \(k\) the first endpoint tends to \(\log(3/2)>0\) and the other tends to infinity, since its subtracted linear term there is \(6k(1+1/q)\).
This proves the asserted uniform exclusion for sufficiently large \(q\) in each fixed-multiple family.
