# Many primitive minimal signed relations in a finite quotient of a quadratic order

<a id="statement"></a>
## Fixed alternating signs in a short exponent interval

Take the fixed quadratic unit \(\alpha=4+\sqrt{15}\), its other conjugate \(\alpha^{-1}\), and the order \(R=\mathbb Z[\alpha]\).
Call a finite set of exponents a minimal signed support in a quotient of \(R\) if a sum of their powers of \(\alpha\) with coefficients in \(\{-1,1\}\) vanishes there, and no nonempty proper subset admits any such sign choice.
For every odd integer \(r\ge3\) there is an integer \(Q_r>2\), of size \(Q_r\asymp\alpha^{(r-1)/2}\), such that \(\alpha\) has order exactly \(2r\) in \((R/Q_rR)^\times\).
For every integer \(K\ge1\) the full quotient \(R/Q_rR\) admits at least
\[
                              K^{(r-3)/2}                              \tag{1}
\]
distinct minimal signed supports \(0=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_{r-1}<(2K+3)r\) with \(t_1=1\).
Each has the same coefficient tuple \(+1,-1,+1,\ldots,+1\) in increasing order, and its only vanishing sign choices are this tuple and its negative.
The normalization removes translations and common integer dilations.
The entire set of nonnegative exponents is dissociated at the actual real unit \(\alpha\).

The moduli \(Q_r\) can have arbitrarily many distinct prime divisors.
Thus simultaneous testing in all prime-power components of a scalar modulus of exponential size can still admit more than \(A^r\) candidates for every fixed \(A\), even with inclusion minimality in the full quotient and a prescribed ordered tuple of unit coefficients.
Here any choice \(K=K(r)\to\infty\) suffices, including arbitrarily slow growth.
On the other hand, the number of normalized supports of length \(r\) and span at most \(Cr\) is at most \(\binom{\lfloor Cr\rfloor+1}{r}\), which is at most exponential in \(r\) for fixed \(C\).
The construction therefore reaches the boundary in the order of the span for this counting obstruction.
The multiplicative order in this example is small relative to the allowed span when \(K\to\infty\).
The unit and its positive Mahler measure are fixed throughout.

<a id="minimal"></a>
## A single signed relation in a consecutive block

For any integer \(r\ge3\), put \(F_r(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}(-1)^iX^i\) and \(I_r=F_r(\alpha)R\).
Every nonzero polynomial \(G\) with coefficients in \(\{-1,0,1\}\) has \(G(\alpha)\ne0\) because its term at the largest exponent dominates all smaller powers when \(\alpha>2\).
In fact the same dominance holds for a nonzero integer polynomial whose coefficient magnitudes are at most two, since \(\alpha>3\) and the leading coefficient has magnitude at least one.

Suppose \(G\ne0\) is ternary of degree at most \(r-1\) and \(G(\alpha)\in I_r\).
At the exterior root the absolute value of \(F_r\) is at least \(\alpha^{r-1}(\alpha-2)/(\alpha-1)\) by its leading term, and that of \(G\) is at most \(\alpha^{r-1}\alpha/(\alpha-1)\).
At the contracting root the same bounds hold without the factor \(\alpha^{r-1}\), using the constant term of \(F_r\).
Thus \(u=G(\alpha)/F_r(\alpha)\) is a nonzero algebraic integer with both real conjugates of absolute value at most \(\alpha/(\alpha-2)<\sqrt2\).
Its integral norm has magnitude one.
If the norm is positive, the two conjugates have the same sign and the absolute integer trace is at least two and less than \(2\sqrt2<3\), forcing \(u=\pm1\) by equality in the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality.
If the norm is negative, the conjugate magnitudes are reciprocal and lie strictly between \(1/\sqrt2\) and \(\sqrt2\).
The absolute trace is then less than \(\sqrt2-1/\sqrt2<1\), giving trace zero and \(u^2=1\).
In either case \(G(\alpha)=\pm F_r(\alpha)\).
Applying the coefficient bound two to \(G\mp F_r\) gives \(G=\pm F_r\) as polynomials.
Conversely both choices vanish in the quotient, proving the claimed uniqueness on the block.

For odd \(r=2s+1\), the integer polynomial \(F_r\) is reciprocal.
Thus \(Q_r=\alpha^{-s}F_r(\alpha)\) is a rational algebraic integer, since it is fixed by conjugation and \(\alpha\) is a unit.
The formula
\[
                 Q_r=\frac{\alpha^{s+1}+\alpha^{-s}}{\alpha+1}
\]
gives the asserted growth and \(Q_r>2\) for \(s\ge1\).
In particular \(I_r=Q_rR\).
The identity \((1+X)F_r(X)=1-(-X)^r\) gives \(\alpha^r=-1\) in the quotient when \(r\) is odd, and \(\alpha^{2r}=1\) for every \(r\).
For odd \(r\), no exponent \(1\le j<r\) can satisfy \(\alpha^j=1\) in the quotient, by the block uniqueness applied to \(X^j-1\).
As \(-1\ne1\bmod Q_rR\), the order cannot be \(r\) either.
Every proper divisor of \(2r\) is at most \(r\), proving that the order is \(2r\).

<a id="placements"></a>
## Independent placements of consecutive pairs

For general \(r\), put \(s=\lfloor r/2\rfloor\).
Keep \(0,1\) fixed.
For \(1\le i<s\), replace the pair \(2i,2i+1\) by
\[
             2r k_i+2i,\quad 2r k_i+2i+1,\qquad 1\le k_i\le K .
\]
If \(r\) is odd, put the remaining singleton at \(2r(K+1)+r-1\).
All positions are distinct by their residues modulo \(2r\).
The placements keep each pair adjacent in sorted order and the odd singleton last.
Each pair therefore carries the signs \(+1,-1\) of its original residues, and the odd singleton carries \(+1\).
Reduction in \(R/I_r\) gives exactly the original consecutive-block sum, regardless of the placements.
Any sign choice on any nonempty sublist that vanished in the quotient would pull back to a nonzero ternary polynomial on the block, so the uniqueness result proves both full-sign uniqueness and inclusion minimality.
Each set recovers every \(k_i\) from the location of the residue \(2i\), and each has least exponent zero and contains one, so two distinct choices remain distinct under translation and primitive normalization.
The span is strictly less than \((2K+3)r\) and the count is \(K^{s-1}\), proving (1).
In particular this quotient example concerns the complete modular vanishing conditions on the signed words themselves.
The [density comparison for actual signed relations](Yin_C_G0345_v678.md#statement) and the [fixed-tuple construction with unrestricted integer coefficients](Yin_C_G0345_v597.md#statement) have different hypotheses.

<a id="primes"></a>
## Several prime factors and additional prescribed tests

For distinct odd primes \(u,v\), the rational integers \(Q_u,Q_v\) are coprime.
If a rational prime \(p\) divided both, take any residue field of \(R/pR\), which exists since this finite ring has \(p^2\) elements.
There \(y=-\alpha\) satisfies \(1+y+\cdots+y^{u-1}=1+y+\cdots+y^{v-1}=0\).
Thus \(y^u=y^v=1\), giving \(y=1\) and then \(p\mid u,v\), a contradiction.
For odd integers \(r,u\ge3\) with \(u\mid r\), the geometric sum \(F_u\) divides \(F_r\) over \(\mathbb Z[X]\).
Consequently \(Q_rR\subseteq Q_uR\), so \(Q_u\mid Q_r\) as rational integers since \(R\cap\mathbb Q=\mathbb Z\).
Taking \(r\) to be a product of any desired number of distinct odd primes gives at least that many different primes in \(Q_r\).

There is a related assertion for any prescribed finite family of quotient tests by nonzero ideals of \(R\).
Let \(J\) be their intersection, and take any positive multiple \(r\ge3\) of the order of \(-\alpha\) in the unit group of \(R/((1+\alpha)J)\).
This group is finite because a nonzero ideal of the quadratic order contains a nonzero principal ideal of finite additive index.
The geometric identity gives \(F_r(\alpha)\in J\), so each vanishing word constructed above passes all the prescribed tests as well.
Including the test modulo \(I_r\) retains inclusion minimality and sign uniqueness for the simultaneous conditions.
The full test in this extension uses the ideal \(I_r\) for either parity of \(r\).
The scalar-modulus assertion uses odd \(r\).
