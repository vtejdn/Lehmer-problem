# Integral Salem polynomials at fixed poles with very few roots of small dyadic degree

<a id="statement"></a>
## Exact evaluations at an integer and at a cubic Pisot unit

For a polynomial with distinct roots let \(N_B\) be the number of conjugates of local degree \([\mathbb Q_2(z):\mathbb Q_2]\le B\) among those roots in a fixed dyadic closure.
Two families of monic irreducible integer Salem polynomials admit exact evaluations independent of degree.

* For each odd prime \(r\ge5\) there is one of degree \(d=r-1\) with \(F(2)=3\) and \(2^d(2-T)\to3\), where \(T\) is the real exterior root.
  Every dyadic factor is unramified of degree \(\operatorname{ord}_r(2)\ge\log_2(r+1)\).
  Thus \(N_{\lfloor(\log_2 d)/2\rfloor}=0\).
* Let \(\rho\in(1,4/3)\) be the real zero of \(P=X^3-X-1\).
  There are infinitely many degrees \(d=2^k-2\) and Salem polynomials \(S\) of those degrees with \(S(\rho)=1\) and
  \[
       \rho^d(\rho-T)\longrightarrow \frac{\rho^2}{3\rho^2-1},\qquad
       N_B\le C B\exp(C\sqrt B)\quad (B\ge1).
  \]
  All dyadic factors are unramified.
  For \(B=\lfloor(\log_2 d)/2\rfloor\) the count is \(d^{o(1)}\) in the sense of an upper bound, and for any fixed \(\delta>0\) it is \(O_\delta(d^\delta)\) even at \(B=\lfloor c_\delta(\log d)^2\rfloor\) for sufficiently small \(c_\delta>0\).
  A subsequence has no conjugates of local degree at most \(b\), with \(b\to\infty\).

These polynomials retain the entire Salem root signature and a bounded Mahler measure; their exterior roots tend to the indicated fixed poles, above one.
The varying rational pole \((q+1)/q\to1\) and its prescribed homogeneous evaluation are essential additional hypotheses in the proposed low-degree implication.

<a id="integer"></a>
## The integer pole and its individual residual orders

Set \(d=r-1\) and \(F(X)=X^d+1-\sum_{j=1}^{d-1}X^j\).
Its [circle argument and Salem factor](../ledger/Yin_L_G0462_v296.md#barrier) give a single exterior root \(1<T<2\), its inverse, and all other roots on the circle.
Moreover \(T\to2\) and \((2-T)T^d=2T-1\), from
\[
 (X-1)F(X)=X^d(X-2)+2X-1 .
\]
Thus \(F(2)=3\), and \(d(2-T)\to0\) gives \((2/T)^d\to1\).
Modulo two \(F=\Phi_r\) is squarefree and all its roots have order \(r\).
Any cyclotomic factor over the integers would have order \(2^j r\): reducing a root of unity removes only the power of two from its order.
Such a factor has degree at least \(r-1=d\) and cannot contain the exterior root.
Since the [Salem factor](../ledger/Yin_L_G0462_v296.md#barrier) contains both off-circle roots, any other monic integer factor would be cyclotomic by Kronecker's theorem.
Hence \(F\) is irreducible.
Its degrees over \(\mathbb Q_2\) equal the Frobenius lengths of its residual roots by simple-root lifting.
The inequality \(r\mid 2^{\operatorname{ord}_r(2)}-1\) gives the claimed uniform lower bound on every local degree.

<a id="cubic"></a>
## The exact value and the cyclotomic cofactor for the cubic pole

Write \(P^*(X)=1-X^2-X^3\), \(n=2^k\) with odd \(k\), and \(A_n(X)=X^nP(X)-P^*(X)\).
The cubic \(P\) is irreducible by the rational root test.
It has discriminant \(-23\), and its conjugate pair has modulus \(\rho^{-1/2}<1\).
The [Salem construction](../audits/literature/Smy15/Yin_U002.md#smy15-u002) gives one Salem factor and cyclotomic factors at every sufficiently large \(n\).
We show that the latter product equals \(X^5-1\) on an infinite arithmetic progression of \(k\).

In characteristic two the ratio \(R=P^*/P\) is reduced of degree three: its numerator and denominator are the distinct irreducible cubics \(X^3+X^2+1,X^3+X+1\).
Its derivative has numerator \((X^2+X+1)^2\).
A repeated zero of \(A_n\bmod2\) must be a zero of that numerator because \(X^n\) has zero derivative.
At either primitive cube root \(\xi\), \(R(\xi)=\xi\) and \(\xi^{2^k}=\xi^2\ne\xi\) for odd \(k\).
Thus \(A_n\bmod2\) is squarefree.
A cyclotomic factor in characteristic zero cannot have order divisible by four, since its reduction would then have a repeated factor.

To see that a fixed finite set contains the possible orders at every such \(k\), work now in characteristic zero and write \(R_0=P^*/P\).
For a cyclotomic root write \(\zeta=\sigma y\) with \(\sigma=\pm1\) and \(y\) of odd order.
Since \(n\) is even, \(y^n=R_0(\sigma y)\).
The automorphism \(y\mapsto y^2\) then gives \(R_0(\sigma y)^2=R_0(\sigma y^2)\).
There are no poles at roots of unity and neither rational-function equality is identically true, since \(R_0(0)=-1\).
Each sign therefore gives only finitely many possible \(y\).
Choose a fixed positive multiple \(H\) of four and of the orders of two modulo all the odd orders \(m>1\) that occur among these possibilities.
For \(k\equiv1\bmod H\) we have \(\zeta^n=\zeta^2\) at every possible root of unity.
Conversely \(n\equiv2\bmod5\), so every fifth root of unity is a root of \(A_n\) because
\[
                 A_2=X^2P-P^*=X^5-1.
\]
Thus \(A_n=(X^5-1)S\) with \(S\) the monic irreducible Salem minimum of degree \(n-2\).
The squarefree reduction also shows that the reductions of \(S\) and \(X^5-1\) are coprime.

At \(\rho\) one has \(A_n(\rho)=-P^*(\rho)=\rho^2+\rho=\rho^5-1=\rho^4\).
This proves \(S(\rho)=1\).
For each fixed \(x\in(1,\rho)\), \(A_n(x)<0\) for large \(n\) while \(A_n(\rho)>0\).
Its unique exterior root \(T\) therefore tends to \(\rho\) from below.
The identity \(T^nP(T)=P^*(T)\) and \(P'(\rho)>0\) yield \(T^n(\rho-T)\to\rho^4/P'(\rho)\).
In particular \(n(\rho-T)\to0\) and \((\rho/T)^n\to1\), giving the stated limit for \(d=n-2\).

<a id="frobenius"></a>
## Short relations for Frobenius over a small residue field

In characteristic two a zero \(z\) of \(A_n\) satisfies \(z^{2^k}=R(z)\).
Since \(R\) has coefficients in \(\mathbb F_2\), all its iterates agree along this orbit with the corresponding iterations of the \(2^k\)-power map.
For \(z\in\mathbb F_{2^f}\) choose integers \(1\le a\le m=\lceil\sqrt f\rceil\) and \(b\) satisfying \(ak\equiv b\bmod f\) and \(|b|\le\lceil f/m\rceil\).
Indeed among the \(m+1\) residues of \(ik\bmod f,\ 0\le i\le m\), two fall in the same one of \(m\) subintervals of length \(f/m\).
If \(b\ge0\), then \(R^{\circ a}(z)=z^{2^b}\).
If \(b<0\), then \(R^{\circ a}(z)^{2^{-b}}=z\), where the exponent \(2^{-b}\) in this second case is a positive integer.
The equations are nonidentical rational-function equations: \(R^{\circ a}\) has degree \(3^a\), which is never a power of two, and its \(2^{-b}\)-th power in the second case has degree greater than one.
Clearing denominators bounds their respective root counts by \(3^a+2^b\) and \(2^{-b}3^a+1\).
Both are \(O(\exp(C\sqrt f))\) absolutely.
Summing over \(f\le B\) counts all the roots of local degree at most \(B\), because the reduction is squarefree.

Finally choose \(k\equiv1\bmod\operatorname{lcm}(H,1,2,\ldots,b)\), taking arbitrarily large such \(k\) for each successive \(b\).
For each \(f\le b\), the equation in \(\mathbb F_{2^f}\) reduces to \(z^2=R(z)\).
All its solutions satisfy \(z^5=1\) and belong to the cofactor already removed.
This proves the asserted subsequence statement.
