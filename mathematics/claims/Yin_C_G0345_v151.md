# A logarithmic measure lower bound at the first nonzero coefficient

<a id="statement"></a>
## A degree bound and a discriminant constraint for every exterior signature

Let \(P\) be the monic minimal polynomial of a nontorsion algebraic integer unit, with degree \(d\), logarithmic Mahler measure \(L>0\), and first nonzero coefficient \(a_q\) below the leading coefficient at depth \(q\).
For every fixed \(\delta>0\), if \(q\ge\delta d\), then
\[
                           L\gg_\delta \frac{\log\log(3d)}{\log(2d)}.              \tag{1}
\]
There is no restriction on the number of nonzero coefficients or the number of exterior roots.
At small \(L\) the actual discriminant gives the stronger necessary bound
\[
       |\operatorname{disc}P|^{1/d}
           \le C_\delta d\,\exp\!\left(-c_\delta (|a_q|^2/L)\log(2+|a_q|^2/L)\right).    \tag{2}
\]
In particular, a uniform lower bound on \(|\operatorname{disc}P|^{1/d}/d\) gives a uniform positive measure gap in this class.
These statements concern the discriminant of the monic polynomial.

Write \(b_1\ge b_2\ge\cdots\) for the positive logarithmic moduli of the exterior conjugates, continued by zeros after the last one.
Along any sequence in this class with \(L\to0\), at least
\[
                  c_\delta\,L^{-1/2}/\log^2(2/L)                    \tag{3}
\]
exterior conjugates satisfy \(q\log|\alpha|\ge\tfrac12\log(1/L)\).
More precisely, for integers \(r\ge0\) such that \((r+1)\sqrt L\log^2(2/L)\to0\), put \(X=|a_q|/((r+1)L)\).
Then
\[
                    qb_{r+1}\ \ge\ \log X+\log\log X-O_\delta(1).   \tag{4}
\]
All counts include distinct conjugates of the original irreducible input.

<a id="filter"></a>
## Filtering the powers of any selected exterior roots

For a subset \(I\) of the exterior roots put \(L_I=\sum_{\alpha\in I}\log|\alpha|\) and form the normalized finite Blaschke product
\[
            F_I(w)=\prod_{\alpha\in I}\frac{1-\alpha w}{1-w/\bar\alpha}
                    =\sum_{\ell\ge0}f_\ell w^\ell,\qquad f_0=1.
\]
Its boundary modulus is \(e^{L_I}\), and it is analytic on a neighborhood of the closed unit disk.
Thus
\[
                   \sum_{\ell\ge1}|f_\ell|^2=e^{2L_I}-1,\qquad
 D_j=\sum_{\alpha\in I}(\alpha^j-\bar\alpha^{-j}),\qquad
          \sum_{\ell=0}^{q-1} f_\ell D_{q-\ell}=-q f_q .             \tag{5}
\]
The second identity follows by multiplying the power series for \(-wF_I'/F_I\) at the origin by \(F_I\).

Let \(p_j\) be the power sums of \(P\) and \(R_j=p_j-D_j\).
Since \(p_j=0\) for \(1\le j<q\) and \(p_q=-q a_q\) by Newton's identities, (5) gives the exact relation
\[
                     p_q=-q f_q+\sum_{\ell=0}^{q-1}f_\ell R_{q-\ell}. \tag{6}
\]
For a general prefix with nonvanishing earlier moments the additional term on the right is \(-\sum_{1\le\ell<q} f_\ell p_{q-\ell}\).

<a id="energy"></a>
## Recovering the discriminant in the reflected power sums

First take \(I\) to contain every exterior root.
Then \(R_j=v_j=\sum\beta^j\), where \(\beta=\alpha\) inside or on the circle and \(\beta=1/\bar\alpha\) outside.
For \(0<L\le1\), the [moving-power bound](Yin_C_G0345_v149.md#powers) gives \(|v_j|/d\ll E(L)=\sqrt L\log^2(2/L)\) uniformly for \(1\le j\le d\) as \(L\to0\).
Apply the [reflected discriminant inequality](Yin_C_G0345_v108.md#phases) at \(\rho=e^{-1/d}\), retaining its discriminant term:
\[
  \sum_{j\ge1}\frac{\rho^{2j}|v_j|^2}{j}
      \le 2(d-1)L+\frac{d(d-1)}{d}
                 +d\log\frac1{1-e^{-2/d}}-\log|\operatorname{disc}P| .
\]
Indeed each ordered pair of distinct original roots contributes its logarithmic distance to \(\log|\operatorname{disc}P|\) before the diagonal is included; the pairwise disk and smoothing inequalities in the cited proof give exactly this subtraction.
Put \(x=2/d\), \(S(t)=\sum_{j\ge1}e^{-tj}|v_j|^2\), and \(H(t)=\sum_{j\ge1}e^{-tj}|v_j|^2/j\).
The preceding bound gives \(H(x)\le dW\), where \(W=C+2L+\log d-d^{-1}\log|\operatorname{disc}P|\) and the absolute constant \(C\) is chosen large enough that \(W\ge1\).

The finite sum over ordered pairs of the Poisson kernels with parameters \(e^{-t}\beta_i\bar\beta_k\), evaluated at angle zero, equals \(d^2+2S(t)\) by Fourier expansion.
For \(x\le t\le1\), each such kernel at \(t\) is at least \(c(x/t)\) times its value at \(x\).
Indeed, write \(r=e^{-x}|\beta_i\beta_k|\) and \(h=e^{-(t-x)}\).
The ratio at a common phase is smallest at angle zero, as follows by differentiating its squared-distance ratio with respect to the cosine.
Its minimum is \((1+hr)(1-r)/((1-hr)(1+r))\).
Since \(1-r\ge1-e^{-x}\gg x\) and \(1-hr\le1-r+(t-x)\), this proves the comparison.
For \(2\le K\le 1/x\), integration of the kernel comparison gives
\[
 H(x)\ge H(x)-H(Kx)=\int_x^{Kx} S(t)\,dt
           \ge c x\log K\, S(x)-Kx d^2/2.
\]
For \(L\le1\) the integral discriminant gives \(W\le C+2+\log d\).
Choose \(K=\max(2,W)\), which meets \(Kx\le1\) for sufficiently large \(d\).
Since \(e^{-xj}\ge e^{-2}\) for \(j\le d\), we get
\[
               \sum_{1\le j\le d}|v_j|^2
                  \ll d^2\frac{W}{\log(2+W)} .                       \tag{7}
\]
The same inequality for the bounded remaining degrees follows by \(|v_j|\le d\) and \(W\ge1\), with an enlarged absolute constant.

Apply Cauchy–Schwarz in (6) to the terms \(1\le\ell<q\) and use (5).
For all sufficiently small \(L\),
\[
       |a_q|\le (e^{2L}-1)^{1/2}
                 +O_\delta(E(L))
                 +O_\delta\!\left(\sqrt{L W/\log(2+W)}\right).        \tag{8}
\]
Since \(|a_q|\ge1\), the first two terms are eventually at most \(|a_q|/2\), uniformly at fixed \(\delta\).
This gives \(W/\log(2+W)\gg_\delta |a_q|^2/L\).
In particular \(W\gg_\delta |a_q|^2/L\), and inserting this estimate into the logarithm gives \(W\gg_\delta (|a_q|^2/L)\log(2+|a_q|^2/L)\), proving (2).
The upper bound \(W\le C+2+\log d\) and monotonicity of \(w/\log(2+w)\) give (1) at small \(L\).
Larger \(L\) satisfies (1) after reducing the implied constant.
The same proof for any \(\delta d\le q\le d\) with \(|p_q|/d\ge\eta>0\) and \(\sum_{1\le j<q}|p_j|^2\le C_1 d^2\) gives \(L\gg_{\delta,\eta,C_1}\log\log(3d)/\log(2d)\), by bounding the additional term after (6) with (5).

<a id="wiener"></a>
## An absolute coefficient bound for the filter

For any choice of \(r=|I|\), the coefficients in (5) satisfy
\[
                    \sum_{\ell\ge0}|f_\ell|\le 1+C r e^{L_I}.       \tag{9}
\]
To see this, the absolute value of the angular derivative of the argument of a finite Blaschke product is the sum of the Poisson kernels of its zeros in the disk.
This follows by logarithmic differentiation of each factor on the circle, where each phase derivative is \((1-|1/\alpha|^2)/|e^{it}-1/\alpha|^2\).
With normalized arc measure, \(\|F_I'\|_1=r e^{L_I}\).
For clarity, an analytic rational function \(G\) without poles in the closed disk has \(\sum_{j\ge0}|[w^j]G|/(j+1)\ll\|G\|_1\).
For nonzero \(G\), divide out a modulus-one finite Blaschke product of its interior zeros, take the analytic square root of the remaining zero-free function in the open disk, and put the Blaschke factor back into one copy of the square root.
This writes \(G=UV\) with \(\|U\|_2\|V\|_2=\|G\|_1\), also with boundary zeros by radial limits of the bounded factors.
The coefficient convolution is bounded by the bilinear matrix \(1/(i+j+1)\) on the two sequences of absolute coefficients.
Its \(\ell^2\) operator norm is bounded by the weighted Cauchy–Schwarz test with weights \((i+1)^{-1/2}\), since splitting at \(j=i\) bounds \(\sum_j (j+1)^{-1/2}/(i+j+1)\) by \(C(i+1)^{-1/2}\).
Apply this to \(G=F_I'\) to get (9), with the empty product treated directly.

<a id="radii"></a>
## How many conjugates must supply the first nonzero trace

Take \(I\) to consist of the \(r\) largest exterior roots, or all the exterior roots if fewer exist.
Then \(R_j\) is \(v_j\) plus the excess of all unselected exterior roots.
Writing \(u=qb_{r+1}\), convexity of \(\sinh\) on \([0,\infty)\) and \(1\le j\le q\) give
\[
           |R_j|/d\ll E(L)+(q/d)L\,\frac{\sinh u}{u} .
\]
The quotient is continued by one at zero.
Using (6), (5) and (9) we obtain
\[
             |a_q|\le C_\delta\sqrt L
                   +C_\delta(1+r)\left(E(L)+L\frac{\sinh u}{u}\right). \tag{10}
\]
If \((1+r)E(L)\to0\) then the first term and the contribution of \(E(L)\) are eventually smaller than \(|a_q|/2\).
The remaining inequality gives \(e^u/u\gg_\delta X\) once \(u\) is bounded away from zero, and indeed \(u\to\infty\) because \(X\to\infty\) and \(\sinh u/u\) is bounded on compact intervals.
Taking logarithms twice gives (4).
Finally take \(r=\lfloor c/E(L)\rfloor\) with \(c>0\) sufficiently small in terms of \(\delta\); the same absorption in (10) works for this fixed choice of \(c\).
It yields \(u\ge\tfrac12\log(1/L)\) eventually, since \(1/((r+1)L)\gg_\delta L^{-1/2}\log^2(2/L)\).
In particular there must be more than \(r\) exterior roots, proving (3).
